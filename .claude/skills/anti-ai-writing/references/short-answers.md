# Short answers and supplements

For college and program questions with a limit of about 350 words or fewer: "why us", "why this major",
community, intellectual interest, the MIT-style 100 to 250 word answers, Tufts' 100-word "Why Tufts?", 35 to 50
word short takes, the Stanford roommate note, and short program applications. With a real student, this is
Coach mode (see `personal-essay.md`, sections 2 to 8): the student writes; you ask, diagnose and correct. Write
a sample only for a clearly fictional person.

## What we learned from real admitted answers

We compared 16 short answers that admitted students published (MIT applicants in The Tech and on MIT's blogs,
Tufts' "Supplements Revisited" and "Essays That Worked", all written for classes of 2026 or earlier, so before AI
writing tools) with answers a model wrote for invented applicants to the same questions. Blind judges (an
admissions reader and an essay coach) called every real answer human and every model answer AI, with or without
the first version of this guide. They also rated the model answers as better answers (about 7 against 5 out of
10). Both results matter:

- **Real admitted answers are plainer than you expect.** They are earnest and direct: "I'm the type of person who
  learns by doing", "I knew that Tufts was it." They state enthusiasm and conclusions whole-heartedly, use long
  sentences (about 22 words on average; a quarter run past 30), almost no contractions (under 1 per 200 words), a
  bigger word now and then, sometimes a cliché or a stock transition. Their weakness is genericness: the "Why
  Tufts?" answers that list course titles or praise "the creators, the thinkers, and the doers" were rated lowest.
- **Model answers fail by over-crafting.** Judges cited the same signs again and again: a dense run of precise
  numbers and names ("eleven Folgers cans", "my cousin Lily, who is 12", "112 eggs, and 79 hatchlings"), a
  labeled-object or campus-trivia hook, comic asides and self-deprecating counts ("23 bowls, maybe four good"), a
  planted metaphor ("Acting and engines are the same: you wait for the thing to catch"), an "honest limitation"
  beat, and a hedged or trailing ending ("Maybe both.", "I think.", "if that is possible"). Hedges were cited in
  about half of the judgments of model answers and in none of the real ones. Plain model answers also used short
  clipped sentences (about 12 words), eight times the real rate of contractions, three times the colons, and a
  short punchy last line in half of the answers (real: 3 of 16).

The target is the specificity that made the model answers better with the plainness and conviction that made the
real ones human. (Caveat: the model answers were written from invented notes that a model also produced, so part
of what judges caught was the invented content itself. A real student's own material does not have that problem;
the risk for a real student is that AI help adds the over-crafted patterns.)

## How to write one (or what to ask the student for)

1. **Answer the question directly**, early. Restating the prompt wastes words; a plain statement of the answer
   (for example "The thing I do for fun is fix bicycles") is fine and common in admitted answers.
2. **One example that carries the point, with two or three details that matter.** A name, a place, one number if
   it is the point. Do not stack numbers, ages and labels to look specific; a run of precise figures is the most
   cited sign of a model answer.
3. **No tricks.** No labeled-object reveal, no campus-trivia hook turned into a metaphor, no comic aside or
   self-deprecating count, no planted metaphor that explains the student to themselves.
4. **State the conclusion and the enthusiasm plainly, as strongly as the student holds them and no stronger.**
   "I want to study materials science because…", "I knew that Tufts was it." No hedged, balanced or trailing
   endings ("Maybe both", "I think") on a conclusion the student is sure of. Where the student is unsure, say so
   once, plainly ("I don't know yet whether it will be chemistry or policy"); never turn an open question into a
   goal, a doubt into a certainty, or "kind of proud" into "proud".
5. **Add no causes or reasons the student did not give.** No "so", "because" or "which is why" that turns two of
   the student's facts into cause and effect, and no motive for a choice the student did not state. Putting the
   student's facts in order and joining them into one story is fine; that is writing, not invention.
6. **Write in the student's careful written voice.** Full sentences, many of them long; ordinary contractions
   where the student would use them (admitted answers used few, but stripping every one makes a 17-year-old sound
   stiff); commas and full stops rather than colons and semicolons; a bigger word where the student would reach for
   one. No clipped fragments for rhythm, no put-on casualness, no speech filler from the notes.
7. **"Why us" starts from the student.** What the student already did, read, built or wondered about, then the
   specific course, lab, tradition or practice at this school that continues it, and why. Include only school
   details the student supplied and can stand behind; never add a program, course number or professor's name.
   An answer any applicant could paste into another school's form is the weakest kind, human or not.
8. **Very short takes (35 to 50 words, 200 characters):** one concrete, slightly unexpected answer; a fragment or
   a list is fine; humor if the student has it, without explaining the joke.
9. **Each answer adds something new**, and none repeats the personal statement.
10. **Count words.** Stay within the limit; 90 to 100 percent of it is normal.

## Coaching a student's draft

Check the draft for the over-crafted patterns above, especially if the student used AI to brainstorm or polish:
stacked figures, quirky labels, quips, hedged endings, clipped fragments. Ask for the plain version in the
student's words. Check the opposite failure too: an answer that is all praise for the school, or all adjectives
about the student, with no example. Give corrections as a list; do not write replacement sentences.

## Activity list and honors (Common App)

Position or role (50 characters), organization (100), description (150). Start with a verb; give scale and
results in numbers (members, hours, money raised, users, rank); no full sentences needed; no adjectives about
yourself ("passionate", "dedicated"); do not repeat the role in the description. Honors: the name of the award
and its level (school, regional, national, international) and, if space allows, how selective it is. With a real
student, edit their entries; do not invent numbers. (This section follows admissions offices' published advice;
we had no set of real activity lists to test it against.)

## Check before returning

- Does the answer address the question early and directly?
- Count the precise numbers, names and labels: are they the two or three that matter, or decoration?
- Any quip, labeled reveal, planted metaphor or self-deprecating count? Cut it.
- Read the last sentence alone: hedged, trailing or a punchline? End on the plain conclusion or the last fact.
- Fact diff: every goal, conclusion, feeling and "so"/"because" link is in the notes, at the same strength.
- Colons and clipped fragments: rare. Contractions: as the student would write them, not zero.
- "Why us": is every school detail supplied by the user, and could another applicant paste this elsewhere?
- Word count within the limit.
