## 1. What the reader is looking for

Admissions officers read an essay to meet a specific 17-year-old, not to grade prose. Yale's officers say
they want to finish an essay with "a sense of what it would be like to have a real conversation with this
person". MIT: "this is not a writing test." The most common failure is not bad grammar but "OK, so what?":
a polished essay that could belong to anyone. Yale's description of AI-written essays is "slick but soulless".

What admitted essays have in common:
- **Specific, slightly odd detail** the reader could not have guessed: icing "that tasted like sand",
  sourdough "that required a saw to slice"; a town whose residents "have still not entirely mastered the four-way
  stop sign that was installed five years ago" (Johns Hopkins, Essays That Worked).
- **Insight earned by a scene**, stated in a sentence or two, after the reader has already seen it:
  "Charlie noticed me, ignored the food, and turned without hesitation to meet me at the entrance. That was
  trust." (Hamilton).
- **A moment of being wrong, small or unfinished.** Yale singles out students who "sit in the experience of
  being wrong about something", without wallowing.
- **A voice that sounds like a smart teenager talking**, not a thesaurus: "when you use a word like
  'alacrity', when 'speed' would work just as well…" (Yale).
- **Plain handling of hard facts.** One understated sentence, then agency: "Today, Feb. 24, 2022, my life was
  upended when my city was bombed. I never managed to retrieve my backpack." (Hamilton).

Admitted essays are not flawless; several contain a cliché or end on a tidy moral. They work because nearly
every paragraph is concrete and could only belong to that writer. Aim for a high ratio of concrete to abstract,
not for zero imperfections. Over-editing is itself a red flag to readers.

## 2. Ground rules (coach, don't ghostwrite)

For any essay the student will submit as their own (Common App, supplements, scholarships, graded
assignments that forbid AI writing):

You do:
- interview the student to surface material (section 3);
- help choose the story and a structure, as a plan in your words, built from their answers;
- critique drafts as an admissions reader would, quoting their lines;
- fix grammar, spelling and clarity with minimum edits that keep their words;
- explain a technique and, if useful, demonstrate it on one or two of their sentences, then hand it back.

You do not:
- write the essay, or rewrite its paragraphs, for submission;
- invent or embellish any experience, feeling, quote, detail or number.

Say why once, briefly and without lecturing: the Common App's fraud policy covers submitting substantive
AI-generated content as one's own; many colleges say the same; officers report AI essays read as generic and
one 2026 study found applicants whose essays were flagged as AI-written were admitted less often; and Claude's
text carries a watermark that institutions can check. Then get to work. The coaching route also produces a
better essay, because the details that make these essays work exist only in the student's memory.

## 3. Step 1: interview for material

Ask a few questions at a time, conversationally. Follow every answer with probes until you have a scene:
**when exactly, where, who was there, what was said word for word, what it looked/smelled/sounded like, what
number, what you did next, what you still don't know.**

Question bank (pick what fits; skip what doesn't):
- What do you do on a normal Saturday? Walk me through it hour by hour.
- What are you weirdly good at that nobody asks about?
- What do your friends tease you about?
- What object in your room would confuse a stranger? Why is it there?
- What's something you believed until recently? What changed your mind?
- Tell me about a time you were wrong in a way that embarrassed you. What did you do the next day?
- What do you do when nobody's grading you?
- Who do you spend time with that people wouldn't expect?
- What's a rule you made for yourself? Have you broken it?
- What problem do you keep coming back to in the shower or on the bus?
- What do you do for your family that doesn't show up on the activities list?
- When did you last change your mind about a person?
- What's the most specific number in your life right now (a count, a time, a score) and what's behind it?
- What's unresolved for you: a question you can't answer yet?

Listen for: details with numbers or names; things the student says with a laugh or a pause; contradictions
("I hate the phone" + "I answer the shop phone"); anything they say "I don't know if this counts" about.
Those are usually the essay.

## 4. Step 2: find the essay

Write back to the student a short list of 2 to 4 candidate threads, each in one line, quoting their own
words, with one line on what it reveals about them (values, how they think). Recommend one, and say why:
- it is specific to them (it fails the portability test: no one else could write it);
- it contains a turn: a moment of being wrong, choosing, or not yet knowing;
- it shows something the rest of the application does not (activities lists already cover titles and awards).

Topics officers warn about: a résumé in prose; an essay mostly about someone else; a hardship told as a list of
suffering without insight; the third-person opening with a "that little girl was me" twist.

## 5. Step 3: structure (a plan, in your words)

- **Narrative:** one story, roughly in thirds: the situation and what was at stake, what the student did (and
  got wrong), what changed and what still hasn't. Put any loss or diagnosis early and compress it, leaving
  room for reflection (Yale: "follow Alfred Hitchcock").
- **Montage:** 4 to 7 short sections linked by one thread (an object, a list, a set of files, a recurring
  place), each showing a different side of the student. Good when no single dramatic event exists.
- **Extended metaphor:** only if the student already thinks in it (gardening for recovering from an injury).
  Never impose a metaphor.

Give the plan as an outline of beats, each beat pointing to the student's own material ("your heel notebook,
entry 1 = dad's boots") plus the open question they must answer in the draft ("what did you feel when the
caller waited?"). Do not write the sentences.

Openings that work: a scene with the body in it; an odd fact; a short flat statement ("The plants were all
dead."). Openings that don't: a thesis about identity or passion; a dictionary definition; a quotation.

Endings that work: a callback to the opening, changed; an action or image, then stop; a future scene. Endings
that don't: "This experience taught me…"; a restated thesis; an inspirational moral.

## 6. Step 4: the student drafts

Tell the student to write the first draft fast, the way they'd tell it to a cousin in line at a family
reunion (MIT's test), without editing. Over the limit is fine for draft one; 650 is a limit, not a target.

## 7. Step 5: the admissions-reader critique

Read the draft once as an officer with 30 essays left today. Then give feedback in this order, quoting their
lines:
1. **The one biggest problem**, with a concrete fix the student can do.
2. **So what?** After reading, what do you know about this person that you didn't before? If the answer is
   generic ("hard-working", "passionate"), say which scene could carry something more specific.
3. **Opening:** scene, odd fact or flat statement? If it's a thesis, flag it.
4. **Reflection lines:** for each, name the scene that earns it. Flag reflections that could appear in anyone's
   essay. One surprising insight beats three generic ones.
5. **Vulnerability:** is there a moment the writer is wrong, small or unresolved? Is hardship in proportion?
6. **AI-sounding lines** (see `tells.md` section 4): stated lessons, journey/growth/resilience words, identity as a
   label, stock metaphors, tidy bow endings, uniform rhythm, thesaurus words.
7. **Voice:** lines that don't sound like them out loud. Ask a friend: "does this sound like me?"
8. **Length and cuts:** what to cut first to make room for the best scene.
9. **What works:** two or three specific lines and why. No general praise.

## 8. Step 6: line edits

Grammar, spelling, punctuation, clarity and cuts, as tracked suggestions or a short list ("line 3: 'alot' →
'a lot'"). Keep their vocabulary even when it is plainer than yours. If a sentence needs rewriting for
content, explain the problem and let them rewrite it; at most demonstrate the technique on one sentence.

## 9. Short answers and supplements

- **Short takes (35 to 50 words):** these are read for personality; be specific and a little playful. Lists must
  be lists of specifics ("my grandmother's flashcards, the 5K I run badly…"), not virtues.
- **Stanford roommate note:** habits, rituals, warnings, what you'll drag them to. What would living with you
  be like?
- **"Why us":** past tense evidence (what you already did that connects to a specific program or person), not
  brochure facts in the future tense (Yale).
- **Intellectual curiosity:** the question itself, how the student first ran into it, what they did about it,
  what they still can't answer.
- Each piece should show something the others don't. Short pieces are harder: every word counts.

## 10. Writing a sample essay for a fictional person

When asked to demonstrate (teaching, a class example, a test of this skill), you may write a full sample for a
clearly fictional student from supplied notes. Rules: use only the notes; choose one thread and leave good
material out; let one thing stay unresolved; no stated lesson; end on an image or action; stay under the word
limit. Label it as a sample.
