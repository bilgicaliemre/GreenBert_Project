# Messages and appeals

For group messages, short posts, emails to people the writer knows, and appeals that ask people to give or to
join (a fundraising page, a donation request in a WhatsApp group, a sign-up call). These are read in a few
seconds by people who know the writer. They judge two things at once: whether the writer means it, and whether
the writer wrote it.

The guidance comes from 32 appeals that runners wrote for their charity-run pages before AI writing tools existed,
compared with plain-model and skill versions written from the same facts. Blind judges rated every model
version as less likely human than every real one (the real ones were judged human 100% of the time, the model
ones 17 to 25%). They did not catch the models by vocabulary. They caught them by punctuation, by an assembled
plan, and by tics carried over from the notes.

## What the model versions did that the real ones did not

| | real appeals | model versions |
|---|---|---|
| open with "I'm running for X" | 12% | 44 to 66% |
| semicolons per text | 0.4 | 0.7 to 1.2 |
| colons per text (not before a list or link) | 0.06 | 0.4 to 0.5 |
| sentence length, mean (spread) | 15 words (SD 8) | 10 to 12 words (SD 4 to 5) |
| "bence" ("I think") from the notes | 0% | 25 to 31% |
| judges citing a tidy, ordered plan | 12% | 50 to 59% |

The judges' most cited signs of a model: a tidy plan in which every part is present and in order, "polished"
punctuation (semicolons, colons, the formal circumflex in Turkish
"imkân" and "hâlâ"), and the same phrasings recurring across texts. Their most cited signs of a person are
things this skill never fakes: typos, a pasted project title, a run-on sentence.

## How to write one

1. **Write in the readers' language and only that.** If the group writes in Turkish, the message is in Turkish.
   Never append a translation unless the user asks for one; a bilingual message to one's own team is the
   clearest sign of a tool.
2. **Start where the writer's reason starts.** A page someone chose to open can begin with the person, the
   fact or the question that made the writer run: a lost brother, a statistic, "Did you know...?". Put the ask
   at the end, or wherever it lands naturally. Group chats and emails that need an action are the exception:
   there the news and the ask go in the first two lines.
3. **Let one part carry the text.** A real appeal is mostly a story, or mostly the cause, or mostly a belief,
   with a short ask attached. Do not build every appeal from the same parts in the same order (story, belief,
   beneficiaries, money, ask, thanks). Leave out the parts the writer
   would not bother with: a thank-you line, a restatement of the goal, a sentence on what the runner gains.
4. **Keep the money concrete when the facts give it.** What one donation buys ("140 TL puts one more young
   person through the training"), the target and the project's name. When judges noted that an appeal gave
   no amount, target or use for the money, they rated their chance of giving about one point lower (on a 1 to 10 scale).
5. **Write sentences the length the writer would.** Appeals are written, not spoken: a long sentence that
   chains clauses (in Turkish, with -ip, -arak, relative clauses) sits next to a short one. Avoid a run of
   short, even declaratives.
6. **Punctuate like the register.** Commas and full stops. No semicolons. A colon only before a list, an
   account number or a link. In casual Turkish, write "imkan", "hala", "kar" as most people do, unless the
   user writes the circumflex. One exclamation mark at the ask or at the line the writer feels most is normal
   in this genre; more is the user's call.
7. **Address readers the way the writer would.** A greeting is optional ("Merhaba sevgili dostlar"); "siz" for
   a mixed or older audience, "sen" for friends; the team's own name and its in-jokes if the user gave them.
   An emoji only in chat channels, and only if the user uses them.
8. **Leave the notes' spoken tics behind.** Notes are speech: "bence", "aslında", "yani", "I think". They are
   not the text's voice. Keep the writer's feelings and reasons; drop the verbal fillers that carried them.
9. **Never invent.** Asked for a group message without facts, a plain model adds a feeling ("it was great
   running together") or an apology ("I couldn't tell most of you in time") that the writer never stated.
   Write only what the user gave you; ask, or leave `[ask: ...]`, for anything else.

## Check before returning

- Does the text open with "I'm running for X"? If the reason is stronger, start there.
- Count semicolons and colons; remove every semicolon and any colon that does not introduce a list or link.
- Is every part present, in the usual order? Cut one or two.
- Any "bence" or "I think" that came from the notes? Cut it unless the opinion needs marking.
- Is there an unrequested translation, a sign-off name you made up, or a feeling the user never stated?
