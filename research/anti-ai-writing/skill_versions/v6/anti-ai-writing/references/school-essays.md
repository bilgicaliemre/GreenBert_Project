## 1. Argument essays

Readers rank arguments by how they are built, not by how many good points they contain. In our tests, essays
with largely the same ideas ranked far apart: one made a single idea carry the whole essay, the other listed
the same ideas as separate reasons. Build the argument first. None of these moves allows an invented fact or a
claim about what everyone does.

- **Thesis against the nearest alternative.** Name the position a reasonable reader already holds (the usual
  compromise, the obvious middle option, a double standard people already accept) and state your thesis
  against it. Let that idea decide what each paragraph does; do not restate it at the end of each one.
- **One worked case.** Prove the claim the essay depends on with a step-by-step example the reader can follow,
  with numbers when a quantity decides the point (what one family pays under each fare plan). Mark a made-up
  case as made up ("Suppose a family…"). Invented statistics, studies and surveys are never allowed.
- **The strongest objection to your own remedy**, not only to the general position. Say what is right in it
  and build that into the remedy (a limit, a cost, an exception), naming the part of the objection that made
  the change necessary. Granting an objection and then waving it off ("That's fair, but…" with nothing changed)
  does not answer it.
- **One argument per body paragraph.** If a paragraph makes two separate points, split it or cut the weaker.
- **Details sit inside a step.** Keep an everyday detail when it makes a step of the argument concrete; cut one
  that starts a topic of its own. Never present a detail as something the writer saw or did unless the user
  said so.
- **Opening.** The first paragraph sets up the view or situation your thesis answers, using something concrete
  the reader can check (a rule, a routine, two practices that contradict each other), stated only as broadly as
  it is true. Do not open on what everyone feels, knows or says ("Every student knows…", "Ask any teacher…"):
  in our tests over half the unsupported-claim flags on first paragraphs fell on such sentences. Do not make
  "I think" the essay's first words.
- **Title**, when the format takes one: short and plain, no colon subtitle.

## 2. The student's register (arguments and literary analysis)

When the essay belongs to a student, make a register pass after the argument is built, not while drafting it.
In our tests a plain student register read as more human, but applied while drafting it turned arguments into
lists of reasons, which lost to the adult-register essay 8 times of 8.

- Contractions at a normal rate; plain words. First person is allowed where the writer makes a judgment, not
  required, and never as a stock stance line. Never invent a personal anecdote the user did not give. Use
  general school experience only as a condition or a marked hypothetical ("If…", "Suppose…"), not as a claim
  about what all students do.
- Split a sentence over about 30 words only when it carries two claims; a long sentence that carries one
  qualified claim stays. No semicolon pairs: split them or join with "because" or "so". No stock student
  openers ("Picture this", "We've all been there").
- In a literary analysis, name the chapter or scene when the source gives it, rather than "these passages" or
  "the quotes".
- List each paragraph's claim before and after the pass. The lists must match: no objection, worked case or
  reasoning step may be cut to make the voice plainer.

## 3. Literary analysis

- **Thesis as a test.** State your answer to the prompt in the first paragraph as a claim about what a word,
  image or speaker does that you can check against each passage, not a theme label. Apply it in every
  paragraph. Each observation gets its consequence for the thesis in the same or the next sentence; an
  observation you cannot connect goes.
- **Beyond the obvious.** Give the readings anyone would make from these quotes (the plain contrasts and
  repetitions) at most a clause each, and spend the paragraphs on one or two readings that come from a
  particular word, speaker or ordering. Such a reading must still pass the reading check (SKILL.md step 4): a
  plain reading stated precisely beats a clever one the words do not carry.
- **Quotes as testimony.** Who says it, to whom, and how sure they are. A character pleading or a narrator
  guessing is weaker evidence than a narrator stating. Where the argument leans on such a quote, say so once
  and look for a passage from another speaker that backs it up. Before contrasting two speakers, check every
  quote in the set for a line that breaks the contrast.
- **Only the quotes' own words.** Do not argue from background the quotes do not give, and never state a plot
  fact you cannot check. Quote a phrase with its own grammar; never splice two pieces so that a word attaches
  to a different subject.
- **The conclusion stays inside the excerpts.** You are working from passages, not the whole book: do not say
  how it ends, what a character "never" does, or what happens later unless a quote shows it. Reread the closing
  claim against your earlier paragraphs; a closing twist that contradicts an earlier paragraph is an error a
  teacher will mark. End on the verdict and its reason, not a list of scenes; add a limit only if the excerpts
  leave the question open.

## 4. Explanations

- **Opening.** Start from something the reader has seen or believes (an everyday observation, a common wrong
  answer, a question the title raises), then give the answer in one sentence before the mechanism. Do not open
  on a misconception unless the brief names one or readers actually hold it.
- **One organizing idea that tests the explanation**, when one fits: a case where the cause is missing or
  reversed, a correction of the question itself, a historical puzzle the piece solves by its end, or the
  obstacle that explains why something took so long. Set it up early and resolve it at the end. If none fits,
  a clear order of mechanism steps is enough; never invent a puzzle. A sequence of textbook definitions with no
  thread is what reads as machine-made.
- **Numbers.** Where the explanation turns on a quantity, give the number if it is verifiable general knowledge
  and one consequence the reader could check (a ratio, a percentage difference). Do this for the one or two
  numbers the explanation depends on, not for every number; if you are not sure of the value, keep the
  qualitative wording.
- **Misconceptions.** Say in a clause why the wrong idea is a fair guess, give the fact that breaks it, and once
  the real cause is established, concede whatever is true in it, with its size. Never close with "it's all
  about X" after conceding a second factor.
- **Familiar half-truths.** School explanations repeat some simplifications that are not quite right. For each
  step, ask whether the familiar version is actually correct, and leave out what you cannot confirm.
- **Terms and paragraphs.** Define a technical term in a few words where it first appears if this reader would
  not know it. One step of the mechanism per paragraph. Contractions are normal in a blog or club piece. If the
  piece runs dense, cut a side fact before adding a definition or a number.
- **An activity is optional.** Add one only if it fits in about 60 words without dropping a mechanism step.
  The reader changes one thing, and the last sentence maps the result onto the explanation.
- **Ending.** Resolve the opening puzzle or the organizing case with the step the opening sentence could not
  give. Not a summary, not a repeat of the opening answer, not a "next time you see…" line, and not a side
  fact that answers nothing the piece set up.
