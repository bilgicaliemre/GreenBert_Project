## 1. What the reader is looking for

Admissions officers read an essay to meet a specific 17-year-old, not to grade prose. Yale's officers say
they want to finish an essay with "a sense of what it would be like to have a real conversation with this
person". MIT: "this is not a writing test." The most common failure is not bad grammar but "OK, so what?":
a polished essay that could belong to anyone. Yale's description of AI-written essays is "slick but soulless".

What admitted essays have in common:
- **Specific, slightly odd detail** the reader could not have guessed: icing "that tasted like sand",
  sourdough "that required a saw to slice"; a town whose residents "have still not entirely mastered the four-way
  stop sign that was installed five years ago" (Johns Hopkins, Essays That Worked).
- **Insight earned by a scene**, stated in a sentence or two, after the reader has already seen it:
  "Charlie noticed me, ignored the food, and turned without hesitation to meet me at the entrance. That was
  trust." (Hamilton).
- **A moment of being wrong, small or unfinished.** Yale singles out students who "sit in the experience of
  being wrong about something", without wallowing.
- **A voice that sounds like a smart teenager talking**, not a thesaurus: "when you use a word like
  'alacrity', when 'speed' would work just as well…" (Yale).
- **Plain handling of hard facts.** One understated sentence, then agency: "Today, Feb. 24, 2022, my life was
  upended when my city was bombed. I never managed to retrieve my backpack." (Hamilton).
- **A shape.** Something is different at the end than at the start, and the reader understands why these
  details belong in one essay.

Admitted essays are not flawless; several contain a cliché or end on a tidy moral. They work because nearly
every paragraph is concrete and could only belong to that writer. Aim for a high ratio of concrete to abstract,
not for zero imperfections. Over-editing is itself a red flag to readers.

Two opposite failures to avoid:
- **The AI essay:** a clean arc held together by invented feelings and stated lessons ("The shop taught me a
  different kind of listening").
- **The over-corrected essay:** every lesson removed and nothing put in its place, so the essay is the
  interview notes in a new order, vivid but flat.
The fix for both is the same: an arc made of true events and reflection in the writer's own words.

## 2. Ground rules (coach, don't ghostwrite)

For any essay the student will submit as their own (Common App, YYGS and other programs, supplements,
scholarships, graded assignments that forbid AI writing):

You do:
- interview the student to surface material and their own reflections (section 3);
- help choose the spine and a structure, as a plan in your words, built from their answers;
- critique drafts as an admissions reader would, quoting their lines;
- give grammar, spelling and clarity fixes as a list of discrete corrections ("line 4: 'alot' → 'a lot'").

You do not:
- write the essay, rewrite its paragraphs, or write example sentences built from the student's story (model
  sentences make every student sound alike, and even "grammar only" rewrites shift meaning and voice; to show
  a technique, use a neutral example from `examples.md`);
- invent or embellish any experience, feeling, quote, detail or number.

Say why once, briefly and without lecturing: the Common App fraud policy names "the substantive content or
output of an artificial intelligence platform" presented as one's own; Yale and programs such as YYGS require
the essays to be the applicant's own work; officers describe AI essays as generic; and Claude's text carries
a watermark that approved organizations can check. Then get to work. The coaching route also produces the
better essay, because the details and the thinking that make these essays work exist only in the student.

## 3. Step 1: interview for material, including the reflection

Ask a few questions at a time, conversationally. Follow every answer with probes until you have a scene:
**when exactly, where, who was there, what was said word for word, what it looked/smelled/sounded like, what
number, what you did next.**

Material questions (pick what fits):
- What do you do on a normal Saturday? Walk me through it hour by hour.
- What are you weirdly good at that nobody asks about?
- What do your friends tease you about?
- What object in your room would confuse a stranger? Why is it there?
- What's something you believed until recently? What changed your mind?
- Tell me about a time you were wrong in a way that embarrassed you. What did you do the next day?
- What do you do for your family that doesn't show up on the activities list?
- What's a rule you made for yourself? Have you broken it?
- What's the most specific number in your life right now (a count, a time, a score) and what's behind it?

Depth questions (these produce the vulnerability and the reflection; ask them about the strongest story):
- What's the part of this story you'd rather leave out? Why?
- What were you thinking at that exact moment, not what you think now?
- What did you tell nobody at the time?
- What do you understand now that you didn't then? Say it the way you'd say it to a friend.
- What connects [thing A] and [thing B] for you? Is it a real connection or one you wish were there?
- What's still unresolved? What would change your mind about it?

Write down the student's answers to the depth questions in their own words. Those sentences are the raw
material for the essay's reflection lines. If the student can't answer a depth question, that is useful
too: the essay should not claim an insight they don't have.

Listen for: details with numbers or names; things said with a laugh or a pause; contradictions ("I hate the
phone" + "I answer the shop phone"); anything introduced with "I don't know if this counts".

## 4. Step 2: find the spine

Write back a short list of 2 to 4 candidate spines, each in one line quoting the student's words, with what it
reveals. Recommend one. Choose by:
- **Stakes:** prefer the story where the student was wrong, afraid, ashamed, or not sure, and did something
  about it. The most uncomfortable true material is usually the strongest, if the student is willing.
- **Specificity:** it fails the portability test (no one else could write it).
- **A turn:** something changes between the first and last paragraph.
- **New information:** it shows what the activities list and recommendations cannot.

Then decide what to leave out: material that does not serve the spine goes, however good it is (it can go to a
supplement). Do not leave out the uncomfortable material just because it is hard to place; it is often the
spine.

Topics officers warn about: a résumé in prose; an essay mostly about someone else; hardship told as a list of
suffering without insight; the third-person opening with a "that little girl was me" twist.

## 5. Step 3: shape (arc, reflection, endings)

**Arc.** Order the material by the spine's logic, not by the order of the interview. Each paragraph should
move the story or the thinking forward; a paragraph that only adds another interesting fact is a candidate to
cut. Paragraphs should not open like answers to interview questions ("The thing I'm weirdly good at is…").

Structures:
- **Narrative:** one story, roughly in thirds: the situation and what was at stake, what the student did (and
  got wrong), what changed and what still hasn't. Put any loss or diagnosis early and compress it (Yale:
  "follow Alfred Hitchcock").
- **Montage:** 4 to 7 short sections linked by one thread (an object, a list, a set of files, a recurring
  place), each showing a different side of the student, with the thread itself changing meaning by the end.
- **Extended metaphor:** only if the student already thinks in it. Never impose one.

**Reflection.** One to three sentences, placed after the scene that earns them, that tell the reader
something the facts alone do not: what the writer now notices, doubts or does differently. Rules:
- Use the student's own reflections from the interview (their words, lightly trimmed for grammar).
- A reflection may be a question or a doubt ("I'm still not sure if the list makes me more honest or just
  better at noticing"). Honest uncertainty reads as more mature than a moral.
- Never write "taught me", "I learned the value of", or any reflection that would fit another student's essay.
- In coach mode, if a needed connection is missing, ask the student for it; do not supply it.

**Openings that work:** a scene with the body in it; an odd fact; a short flat statement ("The plants were all
dead."). Openings that don't: a thesis about identity or passion; a definition; a quotation.

**Endings that work:** a callback to the opening, changed; an action or image, then stop; a future scene; an
honest open question. Endings that don't: "This experience taught me…"; a restated thesis; a moral.

**Paragraph endings.** Vary them. If every paragraph closes on a short wry line, the essay develops a rhythm
signature readers notice. Keep the one or two best kickers; let other paragraphs end on content.

**Checklist before the essay is done** (each item is checkable; in our tests, essays that failed these were the
ones readers ranked lower):
1. *Best details.* Before drafting, list the three most distinctive details in the material: things only this
   person could say (a text message in capitals, a notebook with 61 entries). The essay keeps at least two.
2. *Crowding.* No paragraph carries more than two beats (events, ideas or confessions). Rich depth answers
   tempt the writer to use all of them; cut or move what does not serve the spine.
3. *Glosses.* Every non-English word, local term or acronym is explained on first use ("my äje, my
   grandmother").
4. *Setups before payoffs.* A callback, running joke or key word appears in a setup before the line that pays
   it off.
5. *The prompt.* The essay answers the prompt's actual question (if it asks what makes you lose track of time,
   the reader can say what does).
6. *Facts.* Small numbers and actions match the notes exactly ("refreshed the post", not "refreshed all 63
   comments").

Give the plan to the student as a beat outline. Each beat points to their material ("beat 2: the forged
signature, the two hours at the kitchen table") and to the depth answer it needs ("what did you think when
she laughed?"). Do not write the sentences.

## 6. Step 4: the student drafts

Tell the student to write the first draft fast, the way they'd tell it to a cousin in line at a family
reunion (MIT's test), without editing. Over the limit is fine for draft one; 650 is a limit, not a target.

## 7. Step 5: the admissions-reader critique

Read the draft once as an officer with 30 essays left today. Then give feedback in this order, quoting their
lines:
1. **The one biggest problem**, with a concrete step the student can take.
2. **So what?** After reading, what do you know about this person that you didn't before? If the answer is
   generic ("hard-working", "passionate"), say which scene could carry something more specific.
3. **Shape:** is there a turn? Does every paragraph move the spine forward?
4. **Opening:** scene, odd fact or flat statement? If it's a thesis, flag it.
5. **Reflection lines:** for each, name the scene that earns it. Flag reflections that could appear in anyone's
   essay, and flag the opposite problem: no reflection at all.
6. **Vulnerability:** is there a moment the writer is wrong, small or unresolved? Is hardship in proportion?
7. **AI-sounding lines** (see `tells.md` sections 2 and 4): stated lessons, journey/growth/resilience words,
   identity as a label, stock metaphors, tidy bow endings, kicker endings everywhere, thesaurus words.
8. **Voice:** lines that don't sound like them out loud. Ask a friend: "does this sound like me?"
9. **Length and cuts:** what to cut first to make room for the best scene.
10. **What works:** two or three specific lines and why. No general praise.

## 8. Step 6: corrections

Grammar, spelling, punctuation and clarity as a numbered list of discrete corrections, each quoting the
original words. Keep their vocabulary even when it is plainer than yours. If a sentence needs rewriting for
content, explain the problem and let them rewrite it.

## 9. Short answers and supplements

- **Short takes (35 to 50 words, or 280 characters):** read for personality; be specific and a little playful.
  Facts about a famous person or a place say nothing about the writer: make the answer about the writer's own
  connection to it.
- **Stanford roommate note:** habits, rituals, warnings, what you'll drag them to. What would living with you
  be like?
- **"Why us":** past tense evidence (what you already did that connects to a specific program or person), not
  brochure facts in the future tense (Yale). Phrases every applicant writes ("friendships that last a
  lifetime") cost space and say nothing.
- **Intellectual curiosity:** the question itself, how the student first ran into it, what they did about it,
  what they still can't answer.
- Each piece should show something the others don't. Short pieces are harder: every word counts.

## 10. Writing a sample essay for a fictional person

When asked to demonstrate (teaching, a class example, a test of this skill), you may write a full sample for a
clearly fictional student from supplied notes. Aim for the level of the best admitted essays, which means the
essay needs an arc and a "so what", not only accurate details:
- Draft in the two passes described in SKILL.md step 2: shape first, then a full Edit-mode pass on your own
  draft.
- Events, people, numbers, quotes and objects come only from the notes.
- Pick the spine with the highest stakes (where the student was wrong, ashamed, afraid or unsure) and build the
  whole essay around it. Bring in other material only where it sharpens that spine.
- Order by the spine's logic: setup with stakes, the turn, what changed and what hasn't.
- Reflection: use the student's own reflections from the notes first. You may add a short connective
  observation that links two facts in the notes (two things that rhyme, a pattern across two scenes), stated
  as the writer noticing it, never as a lesson. Do not add an event, a motive or a feeling the notes contradict
  or do not support.
- Run the paragraph-ending count from SKILL.md step 4; stay under the word limit.
- Put any "sample" label outside the essay text.
