\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{abstract}
Advice for making language-model prose read as human usually means banned-word lists or ``humanizer'' rewriters, which treat the surface as the problem and start from model text. A common request starts from a person's own text: ``Can you improve it?'' We test a prompt-level writing skill against real writing from before November 2022 in five genres: admitted college essays and short answers, winning science-fair abstracts, Turkish charity runners' appeals and English learners' essays. The skill puts content before vocabulary, forbids invented facts and minimally edits human text. Blind model judges, mostly the generator model itself, rated single texts. Written from notes, held-out model texts stayed separable from real ones (AUC 0.90 to 1.00); the skill raised judges' estimates of human authorship only for admitted essays and appeals. Asked to improve unseen real abstracts and appeals, plain generation changed 76\% and 75\% of words. The skill (version 10.1) changed 15\% and 24\%, and 91\% and 99\% of ratings judged its edits the writer's own (plain: 0\% and 27\%), though judges could still usually tell them from the originals (AUC 0.89 to 0.91). For English learners, an errors-only copyedit, which also changed some statements, cut the share judged the student's own from 100\% to 61\%. We release the skill (version~\shipver{}, untested on real writing) and code.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Experienced readers have learned to recognize machine-written prose. Words such as \emph{delve} rose sharply after 2022 \citep{kobak2025delving,liang2024monitoring}, and five heavy users of these tools, voting together, misclassified only 1 of 300 articles \citep{russell2025people}.

Two responses dominate practice. The first is the banned-word list, distributed in prompts, style guides and community ``skills''. The second is the commercial humanizer, which rewrites AI-generated text to avoid automated or human detection \citep{roe2026dramaturgies}. Each treats the surface as the problem. Wikipedia's editors, whose guide the most-starred humanizer skill is built on \citep{chen2026humanizer}, warn against exactly this: ``Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder'' \citep{wikipedia2026signs}. Both responses also ignore a second failure of model prose: models fill gaps with plausible details that nobody supplied \citep{ji2023hallucination}.

These responses concern text that a model wrote. A common situation gets less attention: a person who wrote a text asks a model to improve it. Grammar-only AI help can already shift meaning \citep{abdulhai2026distort}, and heavy rewriting erases the signals that identify an author \citep{malik2026erased}. A student who must submit a science-fair abstract or an application as their own work needs the edited text to stay theirs.

We built a prompt-level writing skill (a set of instructions an agent loads when a task matches) that audits content before vocabulary, is told never to add a fact the writer did not supply, and makes minimum edits to human text. In model-versus-model rankings on invented briefs, its version~4 (chosen over version~6 after the held-out round) ranked above plain generation (the same model with no skill) in 47 of 48 held-out judgments, but such rankings cannot show whether any text reads like a person. We therefore built five benchmarks on real writing from before November 2022, rated by blind model judges in piles that mix real and model texts, and tested two uses: writing from notes reconstructed from each real text, and editing the real text itself.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item An edit benchmark. Asked ``Can you improve it?'', plain generation changed 76\% and 75\% of the words of abstracts and appeals and added 3.34 and 2.74 statements per text; version~10.1 of the skill changed 15\% and 24\% and added 0.12 and 0.29, and 91\% and 99\% of ratings judged its edits the writer's own (plain: 0\% and 27\%).
  \item A writing-from-notes result: in four genres, held-out model texts stayed separable from real ones (area under the ROC curve, AUC, 0.90 to 1.00; 0.5 is chance), and the skill helped on admitted essays and appeals only.
  \item Five benchmarks anchored on real human writing, with development and test splits, versions~8 to~10.1 frozen by content hash before testing, blind single-text judging, and blind checks of unsupported statements and of meaning.
  \item Copyedit controls showing that fixing real writers' errors barely changed the share judged real, except for English learners, and a detector audit in which at least one of three open-source detectors scored 38 of 40 real admitted essays at 20\% or more.
  \item A released skill (version~\shipver{}: five modes, a genre router, a strict Edit mode), with every earlier version and the evaluation code; the release itself was not tested on real writing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Stylistic signatures of model text.} Large language model (LLM) adoption shifted the frequency of style words in abstracts and peer reviews \citep{kobak2025delving,liang2024monitoring,juzek2025delve,geng2024chatgpt}. Beyond words, instruction-tuned models write a noun-heavy, informationally dense register \citep{reinhart2025llms}, reuse syntactic templates from pre-training data \citep{shaib2024syntactic}, and, in Mandarin, overuse the corrective ``not X but Y'' construction \citep{peng2026notx}. Professional writers agree on a taxonomy of idiosyncrasies in model-written paragraphs, and automated edits built on it improved paragraphs less than expert edits did \citep{chakrabarty2025salvaged}. Syntactic features predict expert ``slop'' judgments better than lexical ones, though weakly (AUC 0.64 against 0.49) \citep{shaib2026slop}. Banning patterns token by token breaks output at about 2,000 patterns \citep{paech2026antislop}. AI generation leaves a stylometric footprint that AI editing of human text does not reproduce \citep{shan2026stylometric}.

\paragraph{Detecting AI text.} Detectors range from trained classifiers \citep{adam2026gptzero} to zero-shot statistics \citep{mitchell2023detectgpt,bao2024fastdetectgpt,hans2024binoculars}. The RAID benchmark shows that many detectors perform poorly at low false-positive rates and fail under attacks or unseen generators \citep{dugan2024raid}, and paraphrasing and simple evasion defeat them \citep{krishna2023paraphrasing,sadasivan2025reliably,perkins2024simple,weberwulff2023testing}. Detectors flag unedited non-native English writing as AI at high rates \citep{liang2023biased}, one flagged formal human writing at a 76.3\% false-positive rate \citep{creanga2026paradox}, and light AI editing is flagged more often than humanizer output \citep{karr2026detection}. Untrained humans detect AI text at about chance \citep{clark2021human,jakesch2023heuristics}. Watermarks offer a provenance signal that does not depend on style \citep{kirchenbauer2023watermark,dathathri2024synthid}.

\paragraph{AI help with human writing.} LLM-written admissions essays differ from those of 30,000 applicants and resemble the essays of privileged applicants \citep{lee2025poor,alvero2024hegemony}; essay style tracked household income before LLMs \citep{alvero2021essay}. Estimated LLM use in essays rose sharply in 2024 \citep{lee2026divide}, and essays flagged as AI-written were associated with lower admission in one graduate program \citep{isley2026admissions}. AI writing help homogenizes writing across authors \citep{padmakumar2024diversity,doshi2024creativity,agarwal2025homogenize}. In grammatical error correction, LLMs tend to make fluency edits beyond the minimal correction \citep{fang2023chatgptgec}. Our edit benchmark measures how much of a writer's wording and meaning survives a request to improve it.

\paragraph{LLMs as judges.} Strong LLM judges agree with human preferences about as often as humans agree with each other, but show position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair}, and models can recognize and prefer their own outputs \citep{panickssery2024llm}.

\section{The skill}
\label{sec:skill}

The released skill (version~\shipver{}) is a Markdown instruction file of 220 lines (the main file) with six reference files: a catalog of tells (patterns that readers associate with model-written text) with fixes, guides for personal essays, research, short answers, and messages and appeals, and before/after examples. We built it from two reviews. A survey of 16 open-source writing-skill repositories supplied the procedure of \citet{chen2026humanizer}, the ``never inject'' guardrails of \citet{bronsdon2026avoid}, and the minimum-edit principle and portability test of \citet{yang2026noaislop} (all three under the MIT License). An evidence-graded catalog of tells came from about 40 sources. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-versions} lists every version and its evidence.

\paragraph{Hard rules.} The skill never adds a fact, name, number, quote, source, feeling or memory that the user or the source did not provide; where a sentence needs one, it asks or leaves a bracketed question. When editing, it keeps every claim, hedge and number and never makes a claim stronger than its evidence. It uses no evasion tricks (typos, rare synonyms, look-alike characters), makes the minimum edit to human-written text, and coaches application essays instead of writing them.

\paragraph{Audit order.} Several well-measured signals are structural (participial tails \citep{reinhart2025llms}, corrective ``not X but Y'' framing \citep[measured in Mandarin by][]{peng2026notx}, importance flags, uniform sentence length), and expert readers rely on sentence structure and originality as well as vocabulary \citep{russell2025people,shaib2026slop}. The skill therefore audits a draft in a fixed order: content (the portability test: whether a sentence could move unchanged into someone else's text), staging (announcing a point instead of making it), inflation (ordinary facts presented as significant), stated lessons, rhythm, paragraph endings, tone, vocabulary and format. A strong tell justifies an edit on one sighting; weak tells, such as a single transition word, only when several cluster. A final check looks for tells that rewriting creates, such as uniform rhythm and aphoristic paragraph closers, which a blind test found more often after a humanizer pass \citep{lenk2026blind}.

\paragraph{Modes.} \emph{Write} runs the full procedure; for narratives it drafts in two passes, first for shape and then as an editor of its own draft. \emph{Edit} handles the user's own text. \emph{Rebuild}, for drafts the user says an AI wrote, checks every fact, quote and reading against the user's material before writing fresh sentences. \emph{Detect} quotes passages and names patterns without guessing authorship. \emph{Coach}, used for application essays and short answers, interviews the student, proposes a structure, critiques drafts and lists corrections, and does not write the essay or example sentences built from the student's story.

\paragraph{Genre router.} A table in the main file (the mode table) sends each request to a mode and one reference file: personal statements and short answers to Coach; research abstracts and reports to Edit of the researcher's own draft; emails, messages and appeals to Write or Edit; other prose to Write or Edit with short genre notes. Each genre guide's main rules came from comparing model writing with real writing of that kind on development data; the messages guide was built from appeals only, and the activity-list advice follows admissions offices' published advice. Since version~12 the mode table tells the agent to read only the matching reference, and version~13 moves the table to the end of the main file (Section~\ref{sec:rank}).

\paragraph{Strict Edit mode.} The edit benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:edit}) used version~10.1, whose Edit mode asked only for the minimum effective edit that keeps the writer's voice. Version~11, written from that benchmark's development halves, makes the minimum edit even when the user says ``improve it''. It fixes outright errors (spelling, grammar, agreement, articles, punctuation) and anything that blocks the meaning, and it cuts clear AI tells. It keeps the writer's sentences, order, vocabulary, repetitions, paragraphing and punctuation habits. It adds no transitions, thesis preview, definitions, examples, statistics, authorities, idioms or reasons, and never makes a claim stronger. For a writer whose first language is not English, it fixes the grammar without making the phrasing sound native. After the text it lists up to five bigger changes, one line each, without writing them. For a learner's practice or graded essay it returns the corrected text and the list, never a rewrite. Versions~12 and~13 keep this Edit mode unchanged and were not rerun on the edit benchmark.

\paragraph{Corpora.} Table~\ref{tab:corpora} lists the corpora. The admitted essays were drawn at random from 90 essays that Hamilton, Johns Hopkins, Tufts, MIT and Connecticut College published as admitted applicants' work \citep{hamilton2026essays,jhu2026essays,tufts2026essays,mit2026blogs,conncoll2026essays}: 28 personal statements and 12 supplemental essays. The 28 short answers come from 66 answers (40 to 350 words) that MIT and Tufts applicants published on admissions blogs \citep{mit2026blogs,tufts2026essays} and in MIT's student newspaper \citep{thetech2016essays}; we favored international applicants and those in science, technology, engineering or mathematics (STEM) or economics, took at most two per writer, and split them by writer. The abstracts are winning abstracts of the International Science and Engineering Fair (ISEF) from 2014 to 2021 \citep{sfs2026isef}: 40 drawn at random, stratified by group, from 269 that passed screening (English, 150 to 300 words), and 32 others for editing. The appeals are texts in which runners on a Turkish charity-run donation platform explained, for events up to 2022, why they ran. Cleaning and screening left 84 texts of 60 to 300 words in a first-person voice; the first 32 are development texts, 16 test texts were drawn at random from the rest, and 31 others were used for editing. The learner essays are 32 essays by English learners in grades 9 to 12 from the ELLIPSE corpus \citep{crossley2023ellipse} (250 to 500 words, overall proficiency 2.5 to 4.0 of 5, at most three per prompt).

\paragraph{Writing from notes.} For each real text, an agent reconstructed the notes the writer could have had beforehand: every fact, number, quote and feeling, regrouped by topic, without the text's order, images or closing lines, and with copying capped at runs of six words for essays, five for appeals and four for abstracts. Each condition (plain generation, skill versions and, for essays, a word-list prompt; Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-rank}) wrote the text from the notes alone at the real text's length. Short answers were the exception: an agent refused to turn real admitted students' personal answers into source material for new application answers, so we wrote notes for an invented applicant for each real question instead (28 applicants from 16 countries). The real answers serve only as the human anchor, so judges may have recognized model answers partly by their invented content.

\paragraph{Editing.} For the edit benchmark, plain generation and the skill each received every real text with one of two requests, ``Can you improve it?'' or ``Can you fix the grammar and mistakes?'', after a one-line framing (a practice essay for English class; a science-fair abstract; a campaign page). Each agent handled two texts as separate requests and returned the revised text only, so the skill's list of suggested changes was not produced. The same model also made an errors-only copyedit of each text, told to fix outright errors and change nothing else.

\paragraph{Judging.} Judges read piles of 4 essays, or of 12 to 16 shorter texts, by different writers, never two versions of one writer's text in a pile. Each was told that any mix of real and model texts was possible and gave each text a probability that the writer wrote it ($p$, 0 to 100; for edits, with no AI writing or editing), a quality score from 1 to 10, and the evidence. Each genre had two judge roles: an editor and an admissions reader (essays), an admissions reader and an essay coach (short answers), an ISEF judge and a journal editor (abstracts), a donor and an NGO editor (appeals), and an English teacher and a writing researcher (learner essays). \genmodel{} wrote every text and was every judge except a third essay judge, an admissions reader on \judgemodel{}, a different model of the same family. Each judge got its own random piles.

\paragraph{Measures.} We report the share of ratings with $p \geq 50$ (judged real, or for edits judged the writer's own), mean $p$, the AUC for telling the real texts from a version, and differences paired by text with 95\% bootstrap intervals over texts. Every edit was made by a model, so the share judged the writer's own counts how often judges missed an edit; words changed (the only measure that needs no judge) and the meaning check measure how much of the writer's text survived. A blind checker counted unsupported statements (statements the notes do not support) in each model text. A blind meaning check compared each edit with its original: statements added, removed or changed, and voice kept (1 to 10). Words changed is the share of the original's words that an edit changed, averaged over texts: a word-level alignment (Python's difflib, junk heuristic off) counts the longer of the original and edited span for every changed span, and the total is divided by the original's word count. Words are whitespace-separated tokens, so a punctuation fix changes the word it is attached to.

\paragraph{Splits and freezing.} Each corpus was split into development and test texts before any judging (edit texts 16/16 per genre; appeals 16/15). Versions were developed on development texts, except that version~10.1 followed a reading of version~10's short-answer test report and version~11 was built after we saw pooled aggregates of the version~10.1 edit run. Versions~8, 9 and~10 were frozen, with a content hash recorded, before any test text was written with them, and version~10.1 before the edit and ablation texts, after rewriting the 12 test short answers in its own check. Version~11's changelog records a hash and a freeze before testing, but was written after the test run. Test texts seen by accident (judge comments on four test essays; notes of five test appeals) were randomly swapped for unseen ones before testing.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Editing real writing}
\label{sec:edit}

Table~\ref{tab:edit} gives the main result; every text was new to version~10.1. Asked to improve a writer's text, plain generation rewrote most of it (76\% of the words of abstracts, 75\% of appeals, 90\% of learner essays) and added 2.74 to 3.91 statements per text that the writer never made, such as a thesis preview, a definition, an authority or a stronger conclusion. Paired by text, the skill's ``improve'' edits scored 39.5 points of $p$ above plain generation's on abstracts [+36.0, +42.9] and 35.9 on appeals [+30.5, +41.7]. Most still changed some meaning: the meaning check found no change in only 6 of 32 abstract edits and 14 of 31 appeal edits (plain generation: none), and they removed 0.66 and 0.26 statements per text (plain generation: 2.31 and 1.03). Judges could still usually tell them from the originals (AUC 0.89 to 0.91 for each judge), and their $p$ was below the errors-only copyedits' (abstracts $-12.8$ [$-17.9$, $-8.0$]; appeals $-6.6$ [$-9.3$, $-4.3$]).

Asked only to fix the grammar, plain generation came close to the skill on appeals: 97\% and 98\% of ratings judged the edits the writer's own, although the skill's mean $p$ was 7.1 points higher [+3.9, +10.7]. On abstracts it did not: plain generation's grammar pass rewrote 36\% of the words, added 0.91 statements per text and was judged the writer's own in 30\% of ratings, against 91\% for the skill (skill minus plain, mean $p$ +27.4 [+23.4, +31.5]). On ``improve'', the skill's edits were judged lower in quality than plain rewrites (abstracts $-0.28$ [$-0.47$, $-0.09$], appeals $-0.41$ [$-0.61$, $-0.23$], learner essays $-0.94$ [$-1.17$, $-0.70$]) but above the originals on abstracts and appeals (6.42 against 6.11; 6.10 against 5.57). On ``fix the grammar'', quality did not differ (abstracts 0.00 [$-0.22$, +0.22]; appeals $-0.07$ [$-0.26$, +0.14]).

English learners' essays were the exception. An errors-only copyedit alone changed 16\% of the words and cut the share of ratings judging the essay the student's own from 100\% to 61\%. At most 5\% of ratings judged a plain or skill edit the student's own (AUC 1.00 for each judge). The skill's ``improve'' edit still scored 11.3 points above plain generation's [+8.3, +14.2], with fewer added statements (0.62 against 3.91).

\paragraph{Version 11.} On the development halves, judges said the skill's learner-essay edits were given away by template transitions (``First'', ``Additionally''), definitions and authorities the student had not written, native idioms, and uniformly clean grammar. Version~11 changed only the Edit-mode paragraph, and we tested it on the test halves in a new judging run (Table~\ref{tab:v11}). Its ``improve'' edits beat version~10.1's in all three genres on the development texts it was written from, but on test texts only on appeals (+4.1 [+2.3, +6.1]; abstracts +0.2 [$-3.1$, +3.6]; learner essays +1.8 [$-6.1$, +10.7]). In a separate meaning-check run, more of its test ``improve'' edits left the meaning unchanged (abstracts 7 of 16, appeals 15 of 15, learner essays 7 of 16) than version~10.1's had in the first run (1, 6 and 0). Judged quality fell on test appeals ($-0.30$ [$-0.47$, $-0.13$]) and learner essays ($-0.56$ [$-0.81$, $-0.31$]), not detectably on abstracts ($-0.12$ [$-0.41$, +0.16]).

Table~\ref{tab:write} gives the held-out results. In every genre, every real text was judged real in every rating, and model texts stayed separable (AUC 0.90 to 1.00) with or without the skill. On essays the two \genmodel{} judges separated real essays from version~8's almost perfectly (AUC 0.98); the pooled 0.90 includes the third judge, which called 94\% to 100\% of every version's essays real. Judges rated model texts higher in quality than the real ones in appeals, abstracts and short answers, and about equal on essays.

The skill helped in two genres. On essays, version~8 (built on 24 development essays) beat plain generation by 14.7 points of $p$ [+9.3, +20.3], the word-list prompt by 12.7 [+6.8, +18.3] and version~7 by 7.5 [+2.4, +13.1]. It had 0.19 unsupported statements per essay against 3.06 for plain generation, with quality $-0.31$ [$-0.58$, $-0.04$] against version~7 and $-0.40$ [$-0.71$, $-0.04$] against plain generation. On appeals, version~9 (built on 32 development appeals) beat version~8 by 11.7 points [+5.5, +17.9] with no detectable change in quality, and in a later run that reused these test appeals, version~10.1 beat plain generation by 15.8 [+8.8, +23.0].

The skill did not help measurably in the other two genres: version~10 scored +4.0 [$-0.3$, +7.9] over plain generation on abstracts and +2.2 [$-1.7$, +6.0] on short answers, where no model answer was judged real. Judges rated model short answers above the real ones (7.00 and 7.04 against 4.38) and still identified every one. In the development round they named signs of over-crafting: stacked precise figures and names, hooks built on a labeled object, comic asides, metaphors set up early and paid off later, ``honest limitation'' passages and hedged endings (hedges cited in 50 to 53\% of ratings of model answers, none of real ones). Version~10 added more unsupported statements than plain generation (1.08 against 0.58 per answer), mostly by turning the invented applicants' doubts into commitments, which version~10.1 forbids (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-versions}).

\subsection{Errors are a small part of the signal, except for learners}
\label{sec:errors}

In the first development rounds, judges cited errors in 85\% of their ratings of real admitted essays, and errors and non-native phrasing in 56\% and 67\% of their ratings of real abstracts. Fixing them barely changes the share judged real, though it lowers $p$. On 24 development essays a copyeditor agent fixed only outright errors (0 to 6 per essay, under 1\% of words): every copyedited essay was still judged real, $p$ changed by $-1.9$ [$-3.1$, $-0.9$], and AUC against the originals was 0.61. On 24 development abstracts the copyedits stayed 100\% judged real ($-0.8$ [$-2.2$, +0.7]; AUC 0.58 and 0.53), and judges still cited non-native but grammatical phrasing in 52\% of their ratings. In the edit benchmark, copyedits changed $p$ by $-3.7$ on abstracts [$-5.7$, $-1.8$] and $-6.4$ on appeals [$-8.8$, $-4.3$]; 100\% and 98\% were still judged the writer's own, although judges told copyedited appeals from the originals with AUC 0.81 and 0.77.

Learner essays went the other way: $p$ changed by $-40.0$ [$-45.5$, $-34.5$], and AUC was 0.995 and 1.00. The largest drop came with the largest copyedit (16\% of words, against under 1\% for development essays and 3\% and 6\% for abstracts and appeals in the edit benchmark), but these sizes come from different runs and genres. The learner copyedits were also not purely errors-only: the meaning check found 0.62 changed statements per essay (none in abstracts or appeals), and no change of meaning in only 20 of 32 essays. To these judges, removing a learner's errors, and possibly some phrasing, removed much of what marked the essay as theirs.

\subsection{Which parts of the skill matter}
\label{sec:ablation}

We wrote the test appeals and abstracts from notes with five variants of version~10.1 in a separate judging run (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}): the full skill, the skill without the tells catalog, the main file alone, the genre reference alone, and a compact main file of 1,094 words. No variant's paired difference from the full skill excluded zero in either genre. On abstracts the full skill did not beat plain generation (+1.4 [$-3.2$, +5.8]) and was judged lower in quality ($-0.38$ [$-0.69$, $-0.09$]). Unsupported statements differed more (point estimates on 16 appeals, no intervals): 1.06 per appeal without the main file and 0.81 with the compact one, against 0.44 for the full skill and 0.69 for plain generation. This suggests, without a test, that the main file's hard rules and fact diff (a check of every name, number and claim against the notes) carry the no-invention behavior. We detected no contribution of the tells catalog (without it, minus full skill: abstracts +1.0 [$-5.0$, +6.6]; appeals +1.1 [$-3.7$, +6.5]).

\subsection{Rankings among model texts, and a regression}
\label{sec:rank}

Before building the benchmarks on real writing, judges ranked texts from several conditions against each other on invented briefs, with a facts file in hand (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-rank}). On 16 held-out briefs in ten genres, version~4's Write mode ranked above plain generation in 47 of 48 judgments, above a word-list prompt in 43 and above a popular humanizer skill in 44 (Wilcoxon $p \le 0.003$ for each, tests over briefs). We chose version~4 over version~6 after seeing these results. Judges could see condition folder names in file paths. Rankings depended on the panel. Rules distilled from these judges' comments (versions~5 and~6) did not beat version~4 even within this design. Against the 16 test admitted essays, version~7, which kept version~4's Write procedure, beat plain generation (+7.1 [+2.7, +12.2]) but not detectably the word-list prompt (+5.2 [$-0.2$, +10.3]), and judges told all three from the real essays (AUC 0.91 to 0.96). Coach mode (version~6 files) was tested only in the ranking study, where a counselor judge preferred its feedback for all six simulated students (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-coach}).

\paragraph{Regression on general writing.} Versions~10 and~10.1 had been tested only on their own genres. On the 16 held-out ranking briefs, two judges ranked plain generation, version~9 and version~10.1 blind (32 judgments; Table~\ref{tab:regression}). This design measures preference among model texts, so we use it only to detect damage to general writing. Version~10.1 ranked below version~9 in 28 of 32 judgments and was no better than plain generation (no test over briefs). Every reference file was supplied with every brief, so short-answer rules (no contractions, no quips, no causal links the student did not make) appeared to carry into other genres. Version~12's mode table says to read only the matching reference, and version~12 loosens those rules. In its check every version, version~9 included, was given only its genre's reference, so the check tests the loosened rules, not whether an agent reads one file. On the 20 development briefs of the ranking study, version~12 still trailed version~9 (difference in mean rank $-0.65$ [$-1.2$, $-0.05$], positive meaning better; bootstrap over briefs) and was no better than plain generation (+0.12 [$-0.7$, +0.95]); plain generation ranked first in every judgment of the eight personal-statement and short-answer briefs. The released version~13 only moves the mode table to the end of the main file. It was run only on the other 12 briefs (arguments, explainers, literary analyses), without plain generation. There, no difference in mean rank among versions~12, 13 and two independent version~9 samples excluded zero (version~13 minus version~12: +0.46 [$-0.38$, +1.25]; Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-ablation}). This in-sample check cannot show whether version~13 removes the regression.

\subsection{Detectors and surface features}
\label{sec:detectors}

We scored texts with three open-source detectors, D1 to D3 \citep{desklib2025detector,fakespot2025detector,superannotate2024detector}, described in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-rank}. A text passes at 20 when all three score it below 20\% (below which Turnitin shows only an asterisk; \citealp{turnitin2026report}) and passes at 50 when all three score it below 50\%. On admitted essays the detectors reversed the expected order: on average all three scored real essays above every model version, and at least one scored 38 of 40 real essays at 20\% or more. On the edits (English only) they agreed with the judges in direction. The skill's ``improve'' edits of abstracts scored lower than plain generation's on all three detectors (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-edit}), though few passed at 50 (9\% against 0\%) and none at 20. On learner essays an errors-only copyedit alone moved D1's mean from 11 to 50 and cut the share passing at 50 from 78\% to 25\%; no model edit passed more than 3\%. The detectors also flagged real texts: only 78\% of real learner essays and 34\% of real abstracts passed at 50 (41\% and 25\% at 20).

A logistic regression on 22 surface features, cross-validated within each genre, separated real from model texts almost perfectly for essays (AUC 0.997; 40 real, 185 model), well for short answers (0.92), and less for appeals (0.80) and abstracts (0.77; 0.64 when trained on the other genres). For abstracts, where judges reached AUC 1.00, these features explain little; the judges' reasons point to content and structure, which we did not measure.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Edit, don't write.} Minimum edits by version~10.1 stayed much closer to the writer on abstracts and appeals, while plain generation answered the same request with a rewrite. For text a writer must submit as their own, this suggests answering ``improve it'' with the minimum edit, at a cost in judged quality (0.28 to 0.94 points below plain rewrites). An unchanged text would maximize the judged-own share, so that share must be read with quality and words changed. Version~11's test appeal edits were close to errors-only copyedits (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-edit}), so what its strict mode adds beyond a copyedit rests on the list of bigger changes, which we did not evaluate.

\paragraph{In short answers, judges preferred model prose and still rejected it.} Judges rated model short answers above the real ones in quality yet judged none real (Section~\ref{sec:write}). We expect, but did not test, that rules pushing a model toward crafted prose raise its judged quality and make it easier to recognize.

\paragraph{Learners' errors read as voice.} \citet{liang2023biased} found that detectors flag unedited non-native writing as AI. Our judges did not: every rating judged the uncorrected learner essays the student's own. They did separate the errors-only copyedits, which a model made, from the originals (AUC 0.995 and 1.00), though 61\% of ratings still judged them the student's own. A human tutor's corrections would remove the same errors, so we expect, but did not test, that any fluency-based judgment of AI use would also flag learners who received permitted grammar help. Version~11's corrected text for learners, which keeps their grammatical phrasing, did not score detectably above version~10.1's on test essays (+1.8 [$-6.1$, +10.7]), and we did not evaluate its list of bigger changes.

\paragraph{Preference is not human-likeness.} Rankings among model texts put version~4 far ahead of both baselines, but versions~10.1 to~12 were not detectably ahead of plain generation in the later checks; against real essays only version~7's lead over plain generation was detectable (Section~\ref{sec:rank}). Rules distilled from real texts held on test texts in essays and appeals but not detectably in abstracts or short answers, and when every reference was supplied, rules for one genre appeared to hurt another. Style guidance should be measured against real writing of the genre it targets.

\section{Conclusion}

Against real pre-2022 writing in five genres, model texts written from notes stayed recognizable to the judges on the generator model, and the skill closed part of the gap only for admitted essays and appeals. Asked to improve a writer's own text, plain generation rewrote it, while version~10.1's minimum edits were judged the writer's own in 91\% and 99\% of ratings on abstracts and appeals. For text the writer must submit as their own, our results suggest that an assistant should fix outright errors, keep the wording and voice, and list bigger changes; we tested this with one generator, model judges and three genres, and did not evaluate the list.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{No human raters.} Apart from a one-person pilot, excluded from analysis, no human rated any text. The main open question is whether human readers agree with the model judges; a rating survey of texts from the invented briefs is released for this purpose.

\paragraph{Model judges.} \genmodel{} wrote every text and was every judge except the third judge of the essay benchmark and of the held-out ranking round, which ran on a different model of the same family; models can recognize and favor their own output \citep{panickssery2024llm}. Every condition was produced by the same model, so self-preference does not by itself favor one condition, but judges may share the generator's taste, and self-recognition may inflate how well judges separate model texts from real ones. Both judge roles in a genre ran on the same model, so their agreement is not evidence from independent raters. The third essay judge called 94\% to 100\% of every version's essays real, so separability depends on the judge; in the held-out ranking round, the two same-model judges agreed with each other (mean Kendall $\tau$ 0.78) more than with the third judge (0.55 to 0.62). The skill's genre rules (versions~8 to~10) were built from what these judges cited on development texts, so our test sets are held out over texts but not over judges, and a gain may partly reflect these judges' cues rather than what human readers notice. Judge prompts said that any mix of real and model texts was possible, which primes judges to look for AI writing. Judged quality comes from the same model judges and may reward the polish that marks model text, so the quality costs we report for minimum edits may overstate what a human reader would see. Intervals are not corrected for multiple comparisons; we treat single intervals that barely exclude zero as tentative.

\paragraph{Judging runs.} Runs drifted, mostly for model texts. On the same 16 test abstracts, plain generation was judged real in 6\% of ratings in one run and 22\% in another (real abstracts: mean $p$ 87.3 and 86.8; real test appeals 91.6 and 91.2), and the same version~10.1 learner-essay edits were judged the student's own in 3\% of ratings in the main edit run (both halves pooled) and 19\% of ratings of the test-half edits in the version~11 run, whose piles held no plain rewrites. Absolute shares depend on the other texts in a pile, so we compare versions within a run. The exceptions are marked: the two appeal blocks of Table~\ref{tab:write}, version~10.1's short-answer check, and the meaning-check columns of Table~\ref{tab:v11}.

\paragraph{Corpora.} The real texts were selected by others: colleges chose and often edited the essays they published, ISEF abstracts are winners, and the appeals come from one platform. As public web pages they may be in the models' training data, which could help judges recognize them. The corpora lean toward non-native writers: 14 of the 40 abstracts written from notes and 7 of the 32 edited ones are by Turkish winners, and the short answers were chosen to favor international applicants. The Turkish appeals were judged by models whose reading of Turkish we did not check against native speakers, and the detectors could not score them. The notes behind each model text were reconstructed by a model from the finished text; real notes would be messier, and the notes' spoken register shaped what every condition wrote. Short answers were written for invented applicants, so judges may partly have caught invented content. The copyedits and the meaning check of each edit were made by a model, and the learner copyedits changed some statements (0.62 per essay). Because the real texts stay private, others cannot rerun the benchmarks exactly.

\paragraph{Data reuse.} Several versions were developed on the same development texts, so development gains are in-sample. Version~11 was built after we saw pooled (development and test) aggregates of the version~10.1 edit run; its rules came from judge comments on the development halves only. The test appeals and abstracts were reused in the ablation run after earlier tests on them. Version~10.1's short-answer check followed a reading of version~10's test report. The 16 held-out ranking briefs, also used to choose version~4 over version~6, became development data after the regression. The 20 development briefs of the ranking study were reused for the version~12 and~13 checks.

\paragraph{Scope.} One generator model wrote every text. Every result against real writing was measured with versions~7 to~11. The released version~\shipver{} was checked only on 12 development ranking briefs, without plain generation, where it could be told apart from neither version~9 nor version~12, and in a five-request routing probe that version~12 also passed. Its Edit text is version~11's, and its short-answer changes were not tested against real short answers. Neither the word-list prompt nor the humanizer was run on the edit benchmark, and the humanizer was never judged against real writing. The skill's list of suggested changes was not produced in the edit benchmark and was never rated, and Edit mode was not ablated. No plain condition was asked for the smallest possible edit, so ``fix the grammar'' is the closest minimal-instruction baseline. The ranking study used fictional, model-written student personas, and Coach mode was tested only with simulated students and version~6's personal-essay guide, which has changed since; short-answer coaching was not tested. Each condition produced one text per item, so a result on an item also reflects the luck of that sample. A safety filter repeatedly refused one judge pile of abstract edits, so ratings per abstract version are uneven (60 to 67; 64 planned), and in the version~11 development run abstracts had 20 to 30 of 32 ratings per version. We do not report the share of words changed for version~11. Commercial detectors used by schools were not tested, and the detector results rest on small, selected human samples.

\section*{Ethical considerations}

\paragraph{Dual use.} Earlier versions of the skill often lowered detector scores (version~4 on invented briefs, version~10.1 on edited abstracts; Section~\ref{sec:detectors}; Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-rank}), and a student could use the skill to pass off model-written work as their own. Version~10.1's minimum edits were also rarely identified as AI-edited (91\% and 99\% of ratings judged them the writer's own on abstracts and appeals). The released version was measured on neither. It uses no evasion tricks (typos, homoglyphs, invisible characters) of the kind studied in the attack literature \citep{krishna2023paraphrasing,sadasivan2025reliably}, but on six held-out argument, explainer and literary-analysis briefs every plain text was flagged by at least one detector and every text version~4 wrote passed all three. Its mode table sends school essays to Coach when they will be graded as the student's own work, which it learns only from the user; our evaluation prompts asked for Write mode or said the students were fictional, and got finished essays. For application essays it coaches and does not write, and it tells the student that the Common App, Yale and many programs count substantive AI-written content as not the applicant's own work \citep{commonapp2023fraud,yale2026ai}. It tells users that recent models of the generator's family add a provenance watermark \citep{anthropic2026marks,bleeping2026watermark} and that the skill is not a way to hide AI use.

\paragraph{Detector developers.} Because we report low detector scores for model text and high scores for human essays, we \TBD{notified the developers of all three detectors on <date>}, at least 30 days before any preprint.

\paragraph{Real writers' texts.} The five corpora contain real people's writing, much of it by school students. We release only counts and aggregate scores; the real texts and the model texts derived from them stay private. The released detector score files identify essays by source and number, not by writer, so no named person is linked to an AI score. We never quote a text from these corpora in this paper. When an agent refused to turn real students' personal answers into material for new application answers, we accepted the objection and redesigned the short-answer benchmark around invented applicants (Section~\ref{sec:bench}).

\paragraph{Fairness to learners.} Our judges and detectors rated learner essays as more likely AI-written or AI-edited once a model had corrected their errors (Sections~\ref{sec:errors} and~\ref{sec:detectors}), so neither should be read as a test of authorship, least of all for non-native writers.

\paragraph{Human raters.} A planned human rating study, not reported here, uses volunteers who give informed consent, collects no names or contact details, and \TBD{has approval from <school or teacher>; keep it unnamed in the review version}. Raters under 18 take part only with a parent's or guardian's permission, which the survey asks for but cannot verify. The generated texts from our invented briefs and the survey's answer key are public, so a rater who searched for a passage could learn its condition; the survey asks raters not to.

AI agents (\genmodel{} and \judgemodel{}) did a large share of this work: they surveyed the literature and existing skills, drafted every version of the skill, generated all evaluation texts, acted as judges, wrote the analysis scripts, and drafted this paper. \section{Admitted essays: developing version 8}
\label{sec:appendix-essays}

A first design, finding the real essay among four essays telling one story, failed because the three model essays shared paraphrases of the same notes (all 20 judgments found it), so we switched to single-text piles. Every text was normalized (straight quotes, single spaces, no title). On the 24 development essays, version~7's essays differed from the real ones in ways its own rules amplified. They used ``really'' 21.0 times per 10,000 words, against 4.7 in real essays and 13.7 in plain generation, and ``kind of'' 12.1 times (real essays never). They had twice the contractions and shorter sentences (15.5 against 17.9 words), and stated a lesson 0.2 times per essay against 1.2. Told to sound like ``a smart teenager talking'' and not to write ``taught me'', the model carried the notes' spoken register into the essay and ended on a shrug (``I don't actually know.'') where admitted students ended on a conviction. In the first round (360 ratings), every real essay was judged real in every rating, the two \genmodel{} judges put every real essay above every model essay (AUC 1.00), and the real essays got the lowest quality scores (5.97 against 6.14 to 6.42).

Coder agents, blind to version, marked which of 16 cues each judge rationale cited and in which direction. Tidy structure (a neat arc, motifs and callbacks, everything tied together) was cited as a sign of a model in 75\% to 86\% of ratings of every model version and in 6\% of ratings of real essays. A stated moral was cited as a sign of a real student in 54\% of ratings of real essays and as a sign of a model in about half of ratings of model essays.

The development rounds produced three candidates. The first (write, don't talk; state the conclusion) removed the filler but turned stiff, and its round-one gain did not replicate. The second, released as version~8, kept those changes, loosened the structure, stopped adding links between scenes that the notes do not make, and required one whole-hearted conclusion; in round two it beat version~7 by 6.3 points of $p$ [+2.2, +10.5] and cut the share of ratings citing tidy structure from 86\% to 51\%. The third (8c) added a trying-hard voice, scenes and attempted images: mean $p$ tied with version~8 ($-1.7$ [$-5.1$, +1.7]) and quality rose 0.18, but it invented about five times as many small details (0.58 against 0.12 unsupported statements per essay; version~7 had 1.50), so we froze version~8 before any test essay was written with it. On the 16 test essays, the two \genmodel{} judges rated 47\% of version~8's essays real, against 22\% of version~7's and 9\% of plain and word-list essays; their mean $p$ for version~8 was 11.4 points above version~7's [+4.5, +18.7]. On the 16 test essays, version~8 also passed all three detectors less often than version~7 (6 of 16 against 10), plausibly because it moved toward the register of the real essays, which the detectors flag most.

\section{Earlier model-versus-model evaluation}
\label{sec:appendix-rank}

We evaluated the skill in three rounds, after a seven-brief pilot that shaped versions~1 to~4: a development round on 20 briefs, a version comparison on the same briefs, and a held-out round on 16 new briefs written after versions~4 and~6 were frozen.

\paragraph{Briefs.} The development set has 20 briefs in five genres, four each (Table~\ref{tab:briefs}). Four fictional students, each with about 1,100 to 1,600 words of interview notes and answers to the skill's depth questions, supply the material for a Common App personal statement and a 250-word supplemental answer. The other briefs are 11th-grade argument essays and science-club explainers with no facts file, and literary analyses restricted to four passages from a public-domain novel or play, copied from Project Gutenberg and checked by script. The held-out set has 16 briefs: two new students (a personal statement and a short answer each), two arguments, two explainers, two literary analyses, and six briefs in five genres outside the development set. Every brief names the only permitted source of specifics; the people in the facts files are fictional.

\paragraph{Conditions.} Fresh \genmodel{} agents wrote every text. Plain (P): the model's default writing. Word list (L): the same request plus an instruction to avoid 24 common AI words, six stock phrases and constructions, and em dashes and to vary sentence length. Humanizer edit (H): the most-starred of the 11 humanizer skills we surveyed (about 59,000 GitHub stars on 20 October 2026) \citep{chen2026humanizer}, applied to P's draft. The skill conditions were the Write mode, the Edit mode applied to P's draft, and from version~5 on a Rebuild mode. On briefs about fictional people, the skill and humanizer prompts said the people were fictional, so that the skill would write the piece instead of switching to Coach mode.

\paragraph{Judges.} Judge agents read all texts for a brief in a shuffled order under neutral labels, but each judge opened the texts by file path, and the path contained the condition's folder name. Blinding was therefore imperfect: in 6 of the 128 writing-panel judgments the explanation names a text's condition letter. The judges were a strict editor and the genre's real reader. Each ranked the texts, scored each from 1 to 10 on quality, human-likeness, specificity, voice and structure, rated AI suspicion from 1 to 5, and quoted every claim not supported by the facts file (or every invented statistic, source or factual error when there was none), a coarser, judge-based version of checking generated facts against a knowledge source \citep{min2023factscore}. In the held-out round a third judge ran on \judgemodel{}. We report mean rank with bootstrap intervals over judgments, a Friedman test on per-brief mean ranks, and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-brief mean ranks, so the unit of every test is the brief (16 held-out), not the judgment (48). Resampling briefs instead of judgments widens version~4's held-out interval from [1.62, 2.23] to [1.52, 2.38]. Over the 15 held-out pairwise tests, Holm's correction keeps every comparison we report as significant below an adjusted $p$ of 0.025.

\paragraph{Held-out results.} Table~\ref{tab:heldout} and Figure~\ref{fig:rank} give the result. We chose which of versions~4 and~6 to release after seeing these results, so version~4's figures are the better of two candidates. Version~4's Write mode ranked first in 23 of 48 judgments and had a mean rank of 1.92 out of 6, against 5.65 for plain generation (Friedman $\chi^2 = 51.0$, $p < 10^{-8}$). The Rebuild mode, applied to the plain draft, ranked above the humanizer applied to the same draft in 42 of 48 judgments ($p = 0.0008$) and had the fewest unsupported claims (0.79). Version~6, which added rules derived from a diagnosis of the development judgments, ranked above version~4 in 21 of 48 judgments ($p = 0.35$); one judge called its Rebuild of an argument brief ``arithmetic-heavy for an English class.'' The third judge, on a different model, gave the same order (version~4 1.94, plain 5.38). By genre, version~4 ranked best on personal statements (1.33), arguments (1.33), literary analysis (1.33), the emails (1.67) and the news article (1.00); the word-list prompt beat it on the blog post (1.67 against 4.67) and narrowly on the cover letter (2.00 against 2.33). With one or two briefs per genre these are descriptive.

\section{Coaching experiment: details}
\label{sec:appendix-coach}

For each of the six fictional students, an agent playing the student wrote a first draft of the personal statement from their notes, with no style guidance. The draft then received feedback from the skill's Coach mode (version~6 files) or from the plain model asked for feedback, and the student agent revised it once with each. Two blind judges ranked the first draft and both revisions, and a counselor agent rated both pieces of feedback. Both kinds of feedback improved the first drafts: the draft ranked last in all 12 judgments. The revision made with Coach-mode feedback ranked above the one made with plain feedback in 7 of 12 judgments (not significant), with fewer unsupported claims (1.67 against 2.75) and lower AI suspicion (1.83 against 2.08). The counselor judge preferred Coach mode's feedback for all six students (usefulness 8.67 against 7.17 of 10) and found less invented content in it (1.17 items per student against 5.17). One plain response supplied what the counselor called ``the essay's interpretive center,'' a line the student never said, and framed it as his own answer; the counselor counted this as substantive content of the kind admissions policies forbid \citep{commonapp2023fraud,yale2026ai}. The simulated students copied almost nothing from either feedback (on average under 1\% of word 6-grams; at most 3.7\% in one revision). Coach mode's responses were about 1.5 times longer (2,264 words against 1,468), which may have helped them with the counselor, since model judges tend to favor longer answers \citep{zheng2023judging}. The feedback comparison rests on one counselor judgment per student, from a model that could see each condition's folder name in the file path, and four of the six students are development personas.
