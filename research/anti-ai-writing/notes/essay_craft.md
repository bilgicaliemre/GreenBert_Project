# Admitted essays: what works, from the reader's side (craft notes for the anti-AI writing skill)

Researched 2026-10-20. Every quote below was read on the page cited unless it is marked
**SNIPPET** (seen only in a search-engine summary because the page itself was blocked) or
**UNVERIFIED**. Local copies of the fetched pages are in `downloads/` (untrusted data, not executed).

Legend for source strength: **[AO]** = admissions office's own page, podcast or blog. **[ESSAY]** = admitted
student's essay published by the college. **[RES]** = peer-reviewed paper or preprint. **[COACH]** = paid
essay coach. **[PRESS]** = journalism.

---

## 0. The short version (for the skill author)

1. Readers are not grading prose. They want to finish the essay feeling they met a specific 17-year-old.
   Yale: "we want to get to the end of an essay and think, you know, I kind of get a sense of what it would be
   like to have a real conversation with this person" [AO, Yale ep. 4].
2. The most common failure is "so what?", not bad grammar. Yale: "The most common shortcoming is that we
   finish the essay and we say, OK, so what?" [AO, Yale ep. 34]. Polished but empty is the default failure, and
   AI produces it on demand ("slick but soulless", same episode).
3. Specific, checkable, slightly odd detail is what makes an essay feel human. Generic summary is what makes it
   feel written by anyone, or by a model.
4. Insight has to be earned by the scene before it. Conn College: "Anyone can tell us 'I set high standards
   for myself' ... But it's much more compelling to show us" [AO].
5. LLM-written essays are measurably more uniform. They echo the prompt's keywords ("challenge", "growth",
   "community", "journey", "resilience"), use longer words with less variety, and drop in identity facts
   mechanically [RES, Lee et al. 2025; Alvero et al. 2024]. Details in section 4.
6. Admitted essays are not flawless. Several published ones end on a tidy moral or contain a stock phrase. They
   work because the body is concrete. So the skill should not over-polish, and it must never invent details.
7. For college application essays, Claude may coach but must not write substantive content. That is a
   Common App fraud rule; see `policies.md`.

The "I feel I met this person" test, in the officers' own words:
- Yale ep. 4 ends: "the most effective essays are the ones that really make us feel like we know the student and
  it also makes us excited about them joining the Yale community."
- Yale officer Keith's tip for reviewers you ask for feedback: "rather than saying, is this a great essay? ...
  First, ask, does this sound like me?" (ep. 4).

  it doesnt, rewrite it until it does (because if your friends can't see you in your words, how can we?)." (2019 post above).

## 2. Craft techniques, with excerpts from admitted essays

### 2.1 Specific, sensory, slightly odd detail

Why it matters: Yale asks for "vivid language, we want to get a sense of place, and of how you're feeling"
(ep. 4). JHU praised one essay because it "includes vivid and specific details that make the story feel
authentic" (committee comment on Ruoni, https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/clean-closet/).

Excerpts (each is the kind of detail a model will not produce unless the student supplies it):
- Unflattering, specific animal detail: "He'd poop in his water bucket, making it a pain to clean up all the
  stains with soap and a power washer." (Rowan Johnson, Hamilton Class of 2030,
  https://www.hamilton.edu/admission/apply/college-essays-that-worked)
- A small-town fact that does the characterizing: "The residents have still not entirely mastered the four-way
  stop sign that was installed five years ago." (Gillian, JHU,
  https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/the-two-sides-of-america/)
- Failures, told precisely: "granulated sugar icing that tasted like sand and improperly leavened sourdough that
  required a saw to slice." (David, JHU, https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/the-secret-ingredient-is-connection/)
- A real third person with a real quirk: "Mr. Zogby, would pop by every now and then to show us his slider — he
  swore there would have been more motion to the pitch if he hadn't hurt his shoulder shoveling." (Martin Amuso,
  Hamilton, same Hamilton URL)
- Numbers as texture rather than as achievement: "'How many times did I wake up at 4:15 a.m. this summer?' ...
  The answer: twenty-two times." (an admitted Class of 2025 essay,
  https://www.conncoll.edu/admission/apply/essays-that-worked/)

The canonical pre-AI illustration, from MIT's Chris Peterson quoting Vince Gotera
(https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/how-to-write-a-college-essay/, 2012, updated later):
- Generic: "I am honored to apply ... because as long as I can remember I have had a love affair with books."
- Specific: "When I was eleven, my great-aunt Gretchen passed away and left me something that changed my life:
  a library of about five thousand books."
- Peterson: the two paragraphs carry the same information, "But they are extraordinarily different essays, most
  strikingly because the former is generic where the latter is specific. It was a real thing, which happened to a
  real person, told simply. There is nothing better than that."

Skill rule: when a draft says "I have always loved X", ask the student for the first concrete memory, object,
number or name behind it. Never supply one yourself.

### 2.2 Openings: a scene, a strange fact, or a flat statement

- Scene with the body in it: "The chill of the linoleum floor cooled my knees through the thin fabric of my
  scrubs." (Emily Huryk, Hamilton)
- Scene plus the emotional problem in one beat: "I lay on the bamboo mat in a pool of my own sweat, swaying with
  the ship. ... Yet as I looked outside the porthole, the greatest discomfort was my own shame." (Ruoyu, JHU,
  https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/in-the-belly-of-the-ship/)
- Noise and dialogue: "An authoritative voice bellows my name in the crowded school cafeteria: 'MATZ!'" (David, JHU)
- Four flat words. Conn College's comment: "That first sentence is a knockout." The sentence: "The plants were all
  dead." (Jackson Spallone, https://www.conncoll.edu/admission/apply/essays-that-worked/jackson-spallone-28/)
- Self-deprecating hook: "I'm no Hermione Granger — anyone with half-decent vision from Wizards and Wands camp can
  see that from a mile away." (Shiwani Paudel, Hamilton)
- A riddle the essay then answers: "What do you get when you combine a hypodermic needle, international code,
  clay, an early mainframe computer, and the national budget? In a sense, you get me." Conn's comment: "The first
  remark really drew us in" (anonymous, https://www.conncoll.edu/admission/apply/essays-that-worked/anonymous/)

Officer advice: Hamilton's tips say "Use a strong opener – catch our attention right from the start. Poignant
moments in time, with a little bit of reflection, often make great essays."
(https://www.hamilton.edu/admission/apply/college-essays-that-worked/tips-for-a-good-college-essay)

Openings that do not work (Yale ep. 5, 2020-07-10,
https://admissions.yale.edu/posts/2020-07-10-episode-5-essays-what-doesnt-work):
- The third-person reveal: "it usually starts in the third person and there's usually a twist about two or three
  paragraphs in where you turn to, and you say, 'that little girl was me.'"
- The essay as a letter to someone ("I don't necessarily think really works well").

Compare the LLM openings recorded by Lee et al. (section 4): "Growing up in Rabat, Morocco, I have always been
acutely aware of the intricate tapestry of my heritage." That sentence makes a thesis claim about identity
before the reader has seen anything.

### 2.3 Earned insight, not announced insight (the "so what")

- Yale's three-episode series keeps returning to "so what?": "It can sometimes be a lovely piece of writing. It
  can be very well-polished. But I– and then I say, OK, that was nice. But so what?" (ep. 5)
- Conn College on Edie's hiking essay: "Anyone can tell us 'I set high standards for myself,' or 'I am dedicated
  to personal growth,' or 'I set lofty goals and then work to achieve them.' But it's much more compelling to show
  us your values, motivation and character through the story you share with us."
- JHU on Ruoni: "it shows change through action: donating old belongings, making space for new interests, and
  becoming more open to people and experiences."
- An earned line from Ruoni's essay. It comes after a paragraph of concrete objects (Littlest Pet Shop figures,
  dried flowers, pom poms): "The things I've let go of have grown with me: painting became nail art, gymnastics
  became cheer, creating storylines became exploring people."
- Rowan's horse essay ends on observed behavior and adds only three words of gloss: "Charlie noticed me, ignored
  the food, and turned without hesitation to meet me at the entrance. That was trust."
- MIT's Peterson: "You should learn something new about yourself in every essay you write (otherwise, you're
  probably not being introspective enough)." (2019 post)
- College Essay Guy (Ethan Sawyer) [COACH, **SNIPPET**; collegeessayguy.com returned a Cloudflare challenge]:
  treats insight as the answer to "so what?" and says a great insight "is likely to surprise the reader a bit."
  His checklist names four markers: Values, Vulnerability, Insight ("genuine 'so what' moments") and Craft.
  Search summaries of https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/personal-statement-examples and
  https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/college-essay-format. Exact wording UNVERIFIED.

Skill rule: one or two sentences of reflection after the scene that earns them. Delete reflection sentences
that could appear in anyone's essay ("This experience taught me the value of hard work").

### 2.4 Vulnerability without trauma-dumping

Officer guidance:
- Yale ep. 4 (Mark) singles out essays where a student "can really sit in the experience of being wrong about
  something or hurting someone's feelings, having real regret about something. You don't need to paint a truly
  miserable picture of yourself in that moment, you don't need to wallow in self-pity".
- Yale ep. 5: a hardship essay "shouldn't just be an exercise in detailing the challenges or the failures or the
  frustrations. It should also be a chance to reflect and provide some insight on it." Also flags "a story of
  failure without redemption", or redemption arriving too late.
- Hamilton tips: "In sharing something about yourself, you don't need to share everything about yourself. It is
  ok to be personal, and writing about growth and mistakes is good (perhaps even welcomed); but resist the urge to
  be too casual or to over-share."
- Yale on the death-of-a-grandparent essay: "follow Alfred Hitchcock" (Psycho). Put the death "early in the
  essay, to leave you space for reflection and insights", "compress all of that into maybe the first third"
  (ep. 5).
- College Essay Guy [COACH, **SNIPPET**]: warns against manufacturing or inflating hardship and says many
  strong essays use a montage structure with no challenge at all
  (https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/how-to-write-about-challenges-college-essay, search summary only).

Admitted-essay technique: give the hard fact one plain sentence, then move on to agency.
- War, in one understated line, in an essay mostly about math club, climbing and cookies: "Today, Feb. 24, 2022,
  my life was upended when my city was bombed. I never managed to retrieve my backpack." (Tymofii Kolisnyk,
  Hamilton Class of 2030)
- Declining the illness narrative out loud: "But that's not what this story is about — not Martin Amuso the
  Unfortunate Fella but Martin Amuso the Comeback Kid, the Wiffle Baller." (Hamilton)
- Naming the cliché before the reader can: "I'm a teen with divorced parents. That story's been heard hundreds of
  times. ... None of this is groundbreakingly tragic or new." (Nina De Jesus Santiago, Hamilton)
- An unflattering admission: "I decided to do nothing but toss a pack of tissues at a friend crying next to me
  during class instead of being a shoulder to cry on." (Benjamin Frenkel, Hamilton)
- A confession that complicates the scene: "Contrary to this anecdote, I am not a Christian. Never have been, do
  not plan to be." (Emily Huryk, Hamilton, right after she prays with a patient)
- Shame stated plainly, without melodrama: "All I felt I could do was lie flat in my cot and wait for morning."
  (Ruoyu, JHU)

### 2.5 Humor

- Yale ep. 4: "if you are someone who's funny and light, then the voice that comes through in your essay should
  be funny and light. If you're more serious and solemn, then you might be writing a more serious and solemn
  essay. And that's OK."
- Tufts officer on a supplement: "She starts off strong and gets my attention right away with some well-placed
  humor." The line: "I may be a sturdy 5'9," but I cannot sprint or catch a ball to save my life."
  (https://admissions.tufts.edu/blogs/inside-admissions/post/supplements-revisited-ainsley-ball-21; undated, the
  student is Class of 2021, so about 2017-18)
- Conn College on Nancy's essay: she "gets to the point in an amusing but heartfelt way"
  (https://www.conncoll.edu/admission/apply/essays-that-worked/nancy-owusu-28/). Her mock arithmetic: "and I mean,
  I'm no mathematical genius, that would approximately equal 17,680 times a year my name is called."
- A joke made out of a hard fact: "Everyone was aching to take to the field. I know I was, but that could have
  been just another stomach ache." (Martin Amuso, Hamilton; the essay is about Crohn's disease)
- A comic conceit that carries the whole essay: "My General of Wellness is a seasoned military man insisting
  that it's leg day, while my Chairman of Chores, donning a suit, argues that the dog should be walked before
  exercising." (Benjamin Frenkel, Hamilton)
- Limit: "Bathroom humor, anything related to anything happening in the bathroom, it shouldn't be in your essay."
  (Yale ep. 5). Note that Rowan's horse-poop detail above was published by Hamilton anyway, so this is about taste
  and degree, not an absolute rule.

### 2.6 Voice: diction, rhythm, register

- Plain words win. Yale ep. 5 (Mark): "when you use a word like 'alacrity,' when 'speed' would work just as
  well, it's hard for me to imagine that you hang out with your friends and use the word 'alacrity' in
  conversation with them." Hannah describes essays written "with a thesaurus by their side".
- Yale ep. 4: "This is not the place to show off how big your vocabulary is or to try to construct the most
  complex sentences with lots of semicolons and dependent clauses ... Your voice, hopefully as a 17-year-old, is
  sophisticated and thoughtful but also natural, and approachable".
- Hamilton tips: "Trim the fat. (And resist the urge to use the thesaurus!)" and "don't over-edit and make it
  sound like a research paper."
- Rhythm (Tufts officer): "About halfway through the response, I found myself skimming a little bit. I think this
  is because Ainsley's sentence structure is very similar throughout: long, winding sentences ... Some shorter,
  punchier statements could have a really large impact". Recommends reading aloud.
- Over-editing erases voice. Yale ep. 4: "If your essay is worked over and edited, and edited, and edited, by a
  bunch of people, you might start to lose that voice". MIT's Ben Jones (2007,
  https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/advice_on_the_essay/): "we are looking for the applicant's true voice ...
  Not some perfect piece of prose worthy of a magazine, or something that has been edited and edited and edited
  by a variety of different people."
- Peterson's test for voice (added to the 2012 post): "imagine that someone at a family reunion — a cousin's uncle
  you saw once a decade — nonchalantly asked you the prompt while in line for the casserole. how would you respond
  to them — introspectively, but in a way they could understand what you were thinking through — without fear of
  judgment or needing to impress them?"
- Pre-AI "lifeless, imitative style" (Peterson, quoting Orwell): essays built from "billowing clouds of 'my global
  perspective' and 'future potential as a leader' and 'desire to leverage my education'". This is the human
  cliché register that LLMs reproduce.
- Peterson's restatement of Orwell's first rule: "Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which
  you are used to seeing in print."

### 2.7 Structure

- **Narrative vs montage** [COACH, **SNIPPET**] (College Essay Guy; site blocked, so wording comes from search
  summaries): narrative "follows a clear chronology with a beginning, middle, and end"; montage "is arranged
  thematically, so each paragraph might focus on a new idea, feeling, or thought." The narrative
  (challenges-based) version is split roughly into thirds: challenge and effects, what you did, what you learned.
  Montage suits students for whom nothing "major" has happened. The montage outline is "4-7 ways your thread
  connects to different values through different experiences." Essence objects: pick an object or several
  "and use it (or them) to reveal different parts of who you are." Sources:
  https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/college-essay-format,
  https://www.collegeessayguy.com/cyoa/montage/brainstorming/choose-a-path/essence-objects,
  https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/how-to-write-about-challenges-college-essay.
- Montage essays among the admitted examples (verified):
  - Files from a future birthday archive, each labeled `*backpack.jpg*`, `*kombinatoryka.pdf*`,
    `*unbothered_ducks.jpg*`, `*cooookies.jpg*` (Tymofii Kolisnyk, Hamilton).
  - One tree species per trait: sequoia, cottonwood, American chestnut, ash (Etina, JHU,
    https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/developing-roots/). JHU: "The tree metaphor effectively communicates a
    student who thinks across disciplines".
  - Closet objects (Ruoni, JHU) and five women's jobs (anonymous, Conn).
- Narrative essays: the ship internship, failed then repeated (Ruoyu, JHU); a Girl Scout proposal that loses the
  national vote: "We lost." (Taylor Barnes, Hamilton); the horse (Rowan, Hamilton).
- An extended metaphor in place of a common topic. Conn on Jackson: "rather than a simple recollection of
  recovering from an injury—a topic we read about a lot—Jackson created the extended metaphor of gardening".
- Get to the point sooner. A Tufts officer would have moved the "academic punch" earlier and "condensed the part
  about attending a single-sex school".
- The cover-letter essay fails. Yale ep. 5: an essay that "takes your activities list ... and writes the essay
  that's sort of like a cover letter accompanying a resume ... misses the opportunity to tell us something new."
- Writing about someone else is risky. Yale ep. 4: "you might get to the end of that essay and say, OK, well, now
  I've heard a lot about your friend Mark, but I don't know much about you." Yale ep. 5 calls this "reading
  someone's biography". Conn praised Phoebe's essay about her sister because "Although she was not the main
  character in these encounters ... [it] is about her."

### 2.8 Endings that open rather than summarize

What the published examples do:
- Return to the opening image, changed: "A fourth figure enters the conference room: me. ... I declare that I want
  to sleep in until 2 p.m. and hang out with my dog tomorrow." (Frenkel, Hamilton; the essay opens in the same
  "conference room")
- A light callback instead of a moral: "The next time I'm tasked to 'clean closet,' it won't take eighteen years."
  (Ruoni, JHU)
- End on an action or image and stop: "That was trust." (Rowan, Hamilton)
- End by quoting someone else, which leaves the question open: Cate Leydig's golf essay ends on Tommy Fleetwood's
  words and "I suppose that when you really love something, it's hard to let it go." (Hamilton)
- A future scene in place of a lesson: "Today, on my 68th birthday, I have a lecture to give." (Kolisnyk, Hamilton)

The admitted author of the ship essay on writing the ending: "I wrote multiple versions of that final paragraph
over months because I found it difficult to determine what meaning (if any) should be used to define my
experience. I was writing before I'd had the distance to know what my story meant."
(https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/in-the-belly-of-the-ship/). That is a useful warning against forcing a
moral.

Honest caveat: admitted essays also end on summary lines ("The secret ingredient in each baked good I make is
connection." David, JHU; "letting go is part of growing", Ruoni, JHU). Those endings work because the body has
already earned them. The skill should flag summary endings, not ban them.

### 2.9 The 650-word Common App constraint

- 650 words is the maximum for the main personal statement. Yale: "650 words will be the longest one that you
  write" (ep. 4). CalMatters: "the Common App essay response can't exceed 650 words while UC's four essays are
  capped at 350 words each" (2023-10-16, republished at
  https://www.smdp.com/college-application-season-is-here-so-is-the-struggle-to-find-out-if-ai-wrote-students-essays/).
- Common App's in-app instruction is widely quoted as "Remember: 650 words is your limit, not your goal. Use the
  full range if you need it, but don't feel obligated to do so." The 250-word minimum ("The application won't
  accept a response shorter than 250 words") is also widely quoted. **UNVERIFIED on a live commonapp.org page**:
  the help-center article is JavaScript-rendered and could not be read. Secondary copies include the University
  of Michigan transfer essay page (https://admissions.umich.edu/apply/transfer-applicants/essay-questions; 403 on
  fetch) and search summaries.
- 2026-27 prompts (verified on https://www.commonapp.org/apply/essay-prompts, via its Gatsby page-data): seven
  prompts, including "Share an essay on any topic of your choice."
- The optional "Additional information" box shrank from 650 to 300 words starting 2025-26, and "Challenges and
  circumstances" is 250 words max (https://www.commonapp.org/blog/announcing-2025-2026-common-app-essay-prompts/).
  So the main essay is now the only 650-word space.
- What 650 words means for craft: room for one scene, one turn and one reflection, or a montage of 4 to 7 short
  sections. Peterson: "Specificity, clarity, and brevity are your keys." MIT blogger Phoebe C. [AO student
  blogger, **SNIPPET**] argues that a detail without a clear purpose should be cut
  (https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/what-my-college-essays-were-not/).

### 2.10 Short answers and supplements

**Stanford** (verified 2026-10-20, https://admission.stanford.edu/apply/first-year/apply.html, updated 2026-07-21):
- Short questions: "There is a 3-word minimum and a 50-word maximum for each question", e.g. "List five things
  that are important to you." and "How did you spend your last two summers?"
- Three essays of 100-250 words. The roommate essay: "Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus.
  Write a note to your future roommate that reveals something about you or that will help your roommate—and
  us—get to know you better."
- Craft implication (inference, not a Stanford quote): the roommate note is read for what it would be like to
  live with you. Concrete habits, rituals, and warnings (sleep schedule, music, the food you cook, what you will
  drag them to) fit the "friend, future roommate, and classmate" framing better than a résumé. No official
  Stanford sample essays were found.

**MIT** (verified 2026-10-20, https://mitadmissions.org/apply/firstyear/essays-activities-academics/):
- 2026-27: four short essays "of approximately 100–200 words each" and short responses with "only space for 40 to
  50 words each" (e.g. "Who is someone you admire ... Tell us why.").
- Earlier MIT prompt (2013-2019): "Tell us about something you do simply for the pleasure of it." Peterson's
  advice there: "be strategically nonstrategic" and "You are more interesting than anyone you can pretend to be".
- Robo-CJ (MIT student blogger, **SNIPPET**): a GPT-2 model trained on his own blog drafted MIT answers; he
  reported that only about 10% of the output was usable even after about 50 attempts per prompt
  (https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/robo-cj-writes-a-college-app/, search summary only).

**Yale** (ep. 6, 2020-07-31, https://admissions.yale.edu/posts/2020-07-31-episode-6-essays-the-little-stuff):
- Short takes of 35 words: "I think these should be a little bit fun to answer ... they're not trick questions."
- They can change a decision. Officer Reed describes a research-focused applicant whose warmth showed up only in
  the short takes: "They were just so lively and so different from every other piece of writing that he'd produced."
- "Why Yale" belongs in the past tense: "really good responses to this point to specific experiences in a
  student's past ... Weaker responses ... launch right into the future tense". Also: "We are not looking for facts
  about Yale here. We already know those."
- "the shorter essays and shorter pieces of writing are often much trickier to produce than longer pieces,
  because each word matters so much more." (Reed)

**Tufts** (supplements-revisited post above): "she made the smart decision to focus on just one of them ...
rather than listing a lot things like a major, our location, and the awesome food". "Each bit of writing you
give us should tell us something about you".

## 3. What admitted essays are not

Several published admitted essays contain phrases a strict "AI-ism" filter would flag:
- "My contributions were made possible by that crucible at sea, forged in sweat, silence, and routine" (Ruoyu, JHU)
- "cooking has become the invisible string that pulls me along through my ever-so-busy life" (Elle Yarborough,
  Conn, https://www.conncoll.edu/admission/apply/essays-that-worked/elle-yarborough-28/)
- "I'll intertwine my passions with curiosity and forge a path unique to me" (Nina, Hamilton)
- "Baking is incredibly meaningful to me as a way to strengthen my relationships with myself, others, and cultures
  around the world." (David, JHU)

These essays worked anyway because almost every paragraph around those lines is concrete and could only belong to
that student. Implications for the skill:
- The goal is not zero clichés. It is a concrete-to-abstract ratio heavily weighted to the concrete, plus a
  voice that sounds like a teenager.
- Do not "fix" an admitted-style draft by polishing it smoother. Over-editing is itself a reader red flag (Yale,
  MIT, Hamilton above).
- The Class of 2030 essays were written in the 2025-26 cycle, when AI grammar tools were common. Princeton's
  Class of 2029 survey found 47.7% had used at least one AI tool on college essays, mostly Grammarly Free
  ([PRESS] Daily Princetonian, 2025-10,
  https://www.dailyprincetonian.com/article/2025/10/princeton-data-2029-frosh-survey-blog-ai-use-fields).
  Light tool use and admission are not mutually exclusive.

## 4. What AI-written essays get wrong (research plus reader comments)

### 4.1 Research

**Lee, Alvero, Joachims & Kizilcec, "Poor Alignment and Steerability of Large Language Models: Evidence from
College Admission Essays"** [RES] arXiv:2503.20062 (v1 2025-03-25, v2 2026-01-22); presented at COLM 2025.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20062, HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20062v2
- Data: about 30,000 Common App essays to a selective university, written before ChatGPT (2019-20 to 2022-23),
  compared with essays from eight LLMs (OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic, Mistral), with and without the applicant's
  demographics in the prompt.
- "both types of LLM-generated essays are linguistically distinct from human-authored essays, regardless of the
  specific model and analytical approach."
- Prompt-keyword echo: "LLMs favor keywords from the essay question included in the prompt ... whereas
  human-authored essays incorporate more conversational, temporally dynamic, and interpersonal language." LLM
  essays "tended to use words like 'challenge', 'growth', 'community', 'journey', 'understanding', 'experience' and
  'resilience', which were contained in the essay questions."
- Identity facts get dropped in mechanically: "LLMs use demographic indicators verbatim ... which to readers might
  seem unnatural and mechanically inserted." Example: "Growing up in Lexington, South Carolina, with my Asian
  heritage, I often felt like a bridge between two cultures. My parents, both college graduates, emphasized the
  importance of education and hard work".
- Homogenization: "the two sets of LLM-generated essays were noticeably more alike compared to the variability
  observed among human-authored essays." Identity prompting "sometimes exacerbated discrepancies".
- Detectability: per Cornell's press release, a trained classifier separated human from AI essays "with
  near-perfect accuracy" (https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/09/ai-can-write-your-college-essay-it-wont-sound-you).
- Real LLM text from the paper's appendix (useful as anti-examples in the skill):
  - "Embracing the Unknown: My Journey with Engineering and Identity. In a world that often categorizes
    individuals by their achievements, I have come to appreciate the power of identity and the stories that shape us."
  - "My background is a tapestry woven from the threads of two distinct cultures"
  - "Rabat, a city of contrasts, has been my playground and classroom."
  - "As I prepare to embark on the next chapter of my academic journey"
  - "I would dismantle household gadgets—much to my parents' chagrin" (the same stock anecdote appears in more
    than one generated essay)

**Alvero, Lee, Regla-Vargas, Kizilcec, Joachims & antonio, "Large language models, social demography, and
hegemony: comparing authorship in human and synthetic text"** [RES] Journal of Big Data 11 (2024), published
2024-09-27, DOI 10.1186/s40537-024-00986-7 (abstract read via OpenAlex,
https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1186/s40537-024-00986-7; the Springer page served a bot challenge).
- Over 150,000 human admissions essays (UC system plus an elite private engineering program) against more than
  25,000 GPT-3.5/GPT-4 essays on the same prompts.
- "human-authored essays exhibit more variability across various individual writing style features (e.g., verb
  usage) than AI-generated essays" and "the AI-generated essays are most similar to essays authored by students
  who are males with higher levels of social privilege."
- Cornell's summary (https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2024/10/ai-generated-college-admissions-essays-sound-male-privileged):
  "AI was found on average to use longer words (six or more letters) than human writers." AI writing "more
  closely resembled essays from private-school applicants than those from public-school students". AI wrote
  about affiliations "at similar rates – despite the AI not actually having any affiliations." Kizilcec: "if you
  use an AI to help you write an essay, it's probably going to sound less like you and more like something quite
  generic".

**Alvero et al., "Essay content and style are strongly related to household income and SAT scores: Evidence from
60,000 undergraduate applications"** [RES] Science Advances 7, eabi9031, 2021-10-13 (abstract read via Europe PMC;
PMC page https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8514086/ served a bot check).
- 240,000 UC essays from 60,000 applicants: "essay content and style had stronger correlations to self-reported
  household income than did SAT scores".
- Why it matters here: polished, "privileged" essay style was already a class signal before AI. An LLM that
  writes in that register pushes every student toward it.

**Lee, Borchers, Alvero, Joachims & Kizilcec, "The Digital Divide in Generative AI: Evidence from Large Language
Model Use in College Admissions Essays"** [RES] arXiv:2602.17791, submitted 2026-02-19, preprint, not yet peer
reviewed. https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17791
- 81,663 applications to one selective university, 2020-2024: "post-2023 convergence in surface-level linguistic
  features". Estimated LLM use "rose sharply in 2024 across all groups, with disproportionately larger increases
  among lower SES applicants". Higher estimated use "was more strongly associated with declines in predicted
  admission probability for lower SES applicants".

**Jakesch, Hancock & Naaman, "Human heuristics for AI-generated language are flawed"** [RES] PNAS 120(11)
e2208839120, 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07271
- 4,600 participants "were unable to detect self-presentations generated by state-of-the-art AI language models".
  Judgments relied on "intuitive but flawed heuristics such as associating first-person pronouns, use of
  contractions, or family topics with human-written language."
- Implication for the skill: adding "I", contractions or family mentions does not make text more human in any
  real sense. What separates the two in the admissions data is specificity, variety and prompt independence.

### 4.2 What admissions offices say AI essays lack

- Yale ep. 34 (2023-08-29, https://admissions.yale.edu/posts/episode-34): "I think the best description that I've

  super-futuristic, but it's entirely dependent on the past ... If you want to create something new and original
  ... AI is the last place that you want to go." And: "I kind think of AI as the perfect way to write a completely
  forgettable college essay".
- Yale officer Hannah tried it herself: "I've spent a lot of time trying to write a good college essay with it and
  haven't been able to."
- Princeton spokesperson Jennifer Morrill: "An essay generated by an AI platform is unlikely to be as rich and

### 4.3 Checklist of AI tells for this genre (derived from 4.1 and 4.2; each line cites its basis)

| Tell | Basis |
|---|---|
| Echoes the prompt's abstract nouns: journey, growth, resilience, community, challenge, passion, experience | Lee et al. 2025 lexical analysis |
| Identity stated as a thesis in sentence 1 ("Growing up in X, with my Y heritage") | Lee et al. 2025 examples |
| Stock metaphors: tapestry, lens, bridge between two cultures, "city of contrasts", "embark on the next chapter" | Lee et al. appendix text; Orwell's rule via Peterson |
| Title or opener of the form "Embracing X: My Journey with Y" | Lee et al. appendix |
| Longer words, less variety in sentence and verb patterns | Alvero et al. 2024 |
| Generic, interchangeable anecdote (taking gadgets apart "much to my parents' chagrin") | Lee et al. appendix, repeated across outputs |
| Upbeat resolution with no unflattering moment | Lee et al. cite RLHF-driven "overly upbeat narratives"; Yale wants regret and humility |
| Reflection that could belong to anyone ("has shaped who I am", "taught me the importance of") | Conn's "Anyone can tell us..."; Yale "so what?" |
| Uniform rhythm (every sentence long and balanced) | Tufts officer on skimming |
| Thesaurus words a teen would not say aloud | Yale "alacrity" |
| "Why us" written in the future tense with brochure facts | Yale ep. 6 |
| Voice that doesn't match the rest of the file (recommendations, interview, short answers) | Yale ep. 34 |

## 5. Rules this suggests for the skill's essay mode

1. **Coach, don't author** (application essays). Ask questions, flag lines and explain why, and offer
   grammar/spelling fixes. Do not draft or rewrite sentences that carry content. See `policies.md`.
2. **Ask, never invent.** Turn every abstract claim into a question for the student: what exactly happened, when,
   who said what, what did it smell or sound like, what number, what object.
3. **Flag the AI-tell list (4.3)** and explain each flag in one line, with the evidence.
4. **Opening check:** is sentence one a scene, an odd fact, or a short flat statement? If it is a thesis about
   identity or passion, flag it.
5. **Reflection check:** for each reflection sentence, which concrete moment earns it? If none, flag it. Prefer
   one surprising insight to three generic ones.
6. **Vulnerability check:** is there a moment where the writer is wrong, small, embarrassed, or not fully
   resolved? Is hardship told in proportion (plain, short, then agency)?
7. **Voice check:** read-aloud test, the casserole test (Peterson), the "does it sound like you" friend test, the
   alacrity test. Keep the student's own words even when they are plainer.
8. **Rhythm check:** vary sentence length; one short sentence after a long one is often enough.
9. **Ending check:** a callback, an image, an action, or a forward scene. Flag "In conclusion", "This experience
   taught me", and a restated thesis. Don't demand a moral.
10. **Short answers:** specific nouns, lists that are actually specific, humor allowed, past-tense evidence for
    "why us", no brochure facts.
11. **Don't over-polish.** Imperfect but specific beats smooth and generic (Yale, MIT, Hamilton, Conn).
