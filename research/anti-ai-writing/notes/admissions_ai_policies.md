# AI in application essays: Common App rules, college policies, and detection in practice

Researched 2026-10-20. "Verified" means I fetched the page and read the quoted words on it that day. Policies
change every cycle, so re-check before relying on any of this. Local copies of the pages are in `downloads/`.

## 1. Common App fraud policy (verified)

**URL:** https://www.commonapp.org/fraud-policy/ (text read from the page's own data file,
https://www.commonapp.org/page-data/fraud-policy/page-data.json)
**Date on the page:** "Date of Last Update: August 2023". Some blogs say "2024"; the page itself says August 2023.

Exact text of the AI clause, under "Fraud":

> Common App defines Fraud as an intent to deceive, mislead, or otherwise perpetuate misinformation upon a Common
> App member regarding the candidacy of a student-applicant for admission. ... Fraud may occur in the following
> circumstances, without limitation:
> ...
> iii. Submitting plagiarized essays or other written or oral material, or intentionally misrepresenting as one's
> own original work: (1) another person's thoughts, language, ideas, expressions, or experiences or (2) the
> substantive content or output of an artificial intelligence platform, technology, or algorithm;

Consequences, same page ("Actions Subsequent to a Fraud Determination"):

> Common App in its sole discretion may take action as a result of a Fraud determination which may include
> permanent suspension, temporary suspension, or termination of their Common App user account, and disclosure of
> the Fraud to the Registered User's "My Colleges List."

The process on the page: written notice to the applicant, an investigation, a determination, and an appeal
window of 10 business days.

**The affirmation every applicant signs** (quoted verbatim on JHU's FAQ, verified,
https://apply.jhu.edu/frequently-asked-questions/):

> "I certify that all information submitted in the admission process — including this application and any other
> supporting materials — is my own work, factually true, and honestly presented ... I understand that I may be
> subject to a range of possible disciplinary actions, including admission revocation, expulsion, or revocation of
> course credit, grades, and degree should the information I have certified be false."

**"Substantive" is not defined, on purpose.** Common App CEO Jenny Rickard told CalMatters "there's no
definition, and that's intentional", adding: "we will evaluate the totality of the circumstances to determine if
a student truly intended to misrepresent content generated by AI technology as their own work." Common App
"doesn't determine whether students are being honest — that's up to the member colleges to figure out."
([PRESS] CalMatters, Jones & Zinshteyn, 2023-10-16, republished at
https://www.smdp.com/college-application-season-is-here-so-is-the-struggle-to-find-out-if-ai-wrote-students-essays/)

Reading of the clause: the key words are "intentionally misrepresenting as one's own original work" plus
"substantive content or output". Spell-checking does not produce substantive content. Pasting or lightly editing
AI-drafted paragraphs does.

### What the policies have in common

From most to least restrictive:

1. **Brown:** grammar and spelling only.
2. **Yale, JHU, Notre Dame, Caltech:** grammar/spelling plus brainstorming *before* writing (topic ideas,
   questions). Caltech explicitly bans AI outlines and AI translation.
3. **Columbia, Princeton:** principle-based ("your own original work", "authentic voice"). Princeton
   discourages use without banning it.
4. **UC, Georgia Tech:** content advice and editing allowed, but the final text must be the student's.

Every school above prohibits submitting AI-drafted prose as your own. That is the Common App floor.

## 3. Detecting AI essays in practice

### What admissions officers say

- **Yale doesn't rely on detection; it looks for mismatch.** "we don't spend a huge amount of energy trying to
  reverse-engineer how an essay was crafted". The signal they describe is inconsistency: "when we read something
  that's in a voice that doesn't match up the rest of the application– and remember, these essays are never read
  in a vacuum ... We meet this person through their recommendations, through the interview, through their
  activities, we also say, wait a minute. This doesn't seem right." Also: "there are several ways that we could
  find out about something like this, and none of these scenarios end well for the applicant." And: "this isn't
  because we're super geniuses who can smell the stuff from a mile away". (Yale ep. 34, 2023-08-29,
  https://admissions.yale.edu/posts/episode-34)
- **Duke changed how it scores rather than trying to detect.** Asked how it determines whether an essay is
  AI-generated, Guttentag said "there aren't simple answers to these questions" (Duke Chronicle 2024-02-20).
- **Georgia Tech:** "this idea of reverse-AI, as one of our [computer science] professors said, is a pipe dream…
  you're not going to know with certainty if a student used AI." (Rick Clark, CBC 2023-09-08)
- **USC** (2023) said it was not using AI-detection software. **UC** says it runs "regular screenings" and "may
  request authentication" (CalMatters 2023).
- **Hamilton** (about parents, before AI, but the same mechanism): "the voice of a teenager is very different from
  a parent's, and we've gotten pretty good at spotting the differences."
  (https://www.hamilton.edu/admission/apply/college-essays-that-worked/tips-for-a-good-college-essay)
- **MIT** (2007, about heavy editing): "We can always tell when an applicant's essay has been edited to be
  something other than his or her true voice." (Ben Jones, https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/advice_on_the_essay/)
- **Cross-checks inside the file:** Princeton requires a graded paper, and consultancies describe it as an
  authenticity check. That framing is a consultancy's opinion (**UNVERIFIED** as Princeton's intent).

### What the research says

- **Machines can tell; people can't, at least not reliably.** A classifier separated pre-ChatGPT human
  admissions essays from LLM essays "with near-perfect accuracy" (Lee et al., per
  https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/09/ai-can-write-your-college-essay-it-wont-sound-you; arXiv:2503.20062).
  That result is for raw, unedited model output against pre-2022 human essays, so do not treat it as a real-world
  detection rate. In contrast, people judging AI-written self-presentations performed at about chance (N = 4,600;
  Jakesch, Hancock & Naaman, PNAS 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07271).
- **Population-level signal:** after 2023, applicant essays at one selective university showed "convergence in
  surface-level linguistic features". Higher estimated LLM use was linked to lower predicted admission
  probability, more so for lower-SES applicants (arXiv:2602.17791, 2026 preprint).
- **Detectors are probabilistic.** MIT writing faculty (2023): "We do not, however, know of any precedent for
  disciplinary measures ... being instituted based on probabilistic evidence from such automated methods."
- **How common use is:** foundry10 estimated about 30% of students use AI tools to write essays (cited by Cornell,
  2025-09). Princeton's Class of 2029 survey: 47.7% used at least one AI tool on college essays, mostly Grammarly
  Free; 15.8% of tool users used ChatGPT (Daily Princetonian, 2025-10).

**Bottom line for a student:** the realistic risk is not a detector score. It is a generic essay that "doesn't
match up" with the recommendations, the interview, the short answers and the graded paper, read by someone who
sees thousands of essays a year. AI-drafted text also breaks the affirmation the student signs.

## 4. Guardrails this implies for the skill

The user is a high-school student who will use the skill for college essays as well as research papers.

1. **Application essays (Common App, supplements, scholarship essays): coaching only.** Allowed: brainstorming
   questions, structural feedback in prose ("paragraph 3 summarizes; what happened that day?"), flagging AI-isms
   with reasons, and spelling/grammar/punctuation fixes. Not allowed: drafting, rewriting sentences that carry
   content, inventing details, generating outlines (Caltech bans AI outlines), or translating. This fits every
   policy in section 2 except Brown's, which permits only spelling and grammar.
2. **Ask which schools.** If Brown is on the list, restrict to spelling and grammar for those materials. If Caltech
   is, no outlines and no translation.
3. **Never fabricate.** No invented anecdotes, sensory details, quotes, numbers or names. If the student wants
   more detail, ask questions. (This also follows the Common App's "factually true" affirmation.)
4. **Show the rule when declining.** Quote the Common App clause and the school's line so the student sees the
   reason.
5. **Research papers and school reports** follow the teacher's or course's AI policy, not these rules. The
   anti-AI style rules still apply, with no ghostwriting of graded work unless the course allows it.
