Hi, Shoe Repair

Last month a man called my dad's shop to ask if we fix purses. We don't. I answered "Carvalho Shoe Service," and it took me three tries.

I have a stutter. It gets worse on the phone, and worst on hard sounds like C, which is a problem when your family's name starts with one. So working the counter, in a shop that smells like Barge cement and leather dust, I always answered with "Hi, shoe repair." My dad noticed after about a year. He never said anything.

On the second try I almost hung up. I was picturing the man's face, waiting for him to get annoyed. When the name came out I didn't feel proud, just embarrassed it took three tries. Then he asked about purses like nothing had happened. That was the best part. To him it was nothing.

I keep a list on my phone of every lie I tell. It started sophomore year, after I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept that up for three weeks. I forged her signature on the progress report, and there's a detail I'd rather leave out: first I practiced it about eight times on the back of a math worksheet. That means I planned it.

My teacher, Mr. Oduya, emailed home. I never told anyone I was relieved. Keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting. My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test to her, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed. I ended the class with a B+.

The list has 212 items now, mostly small ones about being on time, like "I'm almost there." I'm still not sure whether it makes me more honest or just better at noticing. Only recently did I realize "Hi, shoe repair" was a lie too. It isn't on the list. I'd been doing the same thing with the phone that I did with the test, swapping the true thing for an easier thing. I don't know what to do with that yet. Maybe add it.

I also keep a notebook of heels. It has 61 entries, and entry 1 is my dad's work boots. One woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left, and I said out loud that she must drive for work, her heel resting on the floor by the pedal. She did. She looked at me like I was a psychic.

My avó, Rosa, is harder to read. She had a stroke two years ago and has aphasia. She understands more than she can say. On Tuesdays and Thursdays we go through flashcards I made with pictures, in Portuguese. She is relearning the words while I learn some for the first time. She's better at "agulha." I'm better at "telemóvel." Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, my aunt who died before I was born. I used to correct her. Now I don't. She can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato."

I want to study speech-language pathology, something about how the brain gets words back. I wanted my stutter and her aphasia to be connected, and I read enough to learn they aren't. I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my old speech therapist, if I'd be "allowed" to do this work with a stutter. She said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.

Once I asked my dad whether he wanted me to take over the shop. He said, "The shop isn't a person, it doesn't need anyone." His right thumb is stained black from the dye. I can read a stranger's heel, but I still can't tell if that was a yes or a no.
