A Tree Is Not a Notebook

At my grandmother's 70th birthday, in front of my cousins, I told her that her apple tree was genetically boring. My uncle laughed. My äje didn't.

Her tree is an Aport, the famous Almaty apple, at her dacha. I had just learned that apples don't grow true from seed, so a variety survives only by grafting, and every Aport is technically the same tree, cloned for about 150 years. My 37 tagged trees in the foothills were Malus sieversii, the wild ancestor of basically every apple. I wanted the table to know I knew that.

Two weeks later, while she was in town, I sawed off a big branch and grafted on wild sieversii sticks I'd cut inside the national park, which isn't allowed. Wrong season: scions should be dormant, and mine had leaves and dried out in my backpack in the heat. I hadn't asked her. The graft died and the cut went black.

Dad was at Tengiz, the oil field. I told him on a video call like it was a science update. I said I'd cut the sticks "at the edge of the park." It was pretty far inside. He didn't say anything. When he got back he drove me to the dacha and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound with garden pitch. She said something fast in Kazakh. I got half, so in the car I asked Dad.

"Ағаш дәптер емес." A tree is not a notebook.

The grafting I can explain as a science mistake. The birthday I can't. I said it to sound clever. And I never told anyone I was kind of glad the graft died. If it had taken, a wild branch would sit on her tree forever, basically my signature, and she'd see it every summer.

Afterward Gennady Pavlovich, 81, who sells apples at the Green Bazaar, made me do 200 grafting cuts on willow sticks before he let me touch an apple.

Tree 23 was a smaller mistake. It was my best tree, 15.2 Brix, when wild ones usually run 9 to 15. Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden, cut one open, looked for ten seconds and said, "This one has been to the city." It grows 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably a hybrid with some dacha apple. I wanted to argue, as if the number in my notebook proved where a tree came from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good. I was angry at her, and at the apples.

My äje calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, because I answer her in Russian. Everybody laughs, me included. Tree 23 was half one thing and half the other, a city tree, and I got defensive for it. With the Aport I did the opposite: called her tree boring for being a copy and tried to force the wild thing into it.

Someone chose to keep the Aport the same, graft after graft, for 150 years, and I called that boring. Meanwhile our apartment switches between two houses every fourteen days, as Dad leaves for Tengiz or comes back, and I like that it's predictable. At 21:00 every evening he sends a photo of a flare stack and the sunset, the same view 340-something times. That's the most Aport thing in my life. Maybe I'm making that up because it sounds good. Gennady Pavlovich says if a graft looks too neat, you didn't cut deep enough.

So some of this stays rough. I want to study conservation genetics and be the person who decides which trees get saved. Some places cut out hybrids like Tree 23 so they won't pollinate the wild ones, and I don't know if I'd cut it. Probably the correct answer is yes. I also don't know why Dad sends the photo with no words. I've never asked.
