What the A Becomes

At the start of The Scarlet Letter, the A means what the authorities of Boston want it to mean. It brands Hester Prynne as a sinner. When she stands on the scaffold in "The Market-Place," Hawthorne writes that the letter "transfigured the wearer," so that neighbors who knew her well "were now impressed as if they beheld her for the first time." The effect is close to magic: "It had the effect of a spell, taking her out of the ordinary relations with humanity, and enclosing her in a sphere by herself." That last phrase matters. The punishment here is isolation more than pain, and the letter is the tool that does it. Yet Hawthorne also makes the A strangely beautiful. It is "so fantastically embroidered and illuminated upon her bosom" that it pulls every eye. A mark meant to shame her is already too striking to read only one way, which hints that its meaning won't stay put.

Years later, in "Another View of Hester," the town starts reading the letter differently. Hester has helped so many people that "The letter was the symbol of her calling." Hawthorne goes further: "many people refused to interpret the scarlet A by its original signification. They said that it meant Able; so strong was Hester Prynne, with a woman’s strength." The letter itself hasn't changed. Hester's behavior has, and the community's reading followed it. Still, a kinder public meaning does not make the thing light to carry. In "A Flood of Sunshine," Hester unclasps the letter in the forest and throws it aside, and her relief is enormous: "The stigma gone, Hester heaved a long, deep sigh, in which the burden of shame and anguish departed from her spirit." Then comes the line I find most revealing. "She had not known the weight, until she felt the freedom!" Whatever "Able" meant to her neighbors, to Hester the A was still a stigma, so heavy she had stopped noticing it.

The ending is where the meaning finally becomes hers. In the "Conclusion" she returns to Boston and puts the letter back on, "of her own free will," even though "not the sternest magistrate of that iron period would have imposed it." Nobody forces her this time. "Never afterwards did it quit her bosom." Over her "toilsome, thoughtful, and self-devoted years," the letter "ceased to be a stigma which attracted the world’s scorn and bitterness, and became a type of something to be sorrowed over, and looked upon with awe, yet with reverence too." Reverence is a long way from the spell of the market-place. Across the novel, the A moves from a sentence handed down by the town, to a word the town rewrites, to a symbol Hester picks up again by choice. The magistrates gave the letter one meaning, but Hester wore it long enough, and lived well enough, that nobody could see only that one anymore.
