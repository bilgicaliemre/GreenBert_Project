Every apple seed grows into a new apple. Apples don't come true from seed, so a variety survives only through grafting. Every Aport in Almaty, my grandmother's included, is technically one tree, cloned for about 150 years. The wild ancestor of almost every apple, Malus sieversii, just grows in the foothills past Talgar.

I have 37 wild trees tagged with numbered zip ties. I can guess an apple's Brix, its sugar percentage, from one bite. When my mom tested me with a refractometer, I was within a point on 31 of 40 apples. My best tree, number 23, read 15.2. I brought a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds and said, "This one has been to the city." It grows 30 meters from a picnic spot. Probably a hybrid with somebody's dacha apple.

I wanted to argue. I had 15.2 in my notebook, as if sugar proves where a tree comes from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good.

She also said some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. The correct answer is probably yes. I'm not sure I would. Someone has to decide which trees get saved. I want that job, whatever it's called.

Our apples also live in New York, in a USDA orchard in Geneva grown from seeds Americans collected here in the 90s. That's half of why I'm applying. Maybe more than half.
