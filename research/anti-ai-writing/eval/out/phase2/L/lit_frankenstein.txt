Misery Made Me a Fiend

When the creature tells Victor on the glacier, "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend," he sums up his whole life in a single sentence. In his telling, wickedness is something that was done to him. The novel gives that misery a clear starting point: the night the creature first wakes. Victor admits he had "desired it with an ardour that far exceeded moderation," but the moment the work is finished, "breathless horror and disgust filled my heart." What he cannot bear is "the aspect of the being," in other words its appearance. The creature has said nothing and hurt no one. Victor simply rushes out and paces his bed-chamber, too shaken to sleep. Shelley makes the first rejection a judgment about looks, handed down by the one person who owed the creature care.

The scene after the De Lacey family turns him away shows how that rejection hardens into rage. The creature looks out on a world where "All, save I, were at rest or in enjoyment." He compares himself to "the arch-fiend" and says he "bore a hell within me." The key phrase is the reason he gives for wanting to "tear up the trees, spread havoc and destruction around me": he was "finding myself unsympathised with." The urge to destroy comes after the loss of sympathy, never before it. On the glacier the creature complains, "Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded," and calls himself "the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." The verb "drivest" matters. In the story of the Fall, the rebel angel brings his punishment on himself, but here the creature makes Victor the one doing the driving. He should have been "thy Adam," a first man looked after by his maker. He was cast out before he had done anything to earn it.

Still, the novel does not let the creature pass off all the blame. Speaking to Walton over Victor's body, he describes "the frightful catalogue of my sins," and the possessive is his own. He can hardly connect himself to the being "whose thoughts were once filled with sublime and transcendent visions of the beauty and the majesty of goodness," yet he admits, "But it is even so; the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil." That is a confession. It sits uneasily beside his earlier bargain, "Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous," which reads partly as a plea and partly as a threat. As I read it, Shelley holds Victor responsible for starting the fall and holds the creature responsible for the sins he commits along the way. Loneliness ties the two together. Even at the end, his last grievance is that the devil himself "had friends and associates in his desolation; I am alone." A person can be made into a monster by neglect, the novel suggests, but being made one does not cancel what the monster then chooses to do.
