Ten Hours a Week

Every Friday night, someone in my grade is folding sweaters at the mall, ringing up groceries, or scraping burnt cheese off a pizza oven. Adults tend to look at these students and worry. They see tired kids who should be home studying. I see people learning things a classroom has a hard time teaching. High school students should hold part-time jobs during the school year, as long as the hours stay reasonable and school still comes first.

The first reason is money, and I don't mean that in a shallow way. When you earn your own paycheck, a pair of forty-dollar headphones stops being an abstract number. It turns into an entire Saturday shift of standing behind a register. Teens who work start doing that math without being asked. They think twice before buying lunch out every day. Some save for a car or help their family cover a phone bill. A personal finance unit in health class can explain what a budget is. A job makes you live inside one.

Work also teaches responsibility in a way homework can't. If you forget an assignment, you might lose a few points and get a reminder email. If you skip a shift, a coworker has to cover for you, a manager has to rearrange the schedule, and customers wait longer. The consequences land on real people. That pressure changes how a sixteen-year-old thinks about showing up. You learn to call ahead when you're sick, to ask for days off early, and to apologize to someone you let down. Those habits follow you into everything else.

Then there's dealing with people. At school, almost everyone you talk to is your age or is paid to be patient with you. At work, you meet the customer who wants a refund on a coupon that expired last month. You meet the coworker in his fifties who has strong opinions about how the cereal boxes should face. You figure out how to stay calm and when to go find a manager. Nobody grades you on it. You get better anyway.

The obvious objection is that jobs hurt grades. A student who closes a restaurant at eleven on a Tuesday is going to struggle on a chemistry test the next morning. I agree, which is why the hours matter. Ten or twelve hours a week, mostly on weekends, is very different from twenty-five. The trouble starts when a job takes over a student's life. Parents and managers can help by keeping schedules light during finals and sports seasons. I know plenty of people who spend ten hours a week scrolling on their phones. Trading some of that time for a paycheck and a few real skills is hardly a disaster.

Others argue that teenagers should get to enjoy being young while they can. I understand the feeling. But a lot of teens actually like working. There is a specific kind of pride in being trusted with the keys to open the store, or being the person a manager asks for by name. Being young doesn't have to mean having nothing to do with the adult world.

A part-time job isn't right for every student. Someone who watches younger siblings every afternoon, or who is carrying four AP classes, might not have room for one. For most high schoolers, though, a few shifts a week will teach them more about money, responsibility, and other people than they expect. School gives students knowledge. A job gives them a place to practice being the person they're about to become.
