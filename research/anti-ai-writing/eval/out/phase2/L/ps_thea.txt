Minus One

My record is 19 minutes. The hearse got stuck behind a combine, so I kept playing the same eight bars of "Abide with Me," changing stops so nobody noticed. I know it was 19 because I keep track. Since June 2024 I have played 48 funerals as the organist at Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, on a 1958 Wicks, in socks, because my shoes slip on the pedals.

By February of junior year I had counted 31 funerals and 6 baptisms at Immanuel. I put the numbers in Google Sheets, added a trend line, and posted the chart to the Cedar County Community Corner group on Facebook: "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!" Skull emoji.

It got 63 comments. Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, wrote, "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I told her anecdotes aren't data, a line I'd gotten off YouTube that week. Then I screenshotted her comment and sent it to the quiz bowl group chat to make fun of her. Then a man from Holy Trinity pointed out that the Catholics do most of the town's baptisms. I had counted one church, the old people's one. My whole post was an anecdote.

I deleted it after four hours. My mom, the rural mail carrier, made me write Kathy an apology with a real stamp, so it was legal, and delivered it with the mail.

I told nobody that I liked the 63 comments. I kept refreshing the post in Spanish class. It was the first time anything I made had been read by more people than Arlene Thoene, who played Immanuel's organ for 51 years before me. The mad comments were my favorite, which I know is bad.

I went to the Census website to prove I was right. I proved nothing. I found "natural decrease," the term for a county with more deaths than births, common out here, and behind it a whole field called demography, a word I had never heard. My school has no statistics class, so I take one on Zoom from a professor whose camera always points at his ceiling fan.

The trouble is Leonard Tietz. At his funeral a pipe stuck on, a cipher, one low note through the entire Lord's Prayer, and I ran behind the curtain to shut off the blower. Pastor Jill said, "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word," and everybody laughed, even his wife, and I was kind of mad. I think I wanted it to be my fault so I could fix it. After the lunch, Duane Pinkelman, who runs the funeral home, held the ladder while I pulled the pipe. Low F-sharp. There was a dead wasp inside.

In the census, Leonard is minus one. Duane says every single one was somebody's whole world.

I think I wanted the town to be dying when I made that chart. If it's dying anyway, leaving isn't really leaving anybody. The trend line was a permission slip. The county probably is shrinking. Just not like that, and not by my math.

I can't say cleanly why the numbers hold me. Some days I think the math makes a death bigger, every county at once. Other days it makes it smaller and easier, and the spreadsheet is there so I don't have to feel 48 separate things. I can't tell which yet. I want to study statistics, or demography, partly to find out.

Arlene is 86 now. She listens to the Sunday livestream from assisted living at full volume and texts me in all caps: "TOO FAST ON DOXOLOGY. BREATHE WITH THEM." I still don't know exactly what she means. I think I'm close.

Kathy never mentioned the note. She waves at my mom the same as before. I don't know if that means it worked.
