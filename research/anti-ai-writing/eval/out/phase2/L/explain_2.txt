Why Does Earth Have Seasons?

Ask a room full of people why summer is hot, and many will say the same thing: Earth gets closer to the Sun. It sounds reasonable. Stand closer to a campfire and you feel more heat, so why wouldn't the same be true for a planet?

The trouble is that the facts don't fit. Earth's orbit is very nearly a circle, so our distance from the Sun changes only a little over the year. Stranger still, Earth is closest to the Sun in early January. That's the middle of winter for anyone in the Northern Hemisphere. And if distance were the cause, both hemispheres would have summer at the same time. They don't. When it's July in Canada, people in Australia and Argentina are digging out their winter coats.

So what is going on? Tilt.

Earth spins on an axis, an imaginary line running through the North and South Poles. That axis isn't straight up and down compared to the path Earth follows around the Sun. It leans by about 23.5 degrees, and it keeps pointing the same direction in space all year long, roughly toward Polaris, the North Star.

Because of that steady lean, there's part of the year when the Northern Hemisphere is tipped toward the Sun and part when it's tipped away. The Southern Hemisphere gets the opposite deal, which is why its seasons run six months off from ours.

Tilt changes two things. The first is the angle of the sunlight. Shine a flashlight straight down at a table, then tilt it. The straight beam makes a small, bright spot. The tilted beam spreads the same light over a bigger, dimmer patch. When your hemisphere leans toward the Sun, sunlight arrives more like that straight beam, packing more energy into each square meter of ground.

The second thing is day length. In summer the Sun rises earlier, climbs higher, and sets later, so the ground gets more hours to warm up and fewer hours of darkness to cool off. Winter flips that around: short days, a low Sun, and long cold nights.

Put those two effects together and you get seasons. Near the equator people barely notice them, since the Sun stays fairly high there all year. Near the poles it gets extreme, with months of nearly nonstop daylight in summer and months of darkness in winter.

Want to see it for yourself? Grab a lamp and a ball, push a pencil through the ball at a slant, and carry it in a circle around the lamp, keeping the pencil aimed at the same wall. Watch which half catches the more direct light. That shifting glow is your seasons.
