Minute and Far Away

In the first chapter of The Great Gatsby, the green light barely exists. The narrator watches Gatsby stretch out his arms "toward the dark water in a curious way" and says he "could have sworn he was trembling." When he glances out at the water, he finds "nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." Fitzgerald makes the object as small and unsure as he can, and the narrator can only guess where it sits. All the emotion in the scene comes from Gatsby's body. We learn that the light matters before we know why, by watching a man shake as he reaches for it.

Chapter 5 gives the light an owner. Gatsby tells Daisy, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock." The words "always" and "all night" give him away. He has been watching it for a long time. Once Daisy is actually with him, though, the light starts to shrink. The narrator guesses that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever." "Colossal" is a strange word for something that was "minute" four chapters earlier. The light's real size never changed. Its size in Gatsby's mind did. While Daisy was out of reach, it "had seemed very near to her, almost touching her," as "close as a star to the moon." Then, flatly: "Now it was again a green light on a dock." The word "again" is harsh. It hints that the light was ordinary all along and Gatsby had dressed it up. The narrator adds that "his count of enchanted objects had diminished by one," which sounds like a clerk updating a list. The closer Gatsby gets to Daisy, the less the light can do for him.

By Chapter 9 the light has moved past Daisy completely. "Gatsby believed in the green light," the narrator writes, "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." Daisy isn't in that sentence. The light now stands for the future, and the future follows one rule: it backs away as you chase it. The pronouns shift as well. In Chapter 1 the trembling figure was Gatsby alone, but here the narrator says "us" and "we," pulling the reader to the edge of the water too. The line "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther" repeats Gatsby's gesture from the opening, except now everyone is making it. Then the passage cuts off at "And one fine morning" and never finishes. That broken sentence might be the most honest thing Fitzgerald says about the light. The hope it stands for has no ending, so the sentence describing it doesn't get one either. What started as a speck on a dock has become a promise that keeps getting pushed back a day, which may be why it is so hard to look away from.
