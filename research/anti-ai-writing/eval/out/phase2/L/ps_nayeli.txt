Second Tail

The tail of a lion sees nothing but the floor and the feet in front of it. For five years those feet belonged to my best friend, Bảo-Trân, the head. I held her belt and followed. Her dad, Chú Tâm, played the drum, and we practiced Saturdays in his transmission shop. I got the spot in seventh grade because I could squat with Bảo standing on my legs.

This February we had fourteen Tết shows in three days. At a new bakery, the lettuce and red envelope hung high on the awning. That meant the high stand: Bảo steps onto my thighs and I lift her by the belt to my shoulders. My left knee started shaking. I stepped back, and she had to come down. The lion's head hit the awning, and the little mirror on its forehead rolled under a Camry.

I laughed, a nervous, stupid laugh, and inside the lion you hear everything. Bảo thought I was laughing at her. She never said so, but in the van she didn't look at me, and she always looks at me in the van.

On the second try I couldn't get her past my thighs. The owner's son held the lettuce out on a broom, and our lion ate it off the broom in front of about sixty people. Chú Tâm put Bảo's cousin Khôi in as tail. Khôi is fourteen, maybe 110 pounds. In the van I told everyone, "He's gonna fold like a lawn chair." That night he got Bảo up on the first try, and everybody screamed. I was on cymbals, the little kids' job. I never apologized. I Venmo'd him ten dollars with the note "for takis."

What I told nobody was why my knee shook. I had skipped six of the last eight Saturdays before Tết. I said it was work, and it was, because I asked a coworker to trade shifts so it would be. Someone had posted a TikTok from a grand opening we did in December: the tail comes off, it's me, and the caption says "the back half of the lion is in my AP Lang class" with a skull emoji. I said it was funny. I checked it every day for a month. My first skipped Saturday came nine days after it went up.

I also know what I was thinking up there. At Tết everyone films. If we fell, it would be on somebody's phone, so I stepped back before it could become a fall. A step back looks like a decision. I picked what would look better on video, and Bảo could have landed on the curb.

For five years I've said I liked the tail because nobody sees your face. I think I liked it because I could hide. Even the lawn chair line was hiding. I needed Khôi to fold. If he folded, the position was just hard. If he didn't, it was me.

My dad fixes commercial ice machines. He likes to say nobody thinks about the ice until there's no ice. He works in the back of every restaurant on Bellaire and calls himself the ice guy, like it's a title. I spent five years in the back of a lion, hiding. Same place, opposite reasons.

Chú Tâm wants an answer about next Tết by Thanksgiving. Khôi is the tail now, so I'd be second tail or back on cymbals. I can't tell yet if I'd go back to fix last February or because I miss it. Bảo and I text every day, about everything except the laugh. What I learned at that bakery: when I'm scared of being seen, I step back and call it a decision. This time I want my answer to be a real one.

I still have the furry tail pants. Chú Tâm never asked for them back. I sleep in them.
