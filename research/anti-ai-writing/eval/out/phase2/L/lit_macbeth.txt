Whose Hands Are Clean?

In Macbeth, blood on the hands stands for guilt, and Shakespeare treats that guilt as something physical, a stain that either washes off or doesn't. Right after murdering Duncan, Macbeth stares at his hand and asks, "Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" He answers himself at once. His hand "will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine, / Making the green one red." Macbeth knows right away that the deed can't be undone, and he pictures the stain spreading instead of fading. Lady Macbeth, back from smearing the grooms with blood, sees the same thing and shrugs. "My hands are of your color, but I shame / To wear a heart so white," she tells him, calling his fear cowardice. Then comes the line that will come back for her: "A little water clears us of this deed: / How easy is it then!" To her, blood is a mess to wash off before whoever is knocking gets inside. Put "a little water" next to "all great Neptune’s ocean" and you have the whole play in miniature. He thinks no amount of water is enough. She thinks a basin will do.

By Act III the husband has changed. After Banquo's ghost ruins the banquet, Macbeth doesn't recoil from more killing. He reasons through it like a man halfway across a river: "I am in blood / Stepp’d in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o’er." The blood has spread from his hand into something he wades through, and the word he picks for turning back is "tedious." That is a flat, tired word to use about murder. Just before it he announces, "For mine own good, / All causes shall give way," which shows how small his world has become. The man who once believed the oceans couldn't clean him has stopped trying to get clean.

Lady Macbeth goes the opposite way. In Act V she walks in her sleep while a Doctor and a Gentlewoman watch, and she is still talking about the blood. "Here’s the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand. Oh, oh, oh!" Her exaggeration now sounds like his did in Act II. Arabia's perfumes take the place of Neptune's ocean, one more giant remedy that fails. "A little water" has turned into "this little hand," and the word "little" now works against her, because the hand is small and the guilt is huge. She has picked up the horror he put down, and the two have traded places. The trade suggests that guilt can be pushed aside, as she pushed it aside in Act II, or dulled by repetition, as he dulls it in Act III, but it doesn't go away. It comes back at night as a smell, and three groans are all the language she has left for it.
