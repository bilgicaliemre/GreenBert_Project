On Tuesdays and Thursdays after school, my grandmother and I do flashcards. Two years ago Avó Rosa had a stroke, and now she has aphasia: she understands more than she can say. I made the cards myself, pictures with Portuguese words underneath. She is relearning the words; I'm learning them for the first time. She beats me on "agulha," needle. I beat her on "telemóvel." She can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato."

That's how I got curious about how the brain gets words back. I read a lot about aphasia now, and along the way I found out something I didn't want to know: stuttering and aphasia are totally different things. I have a stutter. I had hoped her trouble with words and mine were connected. They aren't.

What I keep coming back to instead is the problem of understanding someone from the outside when they can't tell you. At my dad's shoe repair shop I read heels. A worn outer edge means the person supinates. One woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left, and it turned out she drives for work. My heel notebook has 61 entries. Heels are easy. Avó isn't. When she gets a word wrong, I can't tell what she's thinking.

I want to study speech-language pathology to learn how to read that. My old speech therapist says some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet, but I want to find out.
