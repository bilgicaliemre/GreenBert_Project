A Tree Is Not a Notebook

At my grandmother's seventieth birthday dinner, I told her that her apple tree was genetically boring.

I had just learned that apples don't grow true from seed; varieties survive only by grafting. Every Aport in Almaty is technically one tree, cloned for about 150 years. My äje's Aport, at her dacha in Kamenka, was a copy of a copy. I said it to sound clever in front of my cousins. My uncle laughed. My äje didn't.

Two weeks later I sawed a big branch off her tree and grafted on wild Malus sieversii, the ancestor of almost every apple, which grows in the foothills where I've tagged 37 trees. I cut the scions inside the national park, which isn't allowed, and carried them for two days in the heat. It was the wrong season; mine had leaves instead of being dormant, and they dried out. The graft died and the cut went black.

My dad works fourteen-day shifts at the Tengiz oil field, so I told him on a video call, like a science update. He said nothing. I also told him I'd cut the sticks "at the edge of the park." They came from pretty far inside. When he got home, he drove me to the dacha and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound with garden pitch. She said something fast in Kazakh; I caught half and asked my dad in the car. "Ағаш дәптер емес." A tree is not a notebook.

The grafting I could explain as a technical mistake. The birthday I couldn't. There is no science excuse for wanting a table to know you know something. And I never asked her. What I told nobody: I was a little glad the graft died. If it had taken, her tree would have carried my signature forever, and she'd have seen it every summer.

Afterward Gennady Pavlovich, an 81-year-old apple seller at the Green Bazaar, taught me grafting properly: 200 cuts on willow sticks before I could touch an apple. He says if a graft looks too neat, you didn't cut deep enough.

So, the less neat part. My best wild tree, Tree 23, measured 15.2 Brix. I took a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds, and said, "This one has been to the city." It grows 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably crossed with someone's dacha apple. I wanted to argue, as if the number in my notebook proved where a tree came from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good. I was angry at her, and also at the apples, which made no sense.

It made sense only recently. My äje jokingly calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, because I answer her in Russian. Everyone laughs, me too. Tree 23 is half one thing and half the other, not the real wild thing, and I got defensive for a tree. With the Aport I did the reverse: I called her tree boring for being a copy and tried to put the wild thing into it. I don't know yet what that says about me.

Some things are still open. Zhanna Serikovna said some places remove hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. I want to study conservation genetics and decide which trees get saved, yet I don't know if I'd cut Tree 23. If I spend four years in America, my Kazakh gets worse, and äje is 70. I haven't said that to my parents. Every evening at 21:00 my dad sends the same photo from Tengiz, a flare stack at sunset, and I've never asked why it comes with no words.

At that birthday dinner I answered a question nobody had asked. Now I have questions I haven't asked yet, and I'm learning to ask before I cut.
