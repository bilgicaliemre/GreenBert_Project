The Letter That Outgrew Its Meaning

When Hester Prynne first stands on the scaffold, the scarlet letter works less as a label than as a wall. Hawthorne writes that it "had the effect of a spell, taking her out of the ordinary relations with humanity, and enclosing her in a sphere by herself." The punishment is not only public shame but isolation, and it takes hold instantly: people who had been "familiarly acquainted with Hester Prynne" now look at her "as if they beheld her for the first time." The letter has "transfigured the wearer," so that her neighbors see the symbol before they see the woman. Yet Hawthorne complicates this from the start. The letter is "so fantastically embroidered and illuminated upon her bosom" that it draws every eye, and words like "embroidered" and "illuminated" belong to artwork and decoration, not to punishment. Even at the moment of its harshest meaning, the A is already more than the authorities intended.

Over the following years, the letter's meaning shifts because the town's reading of it shifts. In "Another View of Hester," Hawthorne reports that "many people refused to interpret the scarlet A by its original signification. They said that it meant Able." The cause is Hester's own conduct: in her there is "so much power to do, and power to sympathize." The cloth itself has not changed; what has changed is the community's interpretation, which suggests that the symbol's meaning was never stitched into the fabric in the first place. Still, the public's new reading does not erase what the letter costs Hester privately. When she unclasps it in the forest, "the burden of shame and anguish departed from her spirit," and the narrator exclaims, "She had not known the weight, until she felt the freedom!" The town may call her Able, but Hester has been carrying the letter as a burden all along, one so constant that she only feels its weight once it is gone.

The final change comes in the "Conclusion," when Hester returns to Boston and puts the letter back on "of her own free will, for not the sternest magistrate of that iron period would have imposed it." This is the most important shift in the novel: the A is no longer a sentence handed down to her but a choice she makes for herself. Over her "toilsome, thoughtful, and self-devoted years," it "ceased to be a stigma which attracted the world's scorn and bitterness, and became a type of something to be sorrowed over, and looked upon with awe, yet with reverence too." Hawthorne is careful here. The letter does not turn into a simple badge of honor; it is still something "to be sorrowed over." Its meaning now holds both the old shame and the long, devoted life that followed it. By tracing the A from spell to calling to chosen symbol, Hawthorne shows that the magistrates who made the letter could control its shape, but not what it would finally mean.
