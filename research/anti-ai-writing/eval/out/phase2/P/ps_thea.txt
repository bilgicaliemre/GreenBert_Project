Minus One

I don't lose track of much. Since June 2024 I have played the organ at 48 funerals, for Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, Nebraska, and for the funeral home when it calls. I keep count. I play the pedals in socks because my shoes slip, and I have never once been to a funeral as just a guest.

In February of my junior year my count stood at 31 funerals and 6 baptisms, so I put the numbers in Google Sheets, added a trend line, and posted the chart to the Cedar County Community Corner group on Facebook: "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!" Skull emoji.

It got 63 comments. Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, wrote, "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I answered, "anecdotes aren't data," a line I'd picked up from a YouTube video that week. Then I screenshotted her comment and sent it to the quiz bowl group chat so we could laugh at her. Then a man from Holy Trinity pointed out that the Catholics do most of the baptisms in town and Immanuel is basically the old people's church. I had only counted my church. My whole post was an anecdote.

I deleted it after four hours. My mom is the rural mail carrier, so she made me write Kathy an apology and delivered it with the mail. Real stamp.

What I told nobody is that I liked it, the angry comments most of all. I kept refreshing the post in Spanish class. It was the first time anything I'd made was read by more people than Arlene, my old piano teacher.

Then I went to the Census website to prove I'd been right, and ended up somewhere else: "natural decrease," the term for a county with more deaths than births, which describes a lot of counties out here. There was a whole field for this, demography, a word I hadn't known. My school doesn't offer statistics, so now I take it as dual credit through Northeast Community College, on Zoom.

At a funeral, a death is Leonard Tietz. During his Lord's Prayer a pipe stuck on, a cipher, one low note droning until I ran behind the curtain and shut off the blower. Pastor Jill said, "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word," and everyone laughed, even his wife. After the lunch I climbed a ladder while Duane Pinkelman, the funeral director, held it, and pulled the pipe. Low F-sharp. There was a dead wasp inside.

In the census, Leonard is minus one.

Duane says every single one was somebody's whole world. I can't decide whether I love the math because it makes death bigger, every county at once, or because it makes it smaller and easier. Some days I think the spreadsheet exists so I don't have to feel 48 separate things.

There's a worse possibility. I think I wanted the town to be dying. If Hartington is finished anyway, then leaving isn't really leaving anybody. The 2070 prediction was a permission slip I wrote myself with a trend line. I didn't know that until someone asked me.

The county probably is shrinking. Just not like that, and not by my math. That gap is what holds me, between minus one and Leonard's wife laughing, between a trend line and Kathy's 22 kids. Next time, I want to count the whole county, not just my church.

Arlene played at Immanuel for 51 years. Now she's 86, listens to the Sunday livestream from assisted living with the volume all the way up, and texts me afterward in all caps: "TOO FAST ON DOXOLOGY. BREATHE WITH THEM." I still don't know exactly what that means. I think I'm close.

I don't know if Kathy ever read the note. She waves at my mom the same as before.
