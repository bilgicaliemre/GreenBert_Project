One Project, Different Grades

Everyone who has done a group project knows the moment. The due date is two days away, the slideshow is half finished, and one member of the group has not answered a text since the assignment was handed out. Someone, usually the student who cares most about their grade, stays up late finishing that person's slides. A week later, the whole group gets the same B-plus. The student who did the extra work and the student who disappeared are treated as equals. That is unfair. Group members should not automatically receive the same grade; teachers should grade each student at least partly on what that student actually contributed.

The strongest case for a shared grade is that it teaches teamwork. In the working world, the argument goes, a team succeeds or fails together, so students should learn to depend on one another and take responsibility for the group as a whole. A shared stake can push students to communicate, divide tasks, and check each other's work. But a shared grade does not teach those skills. It only rewards the result. A group can earn an A because one capable student did everything while the others watched, and the grade tells the teacher nothing about whether anyone learned to cooperate. Workplaces are also less forgiving than the argument suggests. A coworker who never does their job eventually faces consequences. In a classroom with a single group grade, that coworker faces none.

The deeper problem is what a grade is supposed to mean. A grade on a report card is meant to describe what one student knows and can do. If a student who wrote nothing receives the same score as the student who wrote the whole paper, the grade stops describing either of them. It inflates one and hides the effort of the other. Over a semester with several group projects, those distortions add up, and the final grade becomes partly a measure of luck: who you were grouped with.

Identical grades also create bad incentives. Students are practical about effort. If the grade will be the same no matter what, the easy move for a student with a busy week is to let the most anxious group member pick up the slack. Meanwhile, the hardworking student learns a lesson of their own: that doing more than your share is expected and unrewarded. Neither lesson is the one a teacher wants to teach. Over time, students start to dread group work, which defeats the purpose of assigning it.

This does not mean abandoning shared grades entirely. A fair system can mix shared and individual grading. Part of the grade could come from the final product, which everyone shares. Another part could come from individual evidence: a short reflection on what each person did, a confidential peer evaluation, or a quick check-in where the teacher asks each member to explain a section of the work. These methods are not perfect. Peer evaluations can be swayed by friendships, and reflections can be exaggerated. Still, they give the teacher more information than a single score does, and they give students a reason to do their part.

Group projects are worth assigning. Learning to plan with others, divide labor, and work through disagreements is a real skill. But the grade at the end should recognize the people who did the work, not hide them. When a teacher gives every member of a group the same grade no matter what, the message is that individual effort does not matter. Students notice that message, and many of them act on it.
