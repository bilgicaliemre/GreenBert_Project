The Light That Only Works at a Distance

When the green light first appears in Chapter 1 of The Great Gatsby, Fitzgerald shows the reader the gesture before the meaning. The narrator watches Gatsby alone at night as "he stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way," and he "could have sworn he was trembling." When he looks out to see what Gatsby is reaching for, he finds "nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." The scene depends on a mismatch. Gatsby's longing is enormous, physical enough to make him shake, while its object is tiny and so vague that the narrator can only guess it "might have been" a dock. At this point the light means whatever Gatsby needs it to mean, and the reader, like the narrator, sees the reaching without knowing what it is for.

Chapter 5 supplies the answer and then takes it away. Gatsby tells Daisy, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock," which reveals that the light marks her house and that he has spent his nights watching it. Once Daisy is beside him, though, the narrator suspects that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever." From across the water, the light "had seemed very near to her, almost touching her," even "as close as a star to the moon." The comparison gives the game away, since a star only looks close to the moon; the closeness was an illusion produced by distance. With Daisy actually present, "it was again a green light on a dock." The line "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one" suggests that the light's power never belonged to the light. Gatsby enchanted it himself, and getting closer to what he wanted broke the spell.

In the novel's final pages, the light grows from Gatsby's private symbol into a shared one: "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." The light no longer stands for Daisy's dock but for the future itself, and the word "us" pulls the narrator and the reader into Gatsby's position. The next promise, "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther," repeats the outstretched arms from Chapter 1, so the novel ends with the same gesture it began with, now made by everyone. Even the grammar refuses to arrive, as "And one fine morning——" breaks off before the sentence can finish. Across the novel, then, the green light moves from a mystery to a woman to a hope defined by its distance. What never changes is that it matters only while it is out of reach. Gatsby's dream survives as long as it is never tested, which is why the book can end mid-sentence: the light keeps us reaching precisely because we never get there.
