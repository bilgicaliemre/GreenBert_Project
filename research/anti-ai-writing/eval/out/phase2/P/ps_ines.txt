Hi, Shoe Repair

Last month I answered the phone at my dad's shop with its name. It took three tries, and on the second I almost hung up. When "Carvalho Shoe Service" finally came out, the man who had waited just asked if we fix purses, like nothing had happened. We don't. That was the best part: to him, it was nothing.

The shop has been on Pleasant Street in Fall River since 1994. I work the counter Saturdays, and since last summer I also do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. My hoodie smells like Barge cement; my friends say I smell like a cowboy's basement. I hate the phone. I stutter, and it gets worse on the phone and on hard sounds like "C," which is a problem when your last name is Carvalho. So I used to answer, "Hi, shoe repair." My dad noticed after about a year. He never said anything.

I can read how someone walks from the wear on their heels. One woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left, so I said out loud that she must drive for work; that heel rests by the pedal. She did, and looked at me like I was a psychic. My notebook of heels has 61 entries. Entry 1 is my dad's work boots.

On Tuesdays and Thursdays I do flashcards with my avó, Rosa. Since her stroke two years ago she has aphasia; she understands more than she can say. I made her picture cards in Portuguese, and she's relearning words I'm learning for the first time. She's better at "agulha," needle. I'm better at "telemóvel." Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, my aunt who died before I was born. I used to correct her. Now I don't. She can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato."

Heels and avó feel connected: both mean figuring someone out from the outside, when they can't tell you. But heels are easy. When avó gets a word wrong, I can't tell what she's thinking. Maybe the connection is just one I want.

Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept it up for three weeks and forged her signature on a progress report after practicing it about eight times on a math worksheet. I'd rather leave that part out, because it means I planned it. When Mr. Oduya emailed home, I was relieved. My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test, question by question, in Portuguese. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed.

Since then I've kept a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even "I'm almost there." It has 212 items, mostly about being on time. I'm not sure if it makes me more honest or just better at noticing. Only recently did I notice what's missing: "Hi, shoe repair." It's the same move I made with the test, swapping the true thing for an easier one. Maybe it belongs on the list.

I want to study speech-language pathology: how the brain gets words back. Stuttering and aphasia turn out to be unrelated, though I wanted them connected. When I asked my middle school speech therapist, Ms. Halvorsen, whether I'd be "allowed" to do her job with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.

I once asked my dad if he wanted me to take over the shop. He said, "The shop isn't a person. It doesn't need anyone." I still don't know if that was a yes or a no. But the shop has a name, and last month I said it to a stranger who only wanted to know about purses.
