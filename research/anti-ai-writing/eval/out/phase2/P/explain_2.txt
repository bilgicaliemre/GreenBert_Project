Why Does Earth Have Seasons?

Ask a group of people why summer is hot and winter is cold, and a lot of them will say something like, "Because Earth is closer to the Sun in summer." It sounds reasonable. Moving closer to a campfire makes you warmer, after all. But this explanation is wrong, and there's a simple way to show it.

The misconception

Earth's orbit around the Sun isn't a perfect circle, but it's very close to one. Our distance from the Sun changes by only about 3 percent over the year. In fact, Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, which is the middle of winter for everyone in the Northern Hemisphere.

There's a bigger problem with the distance idea. When it's summer in the United States, it's winter in Australia. If distance caused the seasons, the whole planet would have summer at once.

The real reason: tilt

Earth spins on an axis, an imaginary line running through the North and South Poles. That axis isn't straight up and down compared with Earth's path around the Sun. It's tilted by about 23.5 degrees. And it keeps pointing the same direction in space all year.

That means for part of the year the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun, and for the other part it leans away. The Southern Hemisphere always gets the opposite arrangement.

Leaning toward the Sun does two things. First, sunlight hits the ground more directly. Shine a flashlight straight down at a table, then tilt it. The straight-down beam makes a small, bright spot. The tilted beam spreads the same light over a larger, dimmer patch. Direct sunlight concentrates energy on a smaller area, so the ground heats up more.

Second, the hemisphere tilted toward the Sun gets longer days. More hours of daylight mean more time to soak up heat and fewer hours of darkness to cool off.

Put those together and you get summer. Flip them, with slanted light and short days, and you get winter.

Marking the year

The solstices, around June 21 and December 21, are the points when one hemisphere is tilted most directly toward the Sun. The equinoxes, in March and September, are the in-between points when neither hemisphere leans toward the Sun, and day and night are roughly equal in length everywhere.

Places near the equator get fairly direct sunlight all year, which is why they don't see the big temperature swings between summer and winter that places farther north or south do.

So the next time someone blames the seasons on distance, you can tell them it's all about the tilt.
