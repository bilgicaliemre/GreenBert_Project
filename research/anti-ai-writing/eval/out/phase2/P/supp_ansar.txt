Tree 23 was my best wild apple. Of my 37 tagged trees in the foothills past Talgar, it was the sweetest: 15.2 Brix, when wild ones run about 9 to 15. I took a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds, and said, "This one has been to the city." It grows 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably a hybrid with some dacha apple.

I wanted to argue. I had 15.2 in my notebook, as if a sugar reading proves where a tree comes from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good.

Since then I keep asking what "wild" means. Malus sieversii, the ancestor of basically every apple, just grows here. Apples don't grow true from seed, so a variety survives only through grafting; every Aport, Almaty's famous apple, is technically one tree cloned for about 150 years. The wild forests are shrinking, though I don't fully trust the "70 percent gone since the 1960s" line every website copies. Some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees.

If Tree 23 were my decision, I don't know if I'd cut it. Probably the correct answer is yes. My grandmother calls me shala-kazakh, half-Kazakh, because I answer her in Russian, so maybe I'm biased toward half-city things.

I want to study conservation genetics and be the person who decides which trees get saved. I don't know yet what that job is called.
