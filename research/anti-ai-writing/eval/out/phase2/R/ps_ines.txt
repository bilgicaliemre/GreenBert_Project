Until last month, I had never once answered the phone at my dad's shop with its name. Carvalho Shoe Service has been on Pleasant Street since 1994. I work the counter on Saturdays, and since last summer I've been allowed to do heels and half-soles. I hate the phone. My stutter gets worse on it, especially on hard sounds like "C," and the shop is called Carvalho, so I answered "Hi, shoe repair" instead. My dad noticed after about a year and never said anything.

I can tell how someone walks from the wear on their heels. One woman's right heel was ground down way more than her left; it turned out she drives for work, and that heel rests by the pedal. When I said it out loud, she looked at me like I was a psychic. My notebook of heels has 61 entries, starting with my dad's work boots.

My avó, my grandmother Rosa, had a stroke two years ago and has aphasia. She understands more than she can say. On Tuesdays and Thursdays we go through flashcards I made her, with pictures, in Portuguese. Heels and avó are both about figuring out what's going on with someone from the outside, when they can't tell you, except heels are easy. When avó gets a word wrong, I can't tell what she's thinking. She can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato," which means shoe.

Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept it up for three weeks and forged her signature on the progress report. The part I'd rather leave out is that I practiced her signature first, about eight times, on the back of a math worksheet. That means I planned it. At the time I told nobody that I was relieved when Mr. Oduya emailed home; keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting.

My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese. It took two hours. I didn't know the Portuguese word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed. After that I made a rule: I keep a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even small ones like "I'm almost there." I'm still not sure if the list makes me more honest or just better at noticing. It has 212 items since October of sophomore year, most of them about being on time.

Last month a man called the shop, and I tried the name. I was picturing his face, wondering if he was annoyed, and on the second try I almost hung up. He waited. When "Carvalho Shoe Service" finally came out, I didn't feel proud; I felt kind of embarrassed that it took three tries. The best part was that it was nothing to him. He asked if we fix purses (we don't), like nothing had happened.

Only when someone asked me about that call did I notice that "Hi, shoe repair" was a lie too. It was small, and it isn't on my list. I've been doing the same thing with the phone that I did with the test, swapping the true thing for an easier thing, and I'm not sure what to do with that yet, except maybe add it to the list.

I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics, something about how the brain gets words back. I read a lot about aphasia now. I kind of wanted it to be connected to stuttering, and it isn't; they're totally different things. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my middle school speech therapist, whether I'd be "allowed" to be a speech therapist with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.
