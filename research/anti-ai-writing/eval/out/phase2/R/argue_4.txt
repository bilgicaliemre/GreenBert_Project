A few shifts a week

I think high school students should have a part-time job during the school year, as long as the hours stay small: a shift or two on the weekend, or a couple of weeknights. Parents worry that a job will pull grades down, and with too many hours it will. At a few shifts a week, though, a job teaches things school isn't set up to teach, and it can even push students to use their study time better.

Getting paid is the obvious benefit. The bigger change is in how you think about money. When your parents buy things for you, money seems to just show up. Once you've earned it, you start doing math in hours, looking at a pair of sneakers and counting how many Saturday shifts they cost. Your first paycheck will probably be smaller than you expected because taxes come out of it, and the pay stub explains taxes faster than a personal finance unit in class. People who have earned their money tend to think harder before spending it, because they know what it cost them.

At a job, your mistakes land on other people. Miss a homework assignment and you usually lose some points and turn it in late; nobody else has to do anything. Miss a shift and a coworker has to cover for you while the manager redoes the schedule. At school, being on time can feel like a rule that exists for its own sake. At work you can see who's waiting on you, so showing up on time and calling ahead when you're sick start to make sense.

Working also gets you out of a building full of people your own age. Behind a counter you deal with customers who are annoyed that their food came out cold, and you work next to people who have been doing the job for decades. Staying calm while someone complains at you, or asking a supervisor a question you're afraid is dumb, gets easier with practice, and that confidence carries over to college interviews and group projects.

The strongest argument against working is that it takes time from studying and sleep, and that school should be a teenager's only job. Part of that is right. A student working thirty hours a week will almost certainly see their grades drop, and no paycheck is worth that. But a couple of shifts a week still leaves plenty of time for homework, and a tighter schedule can actually help. If you know you only have two hours before work, you start the essay instead of scrolling on your phone. A wide-open afternoon is easy to waste, because it always feels like there's time later.

Another objection is that teenagers should get to be kids before adult responsibilities arrive. But a part-time job isn't a career. It's a place to make small mistakes, like miscounting the cash drawer or forgetting to restock the napkins, at an age when those mistakes don't cost much. I'd rather learn how to handle a demanding boss at seventeen than at twenty-three, when my rent depends on keeping the job.

Parents and schools tend to treat a job as a distraction from school. Kept to a few shifts a week, it's part of an education. So if a junior asks to apply at the grocery store down the street, I think the answer should be yes, with a cap on hours the family agrees on and one rule: if grades slip, the hours get cut first.
