Why does Earth have seasons?

Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, about 147 million kilometers away, and farthest in early July, at about 152 million. So if you live in the Northern Hemisphere, you're nearest the Sun in winter. That rules out a common explanation: that summer comes when Earth moves closer to the Sun.

It's easy to believe, since stepping closer to a campfire does warm you up. But Earth's orbit is nearly a circle, so the distance changes by only about 3 percent. And the hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time. In January it's winter in New York and summer in Sydney, though both cities are the same distance from the Sun, so the real cause must treat the hemispheres differently.

That cause is the tilt of Earth's axis, the imaginary line through the poles that Earth spins around. The axis is tipped about 23.5 degrees from upright, compared with the plane of Earth's orbit, and it keeps pointing the same way in space all year (the north end points close to Polaris, the North Star). For part of the year the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun and for the rest it leans away; the Southern Hemisphere does the opposite.

Leaning toward the Sun changes two things. One is the angle of sunlight. Shine a flashlight straight down at a table and you get a small, bright circle; tilt it and the same light spreads into a bigger, dimmer oval. Summer sunlight comes in steeply, like the straight-down beam, so each square meter of ground gets more energy and warms faster.

The other is day length. The hemisphere tilted toward the Sun gets long days and short nights, so the ground has more hours to heat up and fewer to cool off. Around June 21, the Sun doesn't set at all north of the Arctic Circle.

That date is the June solstice, one of two days (the other is around December 21) when a hemisphere is tilted most directly toward the Sun. At noon that day the Sun is straight overhead along the Tropic of Cancer, about 23.5 degrees north of the equator. The number matches the tilt because the tilt sets how far north the overhead Sun can reach. At the equinoxes in March and September, neither hemisphere leans toward the Sun, and day and night are roughly equal everywhere.

Near the equator, the noon Sun stays high and days stay close to 12 hours all year, so summer and winter feel much alike. Toward either pole, the Sun's angle and day length change more over the year, and the seasons get stronger.
