Blood on the Hands in Macbeth

In Macbeth, blood on the hands stands for guilt, but in Act II the Macbeths don't read it the same way. Macbeth sees a stain that will never come off. Lady Macbeth sees evidence, and evidence can be washed. Right after he kills Duncan, Macbeth asks, "Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" His answer is no: his hand "will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine, / Making the green one red." Water is supposed to clean the hand. Here the hand dyes the sea. Back from smearing the grooms with blood, Lady Macbeth tells him, "My hands are of your color, but I shame / To wear a heart so white." Red hands don't embarrass her. A heart as white, meaning as scared, as his would. Then she hears knocking at the south entry and says, "A little water clears us of this deed: / How easy is it then!" In her version Macbeth's ocean has become "a little water," because what worries her is whoever is at the door.

After Banquo's ghost appears at the banquet in Act III, Macbeth has stopped asking whether the blood will wash off. He says, "I am in blood / Stepp’d in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o’er." The blood used to be on his hand. Now he's standing in it, and going back would take as much effort as going across. The word that bothers me is "tedious." It makes murder sound like a chore, and his reason for going on is just as flat: "For mine own good, / All causes shall give way." That puts him close to his wife's Act II attitude, since the blood no longer slows him down. But he never claims, as she did, that a little water will clear him. He seems to accept that he'll never be clean and keeps going anyway, which I find colder than anything she said.

Lady Macbeth ends up where Macbeth started. Sleepwalking in Act V while the Doctor and Gentlewoman watch, she says, "Here’s the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand. Oh, oh, oh!" It has the same shape as his first speech: something enormous, the ocean then and all the perfumes of Arabia now, can't clean one hand. Even the word "little" has moved. In Act II "a little water" was enough; now the hand is little and nothing is enough for it. The blood has gone from a color to a smell, and she's speaking prose instead of verse. They do trade places, but not evenly. She takes on his Act II horror completely. He takes on her calm but never her belief that the blood washes off, and I think that's why the guilt breaks her and doesn't stop him: she's the one who expected it to be easy.
