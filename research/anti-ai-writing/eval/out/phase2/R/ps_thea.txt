My Saturdays depend on whether somebody died. Since June 2024 I've played 48 funerals (I keep track) as the organist at Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, Nebraska, and for the funeral home when it calls.

By February of junior year I'd counted 31 funerals and 6 baptisms at Immanuel, so I made a chart in Google Sheets with a trend line and posted it to the Cedar County Community Corner group on Facebook with a skull emoji and the words "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!"

It got 63 comments. Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, wrote, "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I answered "anecdotes aren't data," which I'd learned from a YouTube video that week, to a woman with 22 kids in her basement. I also sent a screenshot of her comment to the quiz bowl group chat to make fun of her.

A man from Holy Trinity pointed out that the Catholics do most of the baptisms in town and Immanuel is basically the old people's church. I had counted one church, so my whole post was an anecdote. I deleted it after four hours. My mom, the rural mail carrier, made me write Kathy an apology, with a real stamp so it was legal, and delivered it with the mail.

I kept refreshing the post in Spanish class, and I didn't tell anyone I liked it, especially the mad comments, which is bad, I know. It was the first time anything I'd made got read by more people than Arlene, my old piano teacher.

I went on the Census website to prove I was right and ended up reading about "natural decrease," when a county has more deaths than births, which a lot of counties out here have. It was a whole field, demography, a word I hadn't known, and now I want to study it. My school doesn't offer statistics, so I take it as dual credit through Northeast Community College on Zoom.

The worst funeral was Leonard Tietz's. A pipe got stuck on, and one low note held through the whole Lord's Prayer until I ran behind the curtain and shut off the blower. Pastor Jill said, "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word," and everybody laughed, even his wife. I was kind of mad. I think I wanted it to be my fault so I could fix it.

After the lunch, Duane Pinkelman, the funeral director, held the ladder while I climbed up and pulled the pipe. It was low F-sharp, and there was a dead wasp in it. At the funeral, a death is Leonard and his wife laughing and the wasp. In the census it's minus one.

Duane says every single one was somebody's whole world. I don't know if I like the math because it makes it bigger, all the counties at once, or because it makes it smaller and easier. Sometimes I think the spreadsheet is so I don't have to feel 48 separate things.

I also think I wanted the town to be dying. The 2070 line was a permission slip I wrote myself with a trend line, because if Hartington is dying anyway, leaving isn't really leaving anybody. I still don't know if I'm coming back after college.

The county probably is shrinking, just not like that, and not by my math. I don't know if Kathy ever read the note. She never said anything, and she waves at my mom the same as before.

Arlene was the organist at Immanuel for 51 years. She's 86 now, in assisted living, and listens to the Sunday livestream with the volume all the way up and texts me afterward in all caps: "TOO FAST ON DOXOLOGY. BREATHE WITH THEM." I still don't know exactly what breathe with them means. I think I'm close.
