In The Scarlet Letter, the A starts as a punishment the town puts on Hester Prynne and ends as something she chooses to wear. In between, many people decide it means "Able," but I think the last change, when the choice becomes Hester's, is the bigger one. On the scaffold, the letter's first job is to cut her off. Hawthorne says it "had the effect of a spell, taking her out of the ordinary relations with humanity, and enclosing her in a sphere by herself." Men and women who had been "familiarly acquainted" with her now look at her "as if they beheld her for the first time." So the punishment does more than embarrass her: it makes her a stranger to people who already knew her. Even here, though, the letter is "so fantastically embroidered and illuminated upon her bosom" that it draws "all eyes," and "embroidered" and "illuminated" sound like words for decoration. That's a strange way to describe a punishment, and it hints early that the letter can mean more than one thing.

Years later, in "Another View of Hester," Hawthorne writes that "many people refused to interpret the scarlet A by its original signification. They said that it meant Able." The word "refused" shows that the new meaning was something people chose. The reason was Hester herself: there was "so much power to do, and power to sympathize" in her. The new reading didn't lift the shame for Hester, though. When she unclasps the letter in the forest, "the burden of shame and anguish departed from her spirit," and Hawthorne adds, "She had not known the weight, until she felt the freedom!" While others called it Able, she was still carrying it as shame, and she only felt how heavy it was once it was off.

The bigger change comes in the "Conclusion." Hester returns to Boston and puts the letter on again "of her own free will, for not the sternest magistrate of that iron period would have imposed it," and "Never afterwards did it quit her bosom." In the forest, the stigma left only when the letter came off ("The stigma gone," Hawthorne writes after she throws it aside). At the end it's the other way around. The letter stays on, and over her "toilsome, thoughtful, and self-devoted years" it "ceased to be a stigma which attracted the world's scorn and bitterness." That doesn't make it a plain badge of honor. It became "a type of something to be sorrowed over, and looked upon with awe, yet with reverence too." When people renamed it Able, they swapped one word for another. Here Hawthorne gives no single word, only "something" that people regard with sorrow, awe and reverence at once. Since the narrator still calls it the symbol of "so dark a tale," I read that "something" as Hester's whole history, the dark tale and the years of work after it, which is more than one word like "Able" can hold.
