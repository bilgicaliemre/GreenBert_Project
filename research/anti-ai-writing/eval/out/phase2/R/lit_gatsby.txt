The green light in The Great Gatsby only has power while it's far away, and its meaning changes each time that distance changes. In Chapter 1 we see the reaching before we see the light. Gatsby "stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way," and the narrator "could have sworn he was trembling." The narrator glances seaward "involuntarily," as if the gesture pulled his eyes along, and finds "nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." Gatsby wants something badly enough to shake, but the light is so small that the narrator can't say for sure what it is, and the scene doesn't tell us what it means to him.

Chapter 5 names the light and then takes its power away. Gatsby tells Daisy, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock." Since he knows it burns all night, he has probably spent a lot of nights watching it. Once he's actually with her, though, the narrator guesses it may have occurred to Gatsby that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever." Measured against "the great distance that had separated him from Daisy," the light had seemed "almost touching her," even "as close as a star to the moon." I think that comparison works against itself, because a star only looks close to the moon from Earth. Now that Gatsby is near Daisy, "it was again a green light on a dock." The line "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one" makes it sound like he keeps a tally of things he has made magical. If that's right, the magic came from Gatsby and not from the light, and it ran out once he didn't need the light to stand in for Daisy.

By Chapter 9 the light isn't Daisy's anymore: "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." Nobody "believes in" a light on a dock. You believe in things you can't prove yet, and the light has now become the future, which is always ahead. The word "us" puts the narrator and the reader next to Gatsby, and "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther" brings back the outstretched arms from Chapter 1, except now everyone is reaching. I don't think the plan can work. If the future recedes "year by year," running faster won't close the gap, and the sentence breaks off before it arrives: "And one fine morning——." In Chapter 5 the light lost its power because Gatsby got close. In Chapter 9 nobody can get close, so it keeps its hold. I can't tell how the narrator feels about that. He admits "it eluded us then" and follows it with "that's no matter," which could be hope, or could mean he has fallen for the same light Gatsby did.
