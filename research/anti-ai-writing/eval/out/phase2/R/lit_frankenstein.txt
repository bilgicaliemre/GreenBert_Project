When the creature tells Victor, "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend," he builds the blame into the grammar. "Misery" is the subject of the sentence and he is the object, so he didn't simply turn into a fiend; something made him one. I think the novel mostly agrees with him, and the first thing it points to is Victor. On the night the creature wakes up, Victor feels "breathless horror and disgust," and, "unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created," he rushes out of the room. "Aspect" here means appearance. So Victor runs from the creature's looks before it has done anything. Later, on the glacier, the creature says, "I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." Adam is looked after by his maker and the fallen angel is thrown out, but the fallen angel at least rebelled first; the creature is driven out "for no misdeed."

After the De Lacey family rejects him, the creature compares himself to the devil. That night he says, "I, like the arch-fiend, bore a hell within me." The passage spells out why. Just before that line he notices that "All, save I, were at rest or in enjoyment," and the urge to "tear up the trees, spread havoc and destruction around me" comes only after "finding myself unsympathised with." Even then it's a wish: he "wished" to do it and then "to have sat down and enjoyed the ruin." This fits the deal he offers Victor: "Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous." I wouldn't take that completely at face value, since he's pleading when he says it, but the De Lacey night backs him up: the only cause that passage gives for his rage is being shut out while everyone else rests.

The novel doesn't let him off, though. Standing over Victor's body at the end, he tells Walton about "the frightful catalogue of my sins." "Sins" is his own word, and it's a word for things you did yourself. He can't believe he is "the same creature whose thoughts were once filled with sublime and transcendent visions of the beauty and the majesty of goodness," and he sums it up: "the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil." Then, mid-confession, he goes straight back to the cause he gave Victor: "even that enemy of God and man had friends and associates in his desolation; I am alone." So my answer is that the novel splits the blame, but not evenly. The creature did the things on that catalogue. The misery behind them starts with Victor and gets worse with the De Laceys. What convinces me most is that the part about Victor doesn't depend on the creature's word: Victor himself tells us he couldn't stand the sight of what he'd made and ran from it.
