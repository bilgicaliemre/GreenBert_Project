At the table at her 70th birthday, I told my äje, my grandmother, that her apple tree was "genetically boring." My uncle laughed. Äje didn't. Her tree is an Aport, the famous Almaty apple. I had just learned that apples don't grow true from seed, so every Aport is technically the same tree, cloned by grafting for about 150 years.

Two weeks later, while she was in town, I sawed a big branch off that tree at her dacha in Kamenka and grafted on wild Malus sieversii, the ancestor of basically every apple. I didn't ask her. I'd cut the sticks inside the national park, which isn't allowed, and carried them two days in the heat. They had leaves when they should have been dormant, so they dried out, the graft died, and the cut went black.

Dad was at Tengiz, the oil field, so I told him on a video call, like it was a science update. He didn't say anything. When he got back, he drove me to the dacha and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound with garden pitch. She said something fast in Kazakh. I got half and asked Dad in the car. "Ағаш дәптер емес." A tree is not a notebook.

The grafting I can explain as a science mistake. The birthday has no science excuse: I wanted the table to know I knew something. I told nobody that I was kind of glad when the graft died. If it had taken, her tree would have a wild branch forever, basically my signature, and she'd look at it every summer.

After that, Gennady Pavlovich, an 81-year-old apple seller at the Green Bazaar, taught me grafting properly: 200 cuts on willow sticks before he let me touch an apple.

Of the 37 wild trees I have tagged in the foothills, Tree 23 was the best: 15.2 Brix (percent sugar in the juice). I took a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds and said, "This one has been to the city." It grows 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably a hybrid with some dacha apple. I wanted to argue, as if the 15.2 in my notebook proved where a tree comes from. On the bus home I ate three, they were really good, and I was angry at her and also at the apples, which makes no sense.

I think it bothered me because of "shala-kazakh." Äje calls me that as a joke, half-Kazakh, because I answer her in Russian. Everybody laughs, me too. Tree 23 is a city one, half one thing and half the other, not the real wild thing, and I got defensive for a tree. With the Aport I did the opposite: I called her tree boring for being a copy and tried to put the wild thing into it.

Every fourteen days, when Dad leaves for Tengiz or comes home, our apartment turns into the other house, always the same two. I like that, though I called the Aport boring for exactly that. At 21:00 every evening he sends a photo from Tengiz, the same flare stack and sunset, 340-something so far, with no words. That's the most Aport thing in my life. Or I'm making that up because it sounds good. Gennady Pavlovich says if a graft looks too neat, you didn't cut deep enough.

Zhanna Serikovna said some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. I want to be the person who decides which trees get saved, and I don't know if I'd cut Tree 23. Probably the correct answer is yes. Four years in America won't make my Kazakh better, only worse, and äje is 70. I haven't said that to my parents, and I've never asked Dad why the photo comes with no words.
