Tree 23, one of 37 wild apple trees I've tagged in the foothills past Talgar, was my best: 15.2 Brix, when wild ones run about 9 to 15. I took a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds, and said, "This one has been to the city." It grows 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably a hybrid with some dacha apple.

I wanted to argue. I had 15.2 in my notebook, as if a sugar reading proves where a tree comes from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good, and I was angry at her and at the apples, which makes no sense.

My best wild apple wasn't wild. Malus sieversii, the wild ancestor of basically every apple, just grows here. The wild forests are shrinking, though I don't fully trust the "70 percent gone since the 1960s" line every website copies. Zhanna Serikovna said some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees.

My grandmother calls me shala-kazakh, half-Kazakh, because I answer her in Russian, and I think that's why Tree 23 bothered me: half one thing, half the other, not the real wild thing. If it were my decision, I don't know if I'd cut it, though the correct answer is probably yes.

I want to study plant or conservation genetics and be the person who decides which trees get saved. I don't know what that job is called.
