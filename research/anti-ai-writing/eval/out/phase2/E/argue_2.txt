The Case for Retakes

A low score on a major test can mean a student misread the directions on the second page, had a rough night at home, or had not learned the material yet when the test came. Whatever the cause, the question is whether that one score should be final. I believe students should be allowed to retake major tests to raise their grade, as long as the retake comes with real conditions attached. A well-designed retake policy rewards learning, which is the point of school.

A grade in chemistry should say how well a student understands chemistry, not how well they understood it on one particular Tuesday in October. If a student fails a unit test on stoichiometry, then studies, goes to tutoring, and later shows full command of the topic, the original grade is no longer accurate. Leaving it on the report card means the grade describes what the student knew in October, and a retake updates it to what they know now.

Retakes also change what students do with their mistakes. Under a one-shot system a bad test is a dead end: most students glance at the score and shove the paper into a backpack, since there is no reason to study material that has already been graded. When a retake is possible, the same test becomes a list of what to fix. The student has a reason to go back through each wrong answer and ask the teacher about the ones they still do not understand. In a class like math, where each unit builds on the last, that extra work can decide whether a student keeps up or falls behind for the rest of the semester.

The main objection is that retakes encourage laziness: if students know they can try again, they will not bother preparing the first time. That concern is fair, but it is an argument against a badly designed retake policy, not against retakes. Teachers can require test corrections or a review session before a student is allowed to retest. They can cap the retake score, average it with the original, or limit retakes to one or two per semester. Under rules like these, a student who skips studying the first time only adds work: corrections or a review session, then a second test that may not count in full.

Another objection is that the real world does not give second chances, so school should not either. But adult life is full of second attempts. People retake driving tests and professional licensing exams, writers revise their drafts, and an athlete who loses one game plays another the next week. Adults are expected to learn from a failure and do better the next time, and retakes build that habit, while a system that punishes one bad day forever teaches students that a single performance defines them.

Retakes do cost teachers time, since someone has to write a new version of each test and find time to give it. Shared question banks and scheduled retake days can reduce that work, though not remove it. Still, losing students who give up after one failure costs more, especially in math, where a missed unit makes every later unit harder.

Learning rarely happens on a perfect schedule. Conditions such as a new version of the test, corrections finished first and a cap or average on the second score keep the standard where it was. Without them, the laziness objection would hold. With them, a grade that rewards eventual mastery is more honest than one that freezes a student at their worst moment.
