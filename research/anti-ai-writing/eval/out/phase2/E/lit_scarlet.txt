The Letter That Outgrew Its Meaning

When Hester Prynne first stands on the scaffold, the scarlet letter cuts her off from everyone who knows her. Hawthorne writes that it "had the effect of a spell, taking her out of the ordinary relations with humanity, and enclosing her in a sphere by herself." The isolation is immediate: people who had been "familiarly acquainted with Hester Prynne" now look at her "as if they beheld her for the first time." The letter has "transfigured the wearer," so that her neighbors see the symbol before they see the woman. Yet Hawthorne's description of the letter itself pulls the other way. It is "so fantastically embroidered and illuminated upon her bosom" that it draws every eye, and "embroidered" and "illuminated" are words for decoration and art, not for punishment. Even at the moment of its harshest meaning, the A is already more than the authorities intended.

Over the following years, the letter's meaning shifts because the town's reading of it shifts. In "Another View of Hester," Hawthorne reports that "many people refused to interpret the scarlet A by its original signification. They said that it meant Able." The cause is Hester's own conduct: in her there is "so much power to do, and power to sympathize." The cloth has not changed, only the town's interpretation, which suggests that the meaning was never stitched into the fabric in the first place. Still, the town's new reading does not erase what the letter costs Hester privately. When she unclasps it in the forest, "the burden of shame and anguish departed from her spirit," and the narrator exclaims, "She had not known the weight, until she felt the freedom!" The town may read the A as Able, but Hester has been carrying it as a burden all along, one so constant that she feels its weight only once it is gone.

The final change comes in the "Conclusion," when Hester returns to Boston and puts the letter back on "of her own free will, for not the sternest magistrate of that iron period would have imposed it." The A was once a sentence handed down to her; now it is her choice. Over her "toilsome, thoughtful, and self-devoted years," it "ceased to be a stigma which attracted the world's scorn and bitterness, and became a type of something to be sorrowed over, and looked upon with awe, yet with reverence too." Sorrow is still first in that list, ahead of awe and reverence, so the letter does not turn into a simple badge of honor. Its meaning holds both the old shame and the long, devoted life that followed it. The magistrates could order Hester to wear the letter, but they could not fix what it would finally mean; that came from the years she spent wearing it and from the way other people learned to see it.
