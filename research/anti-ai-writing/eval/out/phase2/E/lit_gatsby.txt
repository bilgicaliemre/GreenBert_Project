The Light That Only Works at a Distance

The green light in The Great Gatsby takes on three meanings (an unexplained object, then Daisy, then the future itself), and in every version it matters only while it is out of reach. Fitzgerald shows the gesture before the meaning. In Chapter 1 the narrator watches Gatsby from a distance as "he stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way," and he "could have sworn he was trembling." Looking for what Gatsby is reaching toward, he finds "nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." The scene depends on a mismatch. Gatsby's longing is enormous and physical, while its object is tiny and so vague that the narrator can only guess it "might have been" a dock. At this point the light means whatever Gatsby needs it to mean, and the reader, like the narrator, sees the reaching without knowing what it is for.

Chapter 5 supplies the answer and then takes it away. Gatsby tells Daisy, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock," so the light is on her dock, and he has spent his nights watching it. Once Daisy is with him, though, the narrator suspects that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever." From across the water, the light "had seemed very near to her, almost touching her," even "as close as a star to the moon." The comparison gives the game away, since a star only looks close to the moon; the closeness was an illusion produced by distance. With Daisy present, "it was again a green light on a dock." The line "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one" suggests that the light's power never belonged to the light. Gatsby enchanted it himself, and getting closer to what he wanted broke the spell.

In the novel's final pages, the light grows from Gatsby's private symbol into a shared one: "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." The light no longer stands for Daisy's dock but for the future, and the word "us" pulls the narrator and the reader into Gatsby's position. The next promise, "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther," repeats the outstretched arms from Chapter 1, now made by "we" instead of Gatsby alone. The sentence after that, "And one fine morning——," breaks off before it has a verb. Gatsby's dream survives only as long as it is never tested, and Chapter 5 was the test: Daisy could be reached, and reaching her ended the light's "colossal significance." A future that "year by year recedes" can never be reached, so it never shrinks back into a green light on a dock, which is why the narrator can admit "It eluded us then" and still call that "no matter."
