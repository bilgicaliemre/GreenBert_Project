Last month I answered the phone at my dad's shop with its name. It took three tries, and on the second I almost hung up. When "Carvalho Shoe Service" finally came out, I didn't feel proud, just kind of embarrassed. The best part was that it was nothing to him: he had waited, then asked if we fix purses, which we don't.

The shop has been on Pleasant Street since 1994. I work the counter Saturdays, and since last summer I'm allowed to do heels and half-soles. My hoodie smells like Barge cement; my friends say I smell like a cowboy's basement. I stutter, and it gets worse on the phone, especially on hard sounds like "C," which is a problem when the shop is called Carvalho. So I used to answer, "Hi, shoe repair," and my dad noticed after about a year.

I can tell how someone walks from the wear on their heels. One woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left, so I said out loud that she must drive for work, since that heel rests by the pedal. She did, and looked at me like I was a psychic. My notebook of heels, which starts with my dad's work boots, has 61 entries.

On Tuesdays and Thursdays I do flashcards with my avó, Rosa. Since her stroke two years ago she has aphasia; she understands more than she can say. I made her picture cards in Portuguese, and she's relearning words I'm learning for the first time. She's better at "agulha," needle, and I'm better at "telemóvel." Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, my aunt who died before I was born. I used to correct her, but now I don't. She can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato."

Reading heels and helping avó both mean figuring someone out from the outside, when they can't tell you. With heels it's easy. When avó gets a word wrong, I can't tell what she's thinking. Maybe the connection is just one I want, the way I wanted stuttering and aphasia to be connected, and they aren't.

I want to study speech-language pathology, or how the brain gets words back. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my middle school speech therapist, whether I'd be "allowed" to do her job with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter, and I don't fully believe her yet.

Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept it up for three weeks and forged her signature on a progress report, after practicing it about eight times on a math worksheet. I'd rather leave that part out, because it means I planned it. When Mr. Oduya emailed home, I was relieved; keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting. My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test, question by question, in Portuguese. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed.

Since then I've kept a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even "I'm almost there." It has 212 items, mostly about being on time, and I'm still not sure if it makes me more honest or just better at noticing. "Hi, shoe repair" isn't on it. I think it was a lie too, a small one, and I only noticed recently. It's what I did with the test: swapping the true thing for an easier thing. I don't know yet what to do about that, besides maybe adding it.

My dad never said anything about "Hi, shoe repair." I asked him once if he wanted me to take over the shop, and he said, "The shop isn't a person, it doesn't need anyone." I still don't know if that was a yes or a no.
