The Making of a Fiend

When the creature tells Victor, "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend," he offers a full theory of his own corruption in a single sentence. In the second clause, "misery" is the subject of the verb and the creature is its object. He did not become a fiend; he was made one. The novel supports the claim, because the first person to inflict that misery is Victor himself. On the night the creature wakes, Victor feels "breathless horror and disgust" and, "unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created," runs from the room. "Aspect" means appearance, so Victor rejects his creation before it has done anything at all, judging it only by how it looks. The creature names this injustice: "I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." Adam is the creature a maker cherishes; the fallen angel is the one he casts out. The creature has been given the outcast's punishment without the outcast's crime.

The rejection by the De Lacey family turns that injury into rage. The night afterward, the creature says, "I, like the arch-fiend, bore a hell within me." He no longer compares himself to a fallen angel but to the arch-fiend himself, and the hell is now inside him. Shelley is careful about what causes it, though. His destructive urge arrives only after "finding myself unsympathised with," and even then he "wished" to "spread havoc and destruction" and "enjoyed the ruin" only in imagination. That night the violence is still a fantasy, and it grows out of watching everyone else "at rest or in enjoyment" while he is shut out. The bargain he offers Victor rests on the same logic: "Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous." On his own account, his goodness depends on company: it left when he was shut out, and it would return if he were let in.

Still, the novel does not let him off entirely. Speaking to Walton over Victor's body, the creature calls his acts a "frightful catalogue of my sins." "Sins" is a word of moral ownership. In "misery made me a fiend," misery was the subject of the sentence; here the acts belong to him. He admits that "the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil," and "becomes" describes a change in him rather than a nature he was born with. Yet the same speech returns to the cause he named to Victor. Even Satan "had friends and associates in his desolation; I am alone." The creature measures himself against the worst figure he knows and finds himself worse off in the one respect he names: Satan had companions, and he has none. Shelley holds the creature accountable for what he did, but she places the root of the change in Victor's horror at his appearance and in a world that refused him sympathy.
