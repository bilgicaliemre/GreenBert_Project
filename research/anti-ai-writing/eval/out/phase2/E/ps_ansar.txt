At my grandmother's seventieth birthday, I told her that her apple tree was genetically boring.

I'd just learned that apples don't grow true from seed, so every Aport, Almaty's famous apple, is technically one tree, cloned by grafting for about 150 years. My uncle laughed. My äje didn't. I had said it to sound clever in front of my cousins.

Two weeks later, while she was in town, I sawed a big branch off her tree and grafted on wild Malus sieversii, the ancestor of almost every apple, which grows in the foothills where I've tagged 37 trees. I didn't ask her. I'd cut the scions inside the national park, which isn't allowed, and carried them two days in the heat. It was the wrong season: mine had leaves instead of being dormant, and they dried out, so the graft died and the cut went black.

My dad was on his fourteen-day shift at the Tengiz oil field, so I told him on a video call, like a science update. He said nothing. I also told him I'd cut the scions "at the edge of the park." They came from pretty far inside. When he got home, he drove me to her dacha in Kamenka and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound with garden pitch. She said something fast in Kazakh; I caught half and asked Dad in the car. "Ағаш дәптер емес." A tree is not a notebook.

The grafting I can explain as a science mistake. The birthday has no science excuse. I didn't tell anyone I was kind of glad the graft died. If it had taken, my signature would be on her tree forever, and she'd see it every summer.

Afterward Gennady Pavlovich, an 81-year-old apple seller at the Green Bazaar, taught me grafting properly: 200 cuts on willow sticks before he let me touch an apple.

My best wild tree, Tree 23, measured 15.2 Brix (percent sugar); wild ones run from about 9 to 15. I took a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds, and said, "This one has been to the city." Tree 23 grows 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably crossed with someone's dacha apple. I wanted to argue, as if the number in my notebook proved where a tree came from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good, and I was angry at her and at the apples, which made no sense until I thought about my äje's joke.

She calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, because I answer her in Russian. Everyone laughs, me too. Tree 23 is a city one, half one thing and half the other, not the real wild thing, and I got defensive for a tree. With the Aport I did the reverse: I called her tree boring for being a copy and tried to put the wild thing into it. I don't know what that says about me, maybe nothing.

Zhanna Serikovna said some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. I want to study conservation genetics and decide which trees get saved, and I don't know if I'd cut Tree 23, though I probably should. If I spend four years in America, my Kazakh gets worse, and äje is 70, which I haven't said to my parents.

Every evening at 21:00 my dad sends the same photo from Tengiz, a flare stack at sunset, no words. There are 340-something, and I've never asked why. I like that dad weeks and not-dad weeks repeat, always the same two, though I called the Aport boring for that. Those photos are the most Aport thing in my life. I could be making that up because it sounds good. Gennady Pavlovich says if a graft looks too neat, you didn't cut deep enough.
