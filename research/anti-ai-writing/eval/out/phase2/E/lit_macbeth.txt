The Stain That Changes Hands

In Macbeth, blood on the hands stands for guilt, and especially the question of whether guilt can ever be removed. Right after murdering Duncan, Macbeth answers that question with horror: "Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine, / Making the green one red." The image reverses the normal order of cleaning. Instead of the ocean purifying his hand, his hand pollutes the ocean, as if one murder could stain the whole world. Lady Macbeth, returning from smearing the grooms, sees the same blood as ordinary dirt. "My hands are of your color," she tells him, "but I shame / To wear a heart so white." She flips the moral meaning of color: red hands are nothing to be ashamed of, while a white, fearful heart is. Her solution is almost cheerful: "A little water clears us of this deed: / How easy is it then!" Macbeth's "great Neptune’s ocean" shrinks in her mouth to "a little water," and that gap in scale measures the distance between a man who feels his guilt and a woman who refuses to.

By the banquet in Act III, Macbeth has moved toward his wife's old position. Shaken by Banquo's ghost, he speaks of wading instead of washing: "I am in blood / Stepp’d in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o’er." The blood that stained one hand is now a river he stands in, and he reasons that going back would be no easier than pushing across. The word "tedious" is chilling because it is so flat. Murder has become a matter of effort and convenience, justified by "For mine own good, / All causes shall give way." He is no cleaner than before; he has decided that being clean no longer matters, a harder version of the practical attitude Lady Macbeth showed in Act II.

Lady Macbeth, meanwhile, ends up where her husband began. Sleepwalking in Act V while the Doctor and Gentlewoman watch, she says, "Here’s the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand. Oh, oh, oh!" The sentence repeats the pattern of Macbeth's first speech: an enormous resource, the ocean then and the perfumes of Arabia now, fails to clean a single hand. The "little water" that once cleared them has become useless, and the blood has moved from something seen to something smelled, a sense that cannot be shut off. Her speech, no longer in verse, collapses into "Oh, oh, oh!" The two characters have traded places: the one who said guilt could be rinsed away cannot rinse it, and the one who feared it would dye the seas has chosen to wade on. Neither has got clean, though: by Act V the play has confirmed Macbeth's first answer, that this blood does not wash off.
