Ban phones from the first bell to the last

A phone buzzes somewhere in the room, and half the class reaches for their pockets to check whether it was theirs. The teacher pauses, the explanation loses its thread, and by the time everyone settles back in, a few people are scrolling under their desks. High schools should ban student phones for the whole school day, from the first bell to the last. Phones pull attention away from lessons and from the people in the room, and it is unfair to ask teenagers to spend the day resisting apps that are built to be hard to put down.

The most obvious problem is learning. Understanding something difficult, like balancing a chemistry equation or working through a dense passage of Shakespeare, takes sustained attention. Even a silent phone breaks that attention, because part of a student's mind keeps wondering what they might be missing. When a student checks a notification for "just a second," they have to rebuild their focus afterward, and over a full class period those small interruptions eat into the lesson. Teachers lose time too, policing phones instead of teaching, which hurts even the students who follow the rules.

Phones also hurt the social life of a school. Walk into a cafeteria where phones are allowed and you will often see tables of students sitting together but staring at separate screens. Lunch and passing periods should be when students talk, joke, argue, and get to know people outside their usual circle; those unstructured minutes are where friendships start, especially for students who are new or shy. Phones bring online drama into the building as well. A cruel group chat or an embarrassing video can spread through an entire grade before the next period begins. Taking phones away during the day will not end online conflict, but it gives students several hours of relief from it.

Instead of a ban, a school could try teaching students to use their phones responsibly. The trouble is that this asks teenagers to win a fight that many adults lose. Apps are built to keep people scrolling, and expecting a sixteen-year-old to ignore them during a slow lecture is like putting a plate of cookies on every desk and grading students on not eating them. A clear rule takes the decision out of the moment. If nobody has a phone, nobody is left out of the conversation happening online, and no student has to be the only one who puts theirs away.

The strongest objection is safety. Parents understandably want to reach their children in an emergency. But schools already have phones in their offices and ways to contact families quickly, and in a real crisis, hundreds of students calling and texting at once could spread panic and rumors and drown out instructions from the adults in charge. Academic uses such as calculators or research can be covered by laptops or tablets built for schoolwork. Students with medical needs, such as those who use a phone to monitor a health condition, should of course get exceptions.

A ban is a decision about what the school day is for, and it has a cost: for those hours, students cannot text their parents or their friends. In return, their attention belongs to the lesson in front of them and the people beside them. Their phones will still be waiting at the final bell. The chance to focus, and to know the classmates around them, might not be.
