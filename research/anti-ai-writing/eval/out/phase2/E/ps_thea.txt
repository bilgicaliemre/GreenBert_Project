Minus One

I don't lose track of much. Since June 2024 I have played the organ at 48 funerals, for Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, Nebraska, and for the funeral home when it calls, and I have never once been to one as just a guest. I play the pedals in socks, because my shoes slip on the 1958 Wicks.

In February of my junior year I had counted 31 funerals and 6 baptisms at Immanuel. I put them in Google Sheets, added a trend line, and posted the chart in the Cedar County Community Corner Facebook group with a skull emoji: "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!"

It got 63 comments. Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, wrote, "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I answered "anecdotes aren't data," which I'd learned from a YouTube video that week. Then a man from Holy Trinity pointed out that the Catholics do most of the baptisms in town and Immanuel is basically the old people's church. I had only counted my church. My whole post was an anecdote.

Before I deleted it, four hours in, I screenshotted Kathy's comment and sent it to the quiz bowl group chat to make fun of her. My mom is Kathy's rural mail carrier, so she made me write an apology and put a real stamp on it so it was legal, then delivered it with the mail.

I didn't tell anyone that I liked the 63 comments, especially the mad ones, which is bad, I know. I kept refreshing the post in Spanish class. It was the first time anything I'd made got read by more people than Arlene, my piano teacher.

I went on the Census website to prove I was right and ended up reading about "natural decrease," when a county has more deaths than births. It turned out to be a whole field, demography, a word I hadn't known. My school doesn't offer statistics, so I take it as dual credit through Northeast Community College on Zoom.

At a funeral, a death is Leonard Tietz. At his, a pipe stuck on (it's called a cipher), and one low note kept going through the whole Lord's Prayer until I ran behind the curtain and shut off the blower. Pastor Jill said, "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word," and everyone laughed, even his wife. I was kind of mad. I think I wanted it to be my fault so I could fix it. After the lunch, Duane Pinkelman, the funeral director, held the ladder while I pulled the pipe, a low F-sharp with a dead wasp in it.

In the census, Leonard is minus one.

Duane says every single one was somebody's whole world. I can't decide whether I like the math because it makes death bigger, all the counties at once, or because it makes it smaller and easier. Sometimes I think the spreadsheet is there so I don't have to feel 48 separate things.

I think I wanted the town to be dying, too. If it's dying anyway, then leaving isn't really leaving anybody. The 2070 prediction was a permission slip I wrote myself with a trend line. The county probably is shrinking, just not like that and not by my math, and I still don't know if I'm coming back after college.

Arlene was Immanuel's organist for 51 years. Now she's 86 and listens to the Sunday livestream from assisted living with the volume all the way up, then texts me in all caps: "TOO FAST ON DOXOLOGY. BREATHE WITH THEM." I still don't know exactly what breathing with them means, but I think I'm close.

I don't know if Kathy ever read the note. She never said anything, and she waves at my mom the same as before.
