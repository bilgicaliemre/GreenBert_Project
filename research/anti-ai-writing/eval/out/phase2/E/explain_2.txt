Why does Earth have seasons?

Ask a group of people why summer is hot and winter is cold, and a lot of them will say it's because Earth is closer to the Sun in summer. It sounds reasonable (moving closer to a campfire makes you warmer, after all), but it's wrong.

Earth's orbit around the Sun isn't a perfect circle, but it's very close to one, so our distance from the Sun changes by only about 3 percent over the year. And Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during the Northern Hemisphere's winter.

The distance idea has a bigger problem. When it's summer in the United States, it's winter in Australia. If distance caused the seasons, the whole planet would have summer at once.

The real cause is the tilt of Earth's axis, the imaginary line through the North and South Poles that the planet spins around. Compared with Earth's path around the Sun, the axis leans about 23.5 degrees away from straight up and down, and it keeps pointing in the same direction in space all year. So for part of the year the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun, and for the other part it leans away. The Southern Hemisphere always gets the opposite arrangement, which is why Australia has its summer while the United States has winter.

Leaning toward the Sun does two things. First, sunlight hits the ground more directly. Shine a flashlight straight down at a table, then tilt it: the straight-down beam makes a small, bright spot, and the tilted beam spreads the same light over a larger, dimmer patch. Direct sunlight is like the straight-down beam. It concentrates energy on a smaller area, so the ground heats up more.

Second, the hemisphere tilted toward the Sun gets longer days. More hours of daylight mean more time to soak up heat and fewer hours of darkness to cool off. Direct light plus long days is summer. Half a year later, the same hemisphere leans away and gets slanted light and short days, which is winter.

The solstices, around June 21 and December 21, are the points when one hemisphere is tilted most directly toward the Sun. The equinoxes, in March and September, are the in-between points when neither hemisphere leans toward the Sun, and day and night are roughly equal in length everywhere.

Places near the equator get fairly direct sunlight all year, which is why they don't see the big temperature swings between summer and winter that places farther north or south do.
