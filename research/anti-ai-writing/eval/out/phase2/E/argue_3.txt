One Project, Different Grades

The slideshow is due in two days, it is half finished, and one member of the group has not answered a text since the assignment was handed out. Someone, usually the student who cares most about their grade, stays up late finishing that person's slides. A week later, the whole group gets the same B-plus. Most students have seen some version of this. The student who did the extra work and the student who disappeared are treated as equals, and that is unfair. Group members should not automatically receive the same grade; teachers should grade each student at least partly on what that student actually contributed.

The strongest case for a shared grade is that it teaches teamwork. In the working world, the argument goes, a team succeeds or fails together, so students should learn to depend on one another and take responsibility for the group as a whole. Sharing a grade can push students to communicate, divide tasks, and check each other's work. But a shared grade does not teach those skills; it only rewards the result. One capable student can do everything while the others watch, the group earns an A, and the grade tells the teacher nothing about whether anyone learned to cooperate. Workplaces are also less forgiving than the argument suggests: a coworker who never does their job eventually faces consequences, while a group member who disappears faces none when everyone gets the same grade.

Report card grades are supposed to describe what one student knows and can do. If a student who wrote nothing receives the same score as the student who wrote the whole paper, the grade stops describing either of them. It inflates one and hides the effort of the other. Over a semester with several group projects, those distortions add up, and the final grade becomes partly a measure of luck: who you were grouped with.

Identical grades also create bad incentives. Students are practical about effort. If the grade will be the same no matter what, the easy move for a student with a busy week is to let the most anxious group member pick up the slack. The busy student learns that someone will cover for them, and the hardworking one learns that doing more than your share is expected and unrewarded. Neither lesson is the one a teacher wants to teach. Over time, students start to dread group work, which defeats the purpose of assigning it.

The fairer option is to keep part of the grade shared and make the rest individual. The shared part could come from the final product. The individual part could come from evidence of what each person did: a short reflection, a confidential peer evaluation, or a quick check-in where the teacher asks each member to explain a section of the work. These methods are not perfect. Peer evaluations can be swayed by friendships, and reflections can be exaggerated. Still, they give the teacher more information than a single score does, and they give students a reason to do their part.

Group projects are still worth assigning. Planning and dividing work with other people and getting through disagreements are real skills. But the grade at the end should recognize the people who did the work, not hide them. When a teacher gives every member of a group the same grade no matter what, the student who stayed up finishing someone else's slides and the student who never answered the group's texts both learn that individual effort does not matter, and many students act on that lesson.
