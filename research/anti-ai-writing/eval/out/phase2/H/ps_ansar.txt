At my grandmother's seventieth birthday, I told her that her apple tree was genetically boring.

I had just learned that apples don't grow true from seed, so every Aport, the famous Almaty apple, is technically the same tree, cloned by grafting for about 150 years. My äje's tree in Kamenka was one more copy. I said it to sound clever in front of my cousins. My uncle laughed. My äje didn't.

Two weeks later I sawed a big branch off her tree and grafted on wild Malus sieversii, the ancestor of almost every apple, which grows in the foothills where I've tagged 37 trees. I cut the scions inside the national park, which isn't allowed, and carried them for two days in the heat. They should have been dormant. Mine had leaves and dried out. The graft died and the cut went black. I hadn't asked her.

Dad works fourteen-day shifts at the Tengiz oil field, so I told him by video call, like a science update. He didn't say anything. I also told him I'd cut the sticks "at the edge of the park." They came from pretty far inside. When he got home he drove me to the dacha and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound with garden pitch. She said something fast in Kazakh. I caught half and asked Dad in the car. "Ағаш дәптер емес." A tree is not a notebook.

The grafting I can explain as a science mistake. There's no science excuse for the birthday; I wanted the table to know I knew something. I never told anyone I was a little glad the graft died. If it had taken, her tree would carry my signature forever, and she'd see it every summer.

Afterward Gennady Pavlovich, an 81-year-old apple seller at the Green Bazaar, taught me to graft properly: 200 cuts on willow sticks before he let me touch an apple.

My best wild tree, Tree 23, measured 15.2 Brix. I took a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds and said, "This one has been to the city." The tree is 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably a hybrid with someone's dacha apple. I wanted to argue, as if 15.2 in my notebook proved where a tree came from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good. I was angry at her and also at the apples.

My äje jokingly calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, because I answer her in Russian. Everybody laughs, me too. Tree 23 is half one thing and half the other, and I think that's why I got defensive for it. (That's a bit much, but I think it's true.) With the Aport I did the opposite: I called her tree boring for being a copy and tried to put the wild thing into it. I don't know what that says about me.

Zhanna Serikovna said some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. I want to study conservation genetics and decide which trees get saved, and I don't know if I'd cut Tree 23. Probably the right answer is yes. Then there's my Kazakh. Four years in America won't improve it, and äje is 70. I haven't said that to my parents.

Every fourteen days our apartment turns into the other house and back, and I like that it's predictable, which is what I called the Aport boring for. Every evening at 21:00 Dad sends a photo from Tengiz, the same flare stack and sunset 340-something times, with no words. It's the most Aport thing in my life, though I could be making that up because it sounds good. Gennady Pavlovich says if a graft looks too neat, you didn't cut deep enough. I still haven't asked Dad why he never writes anything.
