Worth the Shift

Every fall, some version of the same argument happens at kitchen tables. A sixteen-year-old wants to apply at the grocery store down the street, and a parent worries that homework will suffer. The worry is fair, but I think it usually points the wrong way. A part-time job during the school year, kept to a reasonable number of hours, teaches things a classroom can't, and most of those lessons end up helping students in school too.

Money is the obvious reason to work, though what you learn from earning it matters more than the amount. A teenager on an allowance mostly learns that money shows up. One who works Saturday shifts finds out that a pair of sneakers costs, say, eleven hours of bagging groceries, and after that the sneakers look different. That kind of math changes how a person shops and saves. People who have earned a paycheck tend to think harder before spending it because they know exactly what it took to get. A personal finance unit in a textbook can explain taxes, but a first pay stub with taxes already taken out makes the point much faster.

Work also asks for a kind of responsibility school rarely does. Miss an assignment and you usually get a late penalty and a second chance. Miss a shift and a coworker has to cover for you while the manager redoes the schedule. Teens figure out pretty quickly why it matters to show up on time, call ahead when they're sick, and finish a task instead of leaving half of it for the next person. When one person flakes, everyone else on the shift pays for it, and that's how a group of people learns to rely on each other.

At school, students spend most of the day with classmates their own age. Behind a counter they deal with tired, impatient customers, and they might share a shift with a coworker in his sixties who has been stocking shelves for decades. Staying calm while someone complains about a cold order, or asking a supervisor for help without feeling embarrassed, builds a confidence that carries over into group projects and college interviews, and later into whatever job comes next.

Critics argue that jobs take time away from studying and sleep, and that school should be a teenager's only job. They have a point about hours. A student working thirty hours a week will almost certainly see grades slip, and no paycheck is worth that. A shift or two on weekends, or a couple of weekday evenings, still leaves plenty of time for homework, though. A busy schedule can even push students to use their free time better. When you know you have two hours before work, you stop scrolling and start the essay, while a whole empty evening is easy to waste.

Others say teenagers should get to enjoy being young before adult responsibilities show up. A part-time job at seventeen is a pretty low-stakes place to make mistakes, like miscounting a cash drawer or forgetting to restock napkins, because those mistakes cost very little at that age. I would much rather learn how to handle a demanding boss at seventeen than at twenty-three, when rent depends on keeping the job.

High school is supposed to prepare students for adult life, and a modest part-time job does some of that preparing directly. Parents who worry about grades can set a limit on hours and still let their kid take the job.
