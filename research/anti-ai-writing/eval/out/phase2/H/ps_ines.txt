Hi, shoe repair

Last month I answered the phone at my dad's shop with its name. On the second try I almost hung up. "Carvalho Shoe Service" came out on the third, and I didn't feel proud, just embarrassed it took three. The man asked if we fix purses, like nothing had happened. We don't. The best part was that it was nothing to him.

The shop has been on Pleasant Street in Fall River since 1994. I work the counter on Saturdays, and since last summer I also do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. My hoodie smells like Barge cement; my friends say I smell like a cowboy's basement. I hate the phone. I stutter, and it gets worse on the phone and on hard sounds like "C," which is a problem when the shop is called Carvalho. So I used to answer, "Hi, shoe repair." My dad noticed after about a year and never said anything.

I can read how someone walks from the wear on their heels. One woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left, and I said out loud that she must drive for work, since that heel rests by the pedal. She did, and looked at me like I was a psychic. My notebook of heels has 61 entries. Entry 1 is my dad's work boots.

On Tuesdays and Thursdays I do flashcards with my avó, Rosa. Since her stroke two years ago she's had aphasia, so she understands more than she can say. I made her picture cards in Portuguese, and she's relearning words I'm learning for the first time. She's better at "agulha" (needle), and I'm better at "telemóvel" (cell phone). Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, my aunt who died before I was born. I've stopped correcting her. She can't say my name yet, though she can say "sapato."

With heels and with avó, I'm figuring out someone from the outside, when they can't tell me. Heels are easy. When avó gets a word wrong, I can't tell what she's thinking. I don't know if that connection is real or if I just want it to be.

Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept that up for three weeks and forged her signature on my progress report, after practicing it about eight times on a math worksheet. I'd rather leave that part out, because it means I planned it. When Mr. Oduya emailed home, I was relieved; keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting. My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test at the kitchen table, question by question, in Portuguese. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed.

Since then I've kept a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even "I'm almost there." It has 212 items, mostly about being on time. I'm still not sure if it makes me more honest or just better at noticing. I only noticed recently that one is missing: "Hi, shoe repair." It's what I did with the test, swapping the true thing for an easier one. Maybe it goes on the list.

I want to study speech-language pathology, or something about how the brain gets words back. Stuttering and aphasia turned out to be unrelated, though I wanted them connected. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my middle school speech therapist, whether I'd be "allowed" to do her job with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.

I once asked my dad if he wanted me to take over the shop. He said, "The shop isn't a person. It doesn't need anyone." I still don't know if that was a yes or a no.
