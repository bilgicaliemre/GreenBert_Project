The Making of a Fiend

When the creature tells Victor, "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend," he offers a full theory of his own corruption in a single sentence. In the second clause "misery" is the subject of the verb and the creature is its object, so in his account the change was done to him. The novel takes this claim seriously, because the first person to inflict that misery is Victor himself. On the night the creature wakes, Victor feels "breathless horror and disgust" and, "unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created," runs from the room. "Aspect" means appearance, so Victor rejects his creation for how it looks, before it has done anything at all. The creature names this injustice exactly: "I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." Adam is the creature a maker cherishes, and the fallen angel is the one he casts out. The phrase "for no misdeed" carries the complaint, since he gets the outcast's punishment without the outcast's crime.

The De Lacey family's rejection turns that injury into rage. The night afterward, the creature says, "I, like the arch-fiend, bore a hell within me." Here the comparison is to the arch-fiend, and the hell is now inside him. Shelley is careful about what causes it, though. His destructive urge comes only after "finding myself unsympathised with," and even then it stays a wish: he "wished" to "spread havoc and destruction" and only imagines that he would have "enjoyed the ruin." That fantasy grows directly out of watching everyone else "at rest or in enjoyment" while he is shut out, which supports the bargain he offers Victor: "Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous." For the creature, goodness needs company to survive.

Still, the novel does not let him off entirely. Speaking to Walton over Victor's body, the creature calls his acts a "frightful catalogue of my sins." "Sins" is a word of moral ownership. He admits that "the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil," and the verb "becomes" describes a gradual slide, so the devil is something he turned into over time. Yet the same speech returns to the cause he named to Victor. Even Satan "had friends and associates in his desolation; I am alone." He measures himself against the worst figure he knows, and the one way he finds himself worse off is that he is lonelier. Shelley holds the creature accountable for what he did, but she places the root of the change in Victor's horror at his appearance and in a world that refused him sympathy.
