Why Does Earth Have Seasons?

Ask a group of people why summer is hot and winter is cold, and a lot of them will say something like, "Because Earth is closer to the Sun in summer." It's an easy idea to believe, since stepping closer to a campfire makes you warmer. It just doesn't match what happens to Earth.

The distance misconception

Earth's orbit is a slightly stretched circle, so our distance from the Sun does change, but only by about 3 percent over the year. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, the middle of winter for everyone in the Northern Hemisphere.

The distance idea also can't explain why the two hemispheres have opposite seasons. When it's summer in the United States, it's winter in Australia. If distance were the cause, the whole planet would have summer at the same time.

What actually causes seasons: tilt

Earth spins on an axis, an imaginary line running through the North and South Poles. Compared with Earth's path around the Sun, that axis is tilted by about 23.5 degrees, and it keeps pointing the same direction in space all year. So for part of the year the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun, and for the rest of the year it leans away. The Southern Hemisphere always gets the opposite arrangement.

Leaning toward the Sun does two things. First, sunlight hits the ground more directly. You can see the effect with a flashlight: point it straight down at a table and you get a small, bright spot, then tilt it and the same light spreads over a larger, dimmer patch. Direct sunlight packs its energy into a smaller area, so the ground heats up more.

Second, the hemisphere tilted toward the Sun gets longer days. More hours of daylight mean more time to soak up heat, and fewer hours of darkness mean less time to cool off.

Direct light and long days give you summer. Slanted light and short days give you winter.

Solstices and equinoxes

The solstices, around June 21 and December 21, are the points when one hemisphere is tilted most directly toward the Sun. The equinoxes, in March and September, fall in between. At those points neither hemisphere leans toward the Sun, and day and night are roughly equal in length everywhere.

Places near the equator get fairly direct sunlight all year, so they don't see the big swings between summer and winter temperatures that places farther north or south do.
