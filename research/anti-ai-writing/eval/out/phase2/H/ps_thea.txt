Minus One

I don't lose track of much. Since June 2024 I have played the organ at 48 funerals, for Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, Nebraska, and for the funeral home when it calls, and I keep count. I play the pedals in socks because my shoes slip. I have never once been to a funeral as just a guest.

By February of junior year I had counted 31 funerals and 6 baptisms at Immanuel. I put the numbers in Google Sheets, added a trend line, and posted the chart to the Cedar County Community Corner group on Facebook: "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!" with a skull emoji.

It got 63 comments. Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, wrote, "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I replied "anecdotes aren't data," a line I'd learned from a YouTube video that same week, then screenshotted her comment for the quiz bowl group chat to make fun of her. A man from Holy Trinity pointed out that the Catholics do most of the baptisms in town and Immanuel is basically the old people's church. I had counted only my church, so my whole post was an anecdote.

I deleted it after four hours. My mom is the rural mail carrier and Kathy is on her route, so Mom made me write an apology, delivered it with the mail, and made me put a real stamp on it so it was legal.

I didn't tell anyone I liked it. I kept refreshing the post in Spanish class. It was the first time anything I'd made was read by more people than Arlene, my old piano teacher, and the mad comments were my favorite, which is bad, I know.

I went to the Census website to prove I'd been right and ended up reading about "natural decrease," when a county has more deaths than births. There was a whole field for this, demography, which I hadn't known was a word. My school doesn't offer statistics, so I take it for dual credit through Northeast Community College on Zoom.

At Leonard Tietz's funeral a pipe stuck on, called a cipher: one low note going and going through the whole Lord's Prayer. I ran behind the curtain and shut off the blower, and Pastor Jill said, "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word." Everyone laughed, even his wife. I was kind of mad, which doesn't make sense. I think I wanted it to be my fault so I could fix it. After the lunch Duane Pinkelman, the funeral director, held the ladder while I climbed up and pulled the pipe. It was low F-sharp, with a dead wasp inside.

In the census, Leonard is minus one. Duane says every single one was somebody's whole world. I can't decide whether I like the math because it makes death bigger, all the counties at once, or because it makes it smaller and easier. Some days I think the spreadsheet is there so I don't have to feel 48 separate things.

I also think I wanted the town to be dying. If Hartington is finished anyway, then leaving isn't really leaving anybody. The 2070 prediction was a permission slip I wrote myself with a trend line, and I didn't know that until someone asked me. The county probably is shrinking, just not the way I said and not by my math.

Arlene played at Immanuel for 51 years. Now she's 86 and listens to the Sunday livestream from assisted living with the volume all the way up, then texts me in all caps: "TOO FAST ON DOXOLOGY. BREATHE WITH THEM." I still don't know exactly what that means. I think I'm close.

I don't know if Kathy ever read the note. She waves at my mom the same as before.
