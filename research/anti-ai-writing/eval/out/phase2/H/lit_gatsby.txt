The Light That Only Works at a Distance

When the green light first appears in Chapter 1 of The Great Gatsby, Fitzgerald shows the reader the gesture before the meaning. The narrator watches Gatsby alone at night as "he stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way," and he "could have sworn he was trembling." Looking out to see what Gatsby is reaching for, he finds "nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." The scene turns on a mismatch. Gatsby's longing is enormous, physical enough to make him shake, while its object is tiny and so vague that the narrator can only guess it "might have been" a dock. At this point the light means whatever Gatsby needs it to mean, and the reader, like the narrator, sees the reaching without knowing what it is for.

Chapter 5 supplies the answer and then takes it away. Gatsby tells Daisy, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock," which reveals that the light is at her house and that he has spent his nights watching it. Once Daisy is beside him, though, the narrator suspects that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever." From across the water, the light "had seemed very near to her, almost touching her," even "as close as a star to the moon." That comparison gives the game away, since a star only looks close to the moon; the closeness was an effect of distance. With Daisy actually there, "it was again a green light on a dock." The line "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one" suggests that the light's power never belonged to the light. Gatsby enchanted it himself, and getting closer to what he wanted broke the spell.

In the novel's final pages, the light grows from Gatsby's private symbol into a shared one: "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." What once marked Daisy's dock now stands for the future itself, and the word "us" pulls the narrator and the reader into Gatsby's position. The next promise, "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther," repeats the outstretched arms from Chapter 1, so the novel ends with the gesture it began with, now made by everyone. Over the course of the book the green light goes from a mystery to a woman to a hope defined by its distance, and in each version it matters only while it is out of reach. Gatsby's dream survives as long as it is never tested, and the last sentence of the novel never arrives either: "And one fine morning——" breaks off before it can finish.
