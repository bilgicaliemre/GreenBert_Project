The Letter That Outgrew Its Meaning

When Hester Prynne first stands on the scaffold, the scarlet letter cuts her off from everyone around her. Hawthorne writes that it "had the effect of a spell, taking her out of the ordinary relations with humanity, and enclosing her in a sphere by herself." The isolation is immediate. People who had been "familiarly acquainted with Hester Prynne" now look at her "as if they beheld her for the first time," because the letter has "transfigured the wearer" and her neighbors see the symbol before they see the woman. The same passage describes the A as "so fantastically embroidered and illuminated upon her bosom" that it draws every eye. "Embroidered" and "illuminated" are words for decoration and craft, and they make a mark of disgrace sound like a work of art. So even on the scaffold, the letter already means more than the punishment it was supposed to announce.

Years later, in "Another View of Hester," the town reads the letter differently. Hester has shown "so much power to do, and power to sympathize" that "many people refused to interpret the scarlet A by its original signification. They said that it meant Able." Her conduct changed what her neighbors saw in the same piece of cloth, which suggests the meaning had come from the people reading the letter all along. The new name did little for what Hester carried in private, though. When she unclasps the letter in the forest, "the burden of shame and anguish departed from her spirit," and the narrator exclaims, "She had not known the weight, until she felt the freedom!" The years when the town called her Able were also years of carrying a weight so constant that she noticed it only after taking the letter off.

The last change comes in the "Conclusion," and it is the largest. Hester returns to Boston and puts the letter back on "of her own free will, for not the sternest magistrate of that iron period would have imposed it." For the first time, wearing the A is her decision. Over her "toilsome, thoughtful, and self-devoted years," it "ceased to be a stigma which attracted the world's scorn and bitterness, and became a type of something to be sorrowed over, and looked upon with awe, yet with reverence too." Hawthorne keeps the sorrow in that sentence next to the awe and reverence, so the letter's final meaning holds the old shame together with the long, devoted life that followed it. The magistrates decided that Hester would wear the A, but what it came to mean depended on how she lived while wearing it.
