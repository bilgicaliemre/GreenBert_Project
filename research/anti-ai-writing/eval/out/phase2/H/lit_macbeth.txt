The Stain That Changes Hands

In Macbeth, blood on the hands stands for guilt, and especially for the question of whether guilt can ever be washed off. Right after murdering Duncan, Macbeth answers that question with horror: "Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine, / Making the green one red." The image runs cleaning backward. The ocean cannot purify his hand, and his hand will turn the ocean red, as if one murder could stain the whole world. Lady Macbeth, returning from smearing the grooms, treats the same blood as ordinary dirt. "My hands are of your color," she tells him, "but I shame / To wear a heart so white." She reverses the moral meaning of the colors, so that red hands are nothing to be ashamed of and a white, fearful heart is. Her solution sounds almost cheerful: "A little water clears us of this deed: / How easy is it then!" Macbeth's "great Neptune’s ocean" shrinks in her mouth to "a little water," and the difference in scale matches the difference between them. He feels his guilt, and she refuses to.

By the banquet in Act III, Macbeth has moved toward his wife's old position. Shaken by Banquo's ghost, he no longer talks about washing at all. He pictures the blood as something he is standing in: "I am in blood / Stepp’d in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o’er." The stain on one hand has spread into something like a river, and he reasons that going back would be no easier than pushing across. The word "tedious" is chilling because it is so flat. Murder has become a matter of effort and convenience, justified by "For mine own good, / All causes shall give way." Macbeth has found no way to get clean, and he has decided that being clean no longer matters. That is a harder version of the practical attitude Lady Macbeth showed in Act II.

Lady Macbeth, meanwhile, ends up where her husband began. Sleepwalking in Act V while the Doctor and Gentlewoman watch, she says, "Here’s the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand. Oh, oh, oh!" Her sentence has the same shape as Macbeth's first speech, with an enormous resource (the ocean then, the perfumes of Arabia now) failing to clean a single hand. The "little water" that once cleared them is no use to her anymore, and the blood has moved from something seen to something smelled, a sense she cannot shut off. Her speech, no longer in verse, breaks down into "Oh, oh, oh!" By the end the two have traded places: the one who said guilt could be rinsed away cannot rinse it, and the one who feared it would dye the seas has chosen to keep wading.
