One Project, Different Grades

Everyone who has done a group project knows this situation. The due date is two days away, the slideshow is half finished, and one member of the group has not answered a text since the assignment was handed out. Someone, usually the student who cares most about their grade, stays up late finishing that person's slides. A week later, the whole group gets the same B-plus. Group members should not automatically receive the same grade, because it treats the student who did the extra work and the student who disappeared as equals, and that is unfair. Teachers should grade each student at least partly on what that student actually contributed.

The best argument for a shared grade is that it teaches teamwork. In the working world, the argument goes, a team succeeds or fails together, so students should learn to depend on one another and take responsibility for the whole group. Having a shared stake might push students to split up tasks and check each other's work. The trouble is that a shared grade rewards the final product and says nothing about how the group got there. A group can earn an A because one capable student did everything while the others watched, and the grade tells the teacher nothing about whether anyone learned to cooperate. Workplaces are also less forgiving than this argument suggests. A coworker who never does their job eventually faces consequences, but in a class with a single group grade, that person faces none.

A grade on a report card is supposed to describe what one student knows and can do. If a student who wrote nothing receives the same score as the student who wrote the whole paper, the grade stops describing either of them, since it inflates one and hides the other's effort. Over a semester with several group projects, those distortions add up, and the final grade becomes partly a measure of luck, meaning who you happened to get grouped with.

Identical grades create bad incentives, too. Students are practical about effort. If the grade will be the same no matter what, the easy move for someone with a busy week is to let the most anxious group member pick up the slack. The hardworking student, meanwhile, figures out that doing more than your share is expected and goes unrewarded. After a few rounds of this, students start to dread group work, which defeats the purpose of assigning it.

A fair system can still keep part of the grade shared. Some of it could come from the final product, which everyone owns together. The rest could come from individual evidence, such as a short reflection on what each person did, a confidential peer evaluation, or a quick check-in where the teacher asks each member to explain a section of the work. None of these methods is perfect. Peer evaluations can be swayed by friendships, and people exaggerate in reflections. Even so, they give the teacher more information than a single score does, and they give students a reason to do their part.

Group projects are worth assigning, since planning with other people and working through disagreements are real skills. The grade at the end, though, should recognize the people who did the work. When a teacher gives every member of a group the same grade no matter what, students hear that individual effort does not matter, and many of them act on it.
