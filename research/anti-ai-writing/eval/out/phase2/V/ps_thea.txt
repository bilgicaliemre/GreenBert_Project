At Leonard Tietz's funeral, an organ pipe got stuck on. It's called a cipher: one low note going and going through the whole Lord's Prayer. My first thought was that everybody would think I did it. I ran behind the curtain and shut off the blower, and Pastor Jill said, "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word." Everybody laughed, even his wife. I was kind of mad. I think I wanted it to be my fault so I could fix it.

I'm the organist at Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, Nebraska, and the funeral home calls me too. I've played 48 funerals since June 2024; I keep track. Arlene Thoene, Immanuel's organist for 51 years, taught me pedals when her eyes got bad ("You have feet, don't you?"). Now she listens to the Sunday livestream from assisted living in Yankton and texts me afterward in all caps.

By February of junior year, my count at Immanuel was 31 funerals and 6 baptisms. I made a chart in Google Sheets with a trend line and posted it in the Cedar County Community Corner Facebook group: "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!" With a skull emoji. Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, commented, "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I replied "anecdotes aren't data," a phrase I'd picked up from a YouTube video that same week. Then a man from Holy Trinity pointed out that the Catholics do most of the town's baptisms and Immanuel is basically the old people's church. I had counted only my own church, so my whole post was one anecdote.

The post got 63 comments, and I didn't tell anyone I liked it. I kept refreshing it in Spanish class. It was the first time anything I made got read by more people than Arlene, and I liked the mad comments best, which is bad, I know. Before I deleted it, four hours in, I screenshotted Kathy's comment and sent it to the quiz bowl group chat to make fun of her.

My mom is the rural mail carrier and delivers to Kathy. She made me write an apology and put a real stamp on it so it was legal, then delivered it with the mail. Kathy never said anything. She waves at my mom the same as before, and I don't know if that means it worked.

I had gone to the Census website to prove I was right and ended up reading about "natural decrease," when a county has more deaths than births, which a lot of counties out here have. The county probably is shrinking, just not like that, and not by my math. There's a whole field for this, demography, a word I didn't know existed, and it's what I want to study, along with statistics.

I think I wanted the town to be dying. If it's dying anyway, then leaving isn't really leaving anybody. The 2070 line was me writing myself a permission slip with a trend line, and I still don't know if I'm coming back after college.

After the lunch at Leonard's funeral, Duane Pinkelman, the funeral director, held the ladder while I pulled the pipe. It was low F-sharp, and there was a dead wasp in it. At the funeral it's Leonard, his wife laughing, the wasp. In the census it's minus one. Duane says every single one was somebody's whole world. I don't know if I like the math because it makes it bigger, all the counties at once, or because it makes it smaller and easier. Sometimes I think the spreadsheet is so I don't have to feel 48 separate things.

Arlene's texts say things like "TOO FAST ON DOXOLOGY. BREATHE WITH THEM." I still don't know exactly what breathe with them means. I think I'm close.
