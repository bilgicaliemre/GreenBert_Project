At her seventieth birthday, at the table, I told my äje, my grandmother, that her apple tree was genetically boring. My uncle laughed. She didn't. It's an Aport, the famous Almaty apple. I'd just learned apples don't grow true from seed, so a variety is kept by grafting, and every Aport is technically the same tree, cloned for about 150 years.

Two weeks later, while she was in town, I sawed a big branch off that tree at her dacha (summer house) in Kamenka and grafted on Malus sieversii, the wild ancestor of almost every apple. I'd cut the sticks in the national park, which isn't allowed, and carried them two days in the heat. Grafting sticks should be dormant; mine had leaves and dried out. I didn't ask her. The graft died, and where I'd sawed, the cut went black.

Dad was at Tengiz, the oil field where he works fourteen days on, fourteen home. I told him on a video call, like it was a science update. I also said I'd cut the sticks "at the edge of the park." It was pretty far inside. He didn't say anything. I didn't tell anyone I was kind of glad it died. If it had taken, a wild branch would be on her tree forever, basically my signature, and she'd look at it every summer.

When Dad got home, he drove me to the dacha and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound with garden pitch. She said something fast in Kazakh, and I got half. I asked him in the car. "Ағаш дәптер емес." A tree is not a notebook.

The grafting I can explain as a science mistake. For "genetically boring" there's no science excuse. I said it to sound clever in front of my cousins; I wanted the table to know I knew something. Afterwards Gennady Pavlovich, an 81-year-old apple seller at the Green Bazaar, taught me to graft properly. He made me do 200 cuts on willow sticks before he let me touch an apple.

Of the 37 wild trees I've tagged, the best was Tree 23: 15.2 Brix, percent sugar in the juice. I took a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a botanical garden researcher. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds and said, "This one has been to the city." I wanted to argue. I had 15.2 in my notebook, as if that proves where a tree comes from. I ate three on the bus home. They were really good, and I was angry at her and also at the apples. Tree 23 is 30 meters from a picnic spot, likely a hybrid with some dacha apple.

Äje calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, because I answer her in Russian. Everybody laughs, me too. I think that's why Tree 23 bothered me. It's a city one, half one thing and half the other, not the real wild thing, and I got defensive for it. With the Aport I did the opposite: I called her tree boring for being a copy and tried to put the wild thing into it.

I don't know what that says about me, maybe nothing. It doesn't even fit my own life. Every evening at 21:00 Dad sends the same photo from Tengiz, a flare stack and the sunset, 340-something so far. It's the most Aport thing in my life. I could be making this up because it sounds good. Gennady Pavlovich says if a graft looks too neat, you didn't cut deep enough.

Zhanna Serikovna told me some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. If Tree 23 were my decision, I don't know if I'd cut it, though the correct answer is probably yes. I haven't told my parents this, but if I go to America for four years, my Kazakh gets worse, not better, and äje is seventy.
