In Macbeth, blood on the hands stands for guilt, and more exactly for whether a person can still feel it. Macbeth and Lady Macbeth swap attitudes toward that blood over the play, down to the words they use. Just after killing Duncan, Macbeth asks, "Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" His answer is no. His hand "will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine, / Making the green one red." He sets one hand against every ocean on earth, and the hand wins, because nothing can undo the murder. Lady Macbeth, back from smearing the grooms with blood, sees it the other way. "My hands are of your color," she tells him, "but I shame / To wear a heart so white." What shames her is his pale, frightened heart. When knocking starts at the south entry, she turns practical: "A little water clears us of this deed: / How easy is it then!" To her the blood is a mark on the skin, and the danger is someone at the door seeing it.

By the banquet in Act III, Macbeth has crossed to her side and kept going. After Banquo's ghost appears, he says, "For mine own good, / All causes shall give way: I am in blood / Stepp’d in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o’er." The blood has spread from his hand into something he wades through, like the oceans he pictured turning red in Act II. What's changed is his feeling about it, and "tedious" gives it away. Going back would be as tiresome as going on, so he goes on, and "mine own good" settles the rest. At Duncan's murder one bloody hand horrified him. Now he treats blood the way Lady Macbeth treated the water, as a practical question of what's easier.

Lady Macbeth ends up where he started. Sleepwalking in Act V while the Doctor and the Gentlewoman watch, she says, "Here’s the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand. Oh, oh, oh!" It's nearly Macbeth's ocean line again: something huge and far away, Neptune's ocean then and Arabia's perfumes now, can't clean one hand. Her own words come back changed. In Act II "a little water" was enough; now the hand is "little" and nothing is enough. "How easy is it then!" has become "Oh, oh, oh!", an exclamation with no words left in it. So they trade places, but I don't think the trade is even. She only says this in her sleep, so awake she was probably still forcing it down. Macbeth speaks his lines wide awake and calls wading in blood tedious. If the blood stands for guilt a person can still feel, she is the one who still feels it, and his ending is the worse one, even though hers is the one that sounds like pain.
