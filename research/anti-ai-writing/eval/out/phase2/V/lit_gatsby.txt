I think the green light in The Great Gatsby stands for distance more than it stands for Daisy. It means the most when Gatsby is far from her and almost nothing once he gets close. In Chapter 1, Gatsby "stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way," and when the narrator looks the same way he sees "nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." He can't even say for sure what it is, and nothing in the scene tells us whose dock it is. Gatsby reaches for it anyway, and the narrator "could have sworn he was trembling." Before the book explains anything, the light is already tied to reaching and to a distance Gatsby can't cross.

Chapter 5 finally explains the light, and the same scene takes its power away. Gatsby tells Daisy, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock." The words "always" and "all night" give him away, since you only know a light burns all night if you've stayed up watching it. But now Daisy is there to hear him say it, and the narrator guesses that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever." The word "colossal" is strange for something he called "minute" four chapters earlier. The light was tiny and huge at once, and its size in Gatsby's mind came from how far away he was. From across the water it "had seemed very near to her, almost touching her," "as close as a star to the moon." That comparison works against itself. A star can look like it's touching the moon from the ground, but the real distance between them is enormous, so the closeness only existed for someone looking from far off. "Now it was again a green light on a dock," and "again" suggests that's all it ever was. If the light simply meant Daisy, having her there should make it mean more, not less.

By Chapter 9 the light isn't connected to Daisy at all: "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." The light has turned into the future, which is always ahead of you, and "recedes" means it backs away as you move toward it. The phrase "believed in" is also what people use for faith. The pronouns change too. The narrator moves from "Gatsby" to "us" and "we": "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther." That's the same gesture Gatsby made toward the dark water in Chapter 1, except now the narrator puts himself and the reader inside it, which means he's admitting he reaches the way Gatsby did and that we probably do too. The passage stops at "And one fine morning" without finishing the sentence, so the book never says what that morning brings or whether running faster would help.
