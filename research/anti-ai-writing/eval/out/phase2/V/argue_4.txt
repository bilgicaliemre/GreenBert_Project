Adults often tell high school students who want a job that school is their job. I think that's wrong. Most high school students should hold a part-time job during the school year. The strongest argument against working is that it hurts grades, but that's a problem with how many hours a student works, and a limit on hours can fix it.

At a job, the people counting on you aren't grading you, and that changes how you act. In class, if you show up late, you get a tardy and the lesson goes on without you. At work, if you show up late, a coworker has to stay past the end of their shift to cover for you, and they'll remember it the next time you need a favor. A customer arguing about a coupon at the register doesn't care that you had a chemistry test that morning. You have to stay polite and figure out what to do without a teacher to ask. School gives students years of practice taking tests and almost none in calming down an upset adult or telling a manager they made a mistake.

Earning your own money also changes how you spend it. When a pair of sneakers costs two Saturday shifts, you look at the price tag a lot longer than you do when a parent is paying. You notice how fast coffee runs add up when each one costs part of a shift you already worked. For some students, though, the paycheck isn't spending money at all, because their families need the help. Telling every teenager that a school-year job is a mistake doesn't change anything for those students; it just makes them feel like they're doing something wrong by helping out at home.

The worry about grades is fair: a student who closes at a restaurant until eleven on a Tuesday will be half asleep in first period on Wednesday, and if that happens every week, their grades will slip. But schools already accept a time commitment that's just as big. A varsity athlete can have practice every day after school and games on weekends. When an athlete's grades drop, schools usually don't make them quit the sport. They set a grade requirement, and the athlete sits out until the grade comes back up. Jobs can be handled the same way. The difference is that a coach plans around the school calendar and a manager doesn't have to, so students and parents have to set the limits themselves: weekend shifts, maybe one weeknight, no closing shifts before a school day, and fewer hours during finals. If grades drop anyway, the student cuts shifts until the grades recover.

Almost everyone ends up working eventually, and a job in high school means the first big mistakes happen while the stakes are still low. A sixteen-year-old who gets fired for showing up late has a rough week, but they still live at home and have a parent to talk it over with. A twenty-two-year-old who makes the same mistake might lose the money for rent. I'd rather make those mistakes now, when they cost a few shifts instead of an apartment.
