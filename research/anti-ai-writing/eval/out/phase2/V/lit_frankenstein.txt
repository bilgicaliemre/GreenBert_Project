The creature explains what happened to him in one line: "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend." I think the novel mostly agrees with him, and it traces that misery back to Victor. On the night the creature first wakes up in Chapter 5, Victor is "unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created." "Aspect" means appearance here, since the creature hasn't done anything yet. Victor's reaction is about himself: he says "the beauty of the dream vanished," and he paces his bedroom for a long time, "unable to compose my mind to sleep," while the being he just made is left in the room he ran out of. When the creature confronts him on the glacier in Chapter 10, he says who did this: "I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." A fallen angel is supposed to have earned his fall. The creature says he didn't, and "thou drivest" makes Victor the one doing it.

Victor isn't the only one who turns him away, though. The night after the De Lacey family rejects him, in Chapter 16, the creature says, "All, save I, were at rest or in enjoyment; I, like the arch-fiend, bore a hell within me." The cause he names is "finding myself unsympathised with." Then he wishes "to tear up the trees, spread havoc and destruction around me, and then to have sat down and enjoyed the ruin." I noticed that "enjoyment" at the start of the passage comes back as "enjoyed" at the end. Everyone else gets to enjoy things, so he pictures the one kind of enjoyment still open to him: sitting in a ruin he made. The rejection comes first and the "arch-fiend" comes after, the same order as "misery made me a fiend," and at this point the destruction is still just something he "wished" for.

I think the novel puts most of the blame on Victor, because he rejected the creature first and was supposed to treat him as his Adam. But it doesn't make the creature innocent. Over Victor's body in Chapter 24, the creature talks about "the frightful catalogue of my sins." They're "my sins," not Victor's, and he can't believe he's "the same creature" who once had "visions of the beauty and the majesty of goodness." His glacier comparison has gotten worse: "the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil." Even so, what hurts him most is still being alone. The devil "had friends and associates in his desolation; I am alone." That's why another line from the glacier bothers me: "Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous." It sounds like a bargain, but nothing in these passages proves him wrong. His goodness depended on someone sympathizing with him, and in these scenes Victor runs out of the room and the De Laceys turn him away.
