I used to answer the phone at my dad's shop with "Hi, shoe repair." The shop, Carvalho Shoe Service, has been on Pleasant Street in Fall River since 1994. On Saturdays I work the counter and write tickets, and since last summer I'm allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. It smells like Barge cement and leather dust, and so does my hoodie (my friends say I smell like a "cowboy's basement"). I hate the phone. I have a stutter that gets worse on the phone, especially on hard sounds like C, and the shop's name starts with one. My dad noticed after about a year and never said anything.

My avó, my grandmother Rosa, had a stroke two years ago and has aphasia. She understands more than she can say. Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, the name of my aunt who died before I was born. At first I corrected her. Now I don't. She can't say my name yet, but she can say sapato, shoe. On Tuesdays and Thursdays after school we do picture flashcards I made for her in Portuguese. She's relearning the words and I'm learning them for the first time: she's better than me at agulha (needle), and I'm better at telemóvel (cell phone).

I read a lot about aphasia now. I found out it has nothing to do with stuttering, and I kind of wanted the two to be connected. I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics, or something about how the brain gets words back. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my speech therapist from middle school, whether I'd be "allowed" to be a speech therapist with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter, and I don't fully believe her yet.

Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept that up for three weeks. I forged her signature on the progress report, and before that I practiced it about eight times on the back of a math worksheet. That's the part I'd rather leave out, because it means I planned it; it wasn't a panic thing. Keeping the 85 straight was exhausting, and when my teacher, Mr. Oduya, emailed home, I was relieved, which I didn't tell anyone.

My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese, and my Portuguese is weak on chemistry words. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed. I ended the class with a B+. After that I made a rule: I keep a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even small ones like "I'm almost there." It has 212 items since October of sophomore year, most of them about being on time. I'm still not sure if the list makes me more honest or just better at noticing.

Last month the shop phone rang and I tried to say the name. I was picturing the man's face, wondering if he was annoyed, and on the second try I almost hung up. He waited. On the third try "Carvalho Shoe Service" came out, and I didn't feel proud, just kind of embarrassed that it took three tries. The best part was that it was nothing to him, and he just asked whether we fix purses.

"Hi, shoe repair" isn't on my list. I only noticed that recently, after I'd already said the name once. It was a lie too, a small one. I've been doing the same thing with the phone that I did with the chemistry test: swapping the true thing for an easier thing. I'm not sure what to do with that yet. Maybe it goes on the list.
