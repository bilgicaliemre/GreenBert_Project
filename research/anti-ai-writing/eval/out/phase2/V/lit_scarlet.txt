When Hester first stands on the scaffold in Chapter II, the scarlet A is mostly about how other people see her. The letter "drew all eyes" and "transfigured the wearer," so that people who already knew Hester felt "as if they beheld her for the first time." The woman they knew disappears behind it. Hawthorne says it had "the effect of a spell," an odd phrase for an embroidered letter until you think about what a spell does: it works on you whether you agree to it or not. This one takes Hester "out of the ordinary relations with humanity" and shuts her "in a sphere by herself." So at the start, the A means shame, and it also means being alone. The town gives the letter a kinder name later, but I think the bigger change comes in the last chapter, when Hester puts it back on because she wants to.

In Chapter XIII the town does start to read it differently. Hester is so helpful that "many people refused to interpret the scarlet A by its original signification. They said that it meant Able." "Refused" shows that the old meaning is still there. People have to push against it, and only "many" of them manage to, not all. "Able" is also the town's word. What the letter means to Hester shows up in Chapter XVIII, when she unclasps it in the forest and throws it aside: "The stigma gone, Hester heaved a long, deep sigh, in which the burden of shame and anguish departed from her spirit." Five chapters after "Able," the narrator still calls the letter a stigma, and Hester is still carrying the shame. Hawthorne adds, "She had not known the weight, until she felt the freedom!" The new name never made the letter lighter for her. She had only stopped noticing how heavy it was.

Hester comes back to Boston in Chapter XXIV and takes the A up again "of her own free will," and Hawthorne says that "not the sternest magistrate of that iron period would have imposed it." He uses "resumed" twice in that sentence, as if he wants us to be sure nobody made her. Over the "toilsome, thoughtful, and self-devoted years" that follow, the letter "ceased to be a stigma." This time the narrator says so himself; in Chapter XIII it was only what "many people" said. The new meaning still isn't a happy one, though. The letter became "a type of something to be sorrowed over, and looked upon with awe, yet with reverence too." Sorrow comes first, and reverence comes last, tacked on with "yet" and "too." By the end, the A carries the sad parts of Hester's life along with the respect people feel for her, and Hester is the one who chose to keep wearing it.
