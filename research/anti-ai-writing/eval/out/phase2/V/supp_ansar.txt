I have 37 wild apple trees tagged in the foothills outside Almaty. They're Malus sieversii, the wild ancestor of basically every apple, and they just grow here. I measure their Brix, the percent sugar in the juice; wild ones run from about 9 to 15.

Tree 23 was my best, at 15.2. I took a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds and said, "This one has been to the city." It's 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably crossed with someone's garden apple. I wanted to argue, as if 15.2 in my notebook proved where a tree came from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good, and I was angry at her and at the apples.

My grandmother calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, as a joke, because I answer her in Russian. I think that's why Tree 23 bothered me: a city one, half one thing and half the other, not the real wild thing. I got defensive for a tree.

I keep reading that the wild forests are "70 percent gone since the 1960s," but every website copies the same sentence, so I don't fully trust it. I want to be the person who decides which trees get saved. Some places, Zhanna Serikovna told me, cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. I don't know if I'd cut Tree 23. Probably the correct answer is yes.
