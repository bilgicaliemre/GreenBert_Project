"I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend," the creature tells Victor on the glacier. Shelley agrees with him about the cause and traces the misery to Victor, but she does not let the creature treat it as a full excuse. In his sentence "misery" is the subject and the creature is the thing being made, as if he had been produced twice, once by Victor and once by suffering. The speech also names the source of the misery: "Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded." He puts Victor at the start of that exclusion: "I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." "Thou drivest" makes Victor the one acting, and "for no misdeed" denies the creature did anything to earn it.

Victor's own account of the night the creature wakes supports the charge. Once the work is finished, "breathless horror and disgust filled my heart," and he runs from the room "Unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created." "Aspect" means appearance: Victor flees from how the creature looks, on a night when it has had no chance to do anything. In Chapter 16, after the De Lacey family rejects him, the pattern repeats, and this time the misery turns into a wish for violence. "All, save I, were at rest or in enjoyment," he says, echoing "I alone am irrevocably excluded" from Chapter 10. Then, "finding myself unsympathised with," he "wished to tear up the trees, spread havoc and destruction around me, and then to have sat down and enjoyed the ruin." The sentence uses "enjoyment" for what everyone else has and "enjoyed" for the ruin he imagines, so destruction is the only pleasure he can picture for himself. In the same sentence he says he, "like the arch-fiend, bore a hell within me," so he has started to describe himself in the fiend's terms.

That is why the novel stops short of blaming Victor for everything. Speaking to Walton over Victor's body, the creature looks back on "the frightful catalogue of my sins," and the "my" accepts them as his own. He cannot believe he is "the same creature" whose thoughts were once full of visions of "the beauty and the majesty of goodness," so he still insists he began as good, while "the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil" carries the Chapter 10 comparison one step lower. Even so, his explanation returns to loneliness: "even that enemy of God and man had friends and associates in his desolation; I am alone." The novel holds Victor responsible for starting the change and the creature responsible for his sins. It leaves the creature's offer on the glacier untested: "Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous." Over Victor's body he is still alone, so the condition was never met, and the novel never shows whether his goodness could have returned.
