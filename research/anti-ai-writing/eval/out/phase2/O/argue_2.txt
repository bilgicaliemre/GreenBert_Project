Let Students Retake Major Tests

When a major test comes back, the usual routine is to check the number and file the paper in a binder that will not be opened again. If the grade is final, that routine makes sense, because nothing about the old test can change. Schools should give students a reason to open that binder by letting them retake major tests after they correct their mistakes, with the new score replacing the old one instead of being averaged with it.

A grade is supposed to report what a student knows. Take a student who scores a 58 on a chemistry test about stoichiometry in October. She spends two lunch periods with her teacher, reworks every problem she missed, and scores an 88 on a new version in November. If the school does not allow retakes, the 58 stands. If it averages the two scores, her report card shows a 73. Both numbers are weighed down by a confusion she has already cleared up, so neither one tells a parent or a college what she can do now.

Retakes also change what students do after a bad test. Units in math and science build on each other. A student who never learned to factor will struggle when the class reaches quadratic equations, and a chemistry student who cannot balance an equation will be lost once stoichiometry begins. Under a no-retake policy, relearning an old unit takes hours and does nothing for the grade, so the sensible choice is to move on and hope the gap stays hidden. In a cumulative subject, it rarely does. Requiring corrections before the retake makes the student go through every problem she missed, which is the review that a returned test is supposed to start.

The strongest objection is that students will treat the first test as a practice round. If a second chance is guaranteed, why study for the first one? The risk is real, which is why a retake has to cost the student something. Correcting every missed problem and giving up a lunch period to sit a new version is more work than studying properly the first time, and students who notice that trade will prepare for the first test. A student who skips studying anyway still has to learn the material to pass the retake, so she learns it a few weeks after her classmates do.

A second objection is about fairness. Someone who earned an 88 on the first try may resent a classmate who reaches the same grade a month later. But by the end of the unit both students know the material, and the grade should say so. The claim that the real world gives no second chances is also wrong. People who fail the driving test can take it again, and law graduates who fail the bar exam can sit for it again. In both cases the license goes to whoever eventually meets the standard, and it does not list how many attempts that took.

Most of the cost of retakes falls on teachers. Every retake means writing a second version of the test and grading it, often outside class hours. That is a good reason to limit the policy and a weak reason to refuse it. A school could allow one retake per major test, taken only after the corrections are turned in. That keeps the extra grading manageable and saves retakes for students who did badly and were willing to come back and fix it, which is the behavior a grading system should reward.
