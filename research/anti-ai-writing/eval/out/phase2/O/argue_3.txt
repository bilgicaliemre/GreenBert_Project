Same project, different grades

A group project grade gets copied onto every member's report card, but it often describes the work of only some of them. When a teacher gives everyone the same grade, the student who wrote the script and rebuilt the slides at midnight gets the same mark as the student who showed up on presentation day and read off a note card. Group members should not all receive the same grade. Each project should be graded in two parts: a shared grade for the finished product, and an individual grade for what each student contributed and understood.

Grades are supposed to report what one student learned, which is why the gradebook has a row for each name, not for each table group. If three lab partners all get an A on a report that one of them designed and another only copied data into, the gradebook says the same thing about two very different students, and for one of them it is wrong. Parents read that A as a statement about their child, and so do colleges once it is folded into a semester average. The shared grade turns it into a statement about a group that will break up when the unit ends.

A shared grade also decides who does the work. The member who cares most about their average has the most to lose, so that member usually ends up doing the most. The student who does not care sees this too, and learns that waiting pays off: leave your section blank long enough and someone else will fill it in the night before it is due. Teachers assign group projects so students learn to work together, and a shared grade often teaches the opposite. The one who carried the group walks away convinced it is faster to do everything alone than to depend on anyone.

The best argument for a single grade is that it pushes the group to act as a team. If each student were graded only on their own slides, people might guard their sections and leave a stuck partner alone, and the project would become four small assignments stapled together. That concern is fair, and it is why part of the grade should stay shared: if a weak section costs everyone points, strong students still have a reason to help. Another argument, that jobs judge teams as a whole, is weaker. At most jobs, raises and promotions still go to people one at a time, and a worker stuck with a useless colleague can go to a manager or ask to switch teams; a student in a group the teacher assigned usually can do neither.

The practical objection is that a teacher cannot sit in on every group meeting. Many group projects now live in a shared Google Doc or slideshow, though, and the version history shows who wrote what and when. A teacher can also ask each student a couple of questions about a section someone else wrote. A student who did their part and read the rest can answer; a student who did neither cannot. Peer evaluations add information too, but they need a careful reader, because students sometimes use them to protect friends or settle scores.

Two grades will not make every student work. Some will still do nothing and accept a low individual score, and some groups will still argue. But the student who did nothing could no longer borrow a grade from the student who did everything, and the grade next to each name would say something true about that person.
