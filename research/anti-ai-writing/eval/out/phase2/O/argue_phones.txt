Phones away from bell to bell

High schools should require students to put their phones away at the first bell and leave them there until the last one, lunch and passing periods included. The common compromise, a rule against phones only during class, does not work. It leaves the phone in the student's pocket, where it still buzzes, and it makes each teacher decide again and again whether a quick look at a screen counts as breaking the rule.

Phones distract students even when nobody is holding one. Face-down on a desk, a phone lights up when a message comes in; in a pocket, it vibrates. Someone who feels that buzz during a discussion of The Great Gatsby has not broken any rule, but part of their attention has moved to the message they have not read yet. Group chats make the pull stronger, because they do not pause for class. If a chat runs all through third period, the student who waits for the bell has a long backlog to read, so checking under the desk while the teacher writes on the board starts to look like the sensible choice.

Class-only rules also turn teachers into phone monitors. When phones are allowed in the building but not in the room, the teacher has to spot each one and then argue with the student who says they were only checking the time, while the rest of the class waits. The rules shift from room to room as well. One teacher lets students listen to music during independent work and the next one does not, so students test the line in every class. A full-day ban settles the question once, at the start of the day, and nobody has to reopen it in the middle of a lesson.

Students will push back hardest on lunch, and lunch is where the ban does the most good. For many students it is the only time to talk with friends outside their classes. At a table where everyone has a phone, anyone can leave the conversation without getting up, and a new or shy student can spend the whole period looking at a screen while nobody notices. Passing periods are a smaller case of the same problem: whatever someone reads in the hallway, such as a fight starting in a group chat or a test grade that just posted, follows them into the next class.

The strongest objection comes from parents who want to reach their kids in an emergency. Schools handled family emergencies before students carried phones. The parent calls the front office, and the office sends for the student. The ban also does not require confiscation; phones can sit in lockers or in locked pouches that open at dismissal, so students can still sort out rides after practice. Other students would object that sixteen-year-olds should learn to manage their own phones instead of having adults take the choice away. They already get that practice every evening and weekend. A school day without a phone, five days a week, gives them something to measure those evenings against, and that may teach them more about their own habits than another day of trying not to look.

None of this makes a ban a cure. A dull lesson is still dull without a phone, and a student who wants to tune out can stare at the clock instead. The ban also asks something of the adults: it has to cover teachers' phones during class too, or students will have a fair reason to stop taking it seriously.
