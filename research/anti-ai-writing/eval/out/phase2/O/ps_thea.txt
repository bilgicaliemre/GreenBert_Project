At Leonard Tietz's funeral, an organ pipe got stuck on. It's called a cipher: one low note going and going through the whole Lord's Prayer, and I had to run behind the curtain and shut off the blower. Pastor Jill said, "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word," and everybody laughed, even his wife, and I was kind of mad, which doesn't make sense.

I'm the organist at Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, Nebraska, and the funeral home calls me too. I get $85 a funeral and play the pedals of our 1958 Wicks in socks because my shoes slip. I've played 48 funerals since June 2024, none as just a guest. I don't get sad at them; it's a job. I do keep count, and by February of junior year I had 31 funerals and 6 baptisms at Immanuel.

I put the numbers in Google Sheets with a trend line and posted the chart in the Cedar County Community Corner group on Facebook, with a skull emoji and the words "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!" Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, commented, "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I replied "anecdotes aren't data" to a woman with 22 kids in her basement. I'd gotten the line from a YouTube video that week. Then a man from Holy Trinity pointed out that the Catholics do most of the baptisms in town and Immanuel is basically the old people's church, so my whole post was an anecdote about one church.

I deleted it after four hours. My mom is the rural mail carrier, so she made me write Kathy an apology and delivered it with the mail. She made me put a real stamp on it so it was legal. I told nobody at the time that I liked the comments. I kept refreshing all 63 of them in Spanish class, the mad ones especially, which is bad, I know. It was the first time anything I made got read by more people than my 86-year-old piano teacher. And nobody outside quiz bowl knows that before deleting the post, I screenshotted Kathy's comment and sent it to our group chat to make fun of her.

To prove I was right, I went on the Census website and ended up reading about "natural decrease," when a county has more deaths than births, which a lot of counties out here have. It's a whole field, demography, a word I hadn't known, and now it's what I want to study, that or statistics. Everybody says to do actuarial science at UNL for the money, but I don't want to do death math for money. (I know. I already do death music for money.) My school doesn't have statistics, so I'm taking it as dual credit from Northeast Community College on Zoom.

The county probably is shrinking. Just not like that, and not by my math. I think I wanted the town to be dying. If it's dying anyway, then leaving isn't really leaving anybody. The 2070 thing was me writing myself a permission slip with a trend line.

After Leonard's funeral lunch, Duane Pinkelman, the funeral director, held the ladder while I climbed up and pulled the pipe. It was low F-sharp. There was a dead wasp in it. I think I wanted the cipher to be my fault so I could fix it. At a funeral it's Leonard, and his wife laughing, and the wasp. In the census it's minus one. Duane says every one was somebody's whole world. I don't know if I like the math because it makes all that bigger, every county at once, or because it makes it smaller and easier. Sometimes I think the spreadsheet is so I don't have to feel 48 separate things. I can't tell which yet.
