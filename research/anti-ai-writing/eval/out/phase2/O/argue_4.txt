Treat the job like a sport

Nobody tells the varsity soccer player that two hours of practice most afternoons and games on Saturdays are ruining her grades. The school posts her game schedule, and teachers sometimes excuse her from class for away games. The student who spends the same hours bagging groceries usually hears something else: the job is a distraction, and school has to come first. High school students should hold part-time jobs during the school year, kept to about the hours a sport would take, because a job teaches things a classroom is not built to teach.

School is designed so that mistakes can be fixed. Quizzes can often be retaken, and late homework usually still earns partial credit. That forgiveness helps students learn, but it also means most of their mistakes cost nobody but themselves. At a job, other people absorb them. When a cashier shows up twenty minutes late, a coworker stays past the end of a shift or the line backs up while customers wait. When the register comes up short at closing, someone has to count it again before anyone goes home. Nothing gets curved. A student who misses two shifts may find her name gone from the next week's schedule, and that teaches more about showing up than a lecture on responsibility does.

Earning money also changes how a teenager thinks about spending it. The first paycheck usually comes in smaller than the hourly wage times the hours worked, because taxes are taken out, and reading the pay stub explains deductions better than a worksheet does. Once a student knows what an hour of her time is worth, she starts pricing things in hours: a concert ticket stops being a number and becomes a Saturday morning of stocking shelves. That habit is cheaper to pick up at sixteen, when the stakes are a ticket or a pair of shoes, than at twenty-two with rent due.

The serious objection is time. Homework, sleep and a job all come out of the same evening, and a student who closes a restaurant at eleven on a Tuesday and then opens her chemistry homework is going to lose sleep, points or both. The cost is real, and it grows with every hour added. It argues for a limit on hours (weekend shifts and perhaps one weeknight), not for keeping students out of work altogether. Many employers that hire teenagers already schedule around school, and a student can ask for finals week off well in advance, the way an athlete tells her coach about a conflict. For a student who is already failing a class, even a light schedule may be too much until the grade comes back up. Most students, though, can handle a few shifts a week for the same reason athletes get through a season: they plan around it.

If schools treated a job the way they treat a season on a team, the students who would notice first are the ones who have no choice. Some teenagers already work during the school year because their families need the money, and telling them that school is their only job does nothing for them. A school that counted a work shift the way it counts an away game, as a real commitment that can sometimes move a deadline, would tell those students, and every student thinking about applying for a first job, that the hours count.
