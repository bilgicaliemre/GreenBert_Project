Blood on the hands in Macbeth stands for guilt, and the play measures guilt by size: whoever pictures the stain as bigger than the world still has a working conscience. In Act II that person is Macbeth. Just after killing Duncan, he asks, "Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" and answers that his hand "will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine, / Making the green one red." The washing runs backwards: his hand will dye the ocean before the ocean cleans his hand. Lady Macbeth comes back from smearing the grooms with blood and sizes the same stain much smaller. "My hands are of your color," she tells him, "but I shame / To wear a heart so white." She accepts the red on her skin and is embarrassed only by his pale, frightened heart. When knocking starts at the south entry, she handles the murder as a cleanup job: they will go to their chamber, and "A little water clears us of this deed."

By Act III the two have begun to trade places. After Banquo's ghost appears at the banquet, the blood in Macbeth's speech is no longer on his hand. He is standing in it: "I am in blood / Stepp’d in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o’er." Neptune's ocean has shrunk to a river he is halfway across, and his complaint is that turning back would be "tedious," a word for a long, tiring chore. He now reasons the way his wife did in Act II, and "For mine own good, / All causes shall give way" makes her cleanup job into a rule. Lady Macbeth, meanwhile, inherits his old exaggeration. Sleepwalking in Act V, she says, "Here’s the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand." The perfumes of Arabia play the part Neptune's ocean played for Macbeth, the biggest cleanser she can name and still not enough. Both of them described the blood by its color in Act II; now she describes it by smell. The word "little" has switched sides too: it once described the water that would fix everything, and here it describes the hand that nothing can fix.

The trade is uneven, though. Macbeth changes his attitude awake and on purpose, in a sentence that opens with "For mine own good." Lady Macbeth's change shows only in her sleep, with a Doctor and a Gentlewoman watching, and her words break down into "Oh, oh, oh!" That difference changes how her Act II lines read. "How easy is it then!" sounds in hindsight like something she needed to be true, said over the knocking at the south entry as she hurried her husband to their chamber. Macbeth's conscience goes quiet because he decides to keep wading. Hers never went quiet; it waited until she was asleep and could no longer argue it down.
