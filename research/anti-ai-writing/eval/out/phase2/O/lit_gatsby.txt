The green light in The Great Gatsby means something only while it is out of reach. Its meaning moves from Daisy to the future because, once Gatsby closes the distance to Daisy, the light turns back into "a green light on a dock," and the narrator can give it meaning again only by tying it to something nobody can reach. The pattern starts in chapter 1. Gatsby "stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way," and the narrator "could have sworn he was trembling," yet when he glances the same way he sees "nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." He cannot say what the light is attached to, and the light itself is "minute." Everything large in the scene belongs to Gatsby: the reaching arms and the trembling. In chapter 5 the light gets an owner. Gatsby tells Daisy, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock." The possessive "your" hands the light to her, and "always" and "all night" suggest he has watched it for many nights.

The same chapter takes that meaning away. "Possibly it had occurred to him," the narrator guesses, "that the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever." The next sentence gives the reason: the light had seemed "very near to her, almost touching her," but only in comparison with "the great distance that had separated him from Daisy." With the distance gone, "it was again a green light on a dock," and "again" tells us it was an ordinary dock light before Gatsby made it a sign for Daisy. The narrator records the loss as bookkeeping: "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one." A count implies a list, so the light was one enchanted object among others, and getting near Daisy cost Gatsby this one.

In chapter 9 the light appears in a sentence that leaves Daisy out: "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." The appositive renames the light "the orgastic future," and a future that "recedes" can never be reached, so it can never shrink back into a dock light as it did in chapter 5. The passage also moves from Gatsby to "us" and "we": "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther." This is Gatsby's gesture from chapter 1, now made by the narrator and, through "we," by the reader. One sentence in the passage never finishes: "And one fine morning" breaks off before it reaches a verb. In chapter 5 the narrator only guessed at Gatsby's thoughts; in chapter 9 he states as fact what "Gatsby believed," and he is most certain about the light in the sentence where it stands for the future. That last meaning, a future that keeps moving away, may be the narrator's reading of Gatsby as much as Gatsby's own belief.
