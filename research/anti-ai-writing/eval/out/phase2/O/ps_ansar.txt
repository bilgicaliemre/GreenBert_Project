At my äje's seventieth birthday, at the table, I told her that her apple tree was "genetically boring."

It's an old Aport, the famous Almaty apple, at her dacha in Kamenka. I had just learned that apples don't grow true from seed, so varieties are kept by grafting and every Aport is technically the same tree, cloned for about 150 years. My uncle laughed. My äje didn't. I had said it to sound clever in front of my cousins; I wanted the table to know I knew something.

Two weeks later, while she was in town, I sawed a big branch off her tree without asking and grafted on sticks of wild Malus sieversii. I had cut them inside the national park, which isn't allowed, and carried them two days in my backpack in the heat. Grafting sticks should be dormant; mine had leaves and dried out, so the graft died and the cut went black.

Dad was at Tengiz, the oil field where he works fourteen days on, fourteen off. I told him on a video call, like a science update, and he didn't say anything. I also told him I'd cut the sticks "at the edge of the park." It was inside, pretty far inside. I told nobody that I was kind of glad the graft died. If it had taken, a wild branch would have stayed on her tree forever, basically my signature, for her to look at every summer.

When Dad got home, he drove me to the dacha and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound with garden pitch. She said something fast in Kazakh. I got half and asked Dad in the car. "Ағаш дәптер емес." A tree is not a notebook.

After that, Gennady Pavlovich, who is 81 and sells apples at the Green Bazaar, taught me to graft properly. He made me do 200 cuts on willow sticks before he let me touch an apple.

I have 37 wild apple trees tagged in the foothills. Tree 23 was my best: 15.2 Brix (percent sugar in the juice). I took a bag of its apples to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds and said, "This one has been to the city." The tree is 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably a hybrid with some dacha apple. I wanted to argue. I had 15.2 in my notebook, as if that proves where a tree comes from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good, and I was angry at her and also at the apples, which makes no sense.

Äje calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, because I answer her in Russian. Everybody laughs, me too. I think that's why Tree 23 bothered me: a city one, half one thing and half the other, not the real wild thing. I got defensive for a tree. With the Aport I did the opposite, calling her tree boring for being a copy and trying to put the wild thing into it. I don't know what that says about me. Maybe nothing. Zhanna Serikovna also said some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. I want to be the person who decides which trees get saved, and I don't know if I would cut Tree 23, though the correct answer is probably yes. I haven't said this to my parents: if I go to America for four years, my Kazakh will get worse, not better, and äje is 70.

Every evening at 21:00, Dad sends a photo from Tengiz, the same view of a flare stack and the sunset, 340-something so far. It's the most Aport thing in my life, though I could be making that up because it sounds good. He never writes anything, and I have never asked why.
