In The Scarlet Letter, the A goes from a mark that cuts Hester Prynne off from ordinary human company to a sign held in reverence, but at each stage Hawthorne defines it through the people looking at it. The scaffold scene in Chapter II sets the pattern. The letter "drew all eyes" and "transfigured the wearer," so that men and women "familiarly acquainted" with Hester saw her "as if they beheld her for the first time." Hawthorne describes the letter by what it does to the onlookers, and the first thing it does is turn a woman they knew into a stranger. He then names the effect: the A works like "a spell," "taking her out of the ordinary relations with humanity, and enclosing her in a sphere by herself." A spell is cast from outside, so in Chapter II the A means separation, done to Hester by the people who read it.

Years later, in Chapter XIII, the reading changes. Hester has shown so much "power to do, and power to sympathize" that "many people refused to interpret the scarlet A by its original signification. They said that it meant Able." Her helpfulness earned the new reading, but the sentence still gives the naming to others: "They said." The letter's meaning has followed what people think of Hester. Chapter XVIII breaks the pattern by showing the letter from Hester's side, and there the new reading turns out not to have reached her. When she unclasps the A in the forest and throws it aside, "the burden of shame and anguish departed from her spirit," and the narrator adds, "She had not known the weight, until she felt the freedom!" Even after people began calling the letter Able, then, Hester was still carrying it as shame and anguish, so constantly that she had stopped feeling the load.

The last stage begins with the first decision about the letter that Hester makes herself. In the Conclusion she returns to Boston and takes it up again "of her own free will, for not the sternest magistrate of that iron period would have imposed it." Hawthorne writes "resumed" twice, once on each side of that clause, so the reader cannot miss whose decision this is. Over the "toilsome, thoughtful, and self-devoted years" that follow, the letter "ceased to be a stigma which attracted the world's scorn and bitterness" and became something "to be sorrowed over, and looked upon with awe, yet with reverence too." This final meaning is also told through an audience: sorrow, awe and reverence are what onlookers feel, and the letter is still "looked upon." Hester decides to wear it, but other people still decide what it means. In the forest, freedom meant throwing the letter aside. In Boston, Hester uses her freedom to put the letter back on, and the narrator states that "[n]ever afterwards did it quit her bosom."
