How does photosynthesis turn sunlight into food for a plant?

Where does the wood in a tree come from? In the 1600s the Flemish chemist Jan Baptist van Helmont tested the obvious answer, the soil. He planted a five-pound willow in a pot of dried, weighed soil, gave it only water for five years, then weighed both again. The willow had gained about 164 pounds; the soil had lost about two ounces. Van Helmont concluded the tree was made of water. He missed the second ingredient, carbon dioxide, a gas that makes up a small fraction of one percent of the air.

Photosynthesis is how a plant turns that gas into sugar. The overall equation is short: six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six of water, powered by light, give one molecule of glucose and six of oxygen. So the title's question is slightly off. Sunlight does not turn into food; it supplies the energy, while carbon dioxide and water supply the atoms.

The process runs in two stages inside chloroplasts, the green bodies in leaf cells. Stage one takes place on stacks of membranes called thylakoids, which hold the pigment chlorophyll. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light strongly and green light much less, so leaves look green. Absorbed light gives some of chlorophyll's electrons extra energy, and they are passed along a chain of proteins. To replace them, the plant splits water molecules. The leftover oxygen leaves through pores called stomata, so the oxygen you breathe came from water, not carbon dioxide. The energy from light ends up in two carrier molecules, ATP and NADPH.

Stage two, the Calvin cycle, runs in the fluid around the thylakoids. An enzyme called rubisco attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar, and ATP and NADPH from stage one are used to turn the products into a three-carbon sugar. Most of these molecules are rebuilt into the five-carbon starter so the cycle continues; the rest become the plant's food. Melvin Calvin's team at Berkeley traced these steps in the late 1940s and 1950s by feeding algae carbon dioxide containing radioactive carbon-14.

From these sugars a plant makes glucose and sucrose, and the sucrose travels through the phloem to roots, fruit and growing tips. Like animals, plants break some sugar down in their mitochondria for energy; at night, and in roots, this is their only energy source. The rest becomes starch for storage and cellulose for cell walls. The soil adds small amounts of minerals, such as the magnesium at the center of each chlorophyll molecule. Much of the weight van Helmont measured was water, as he thought, and most of the rest had entered the leaves as carbon dioxide.
