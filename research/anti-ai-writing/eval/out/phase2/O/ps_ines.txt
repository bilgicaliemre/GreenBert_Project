I practiced my mom's signature about eight times on the back of a math worksheet before I forged it on my progress report. Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told her it was an 85, and kept it up for three weeks. The practicing is the part I'd rather leave out, because it means I planned it. It wasn't a panic thing. I didn't tell anyone, but I was relieved when my teacher, Mr. Oduya, emailed home; keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting.

My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test to her, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese, and my Portuguese doesn't cover chemistry. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed at that part.

After that I made a rule: I keep a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even small ones like "I'm almost there." It has 212 items since October of sophomore year, and most of them are about being on time. I'm still not sure whether the list makes me more honest or just better at noticing.

On Saturdays I work the counter at my dad's shoe repair shop, which has been on Pleasant Street since 1994. Since last summer I've been allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. The shop smells like Barge cement and leather dust, and so does my hoodie; my friends say I smell like a cowboy's basement. I hate the phone. My stutter gets worse on it, especially on hard sounds like C, and the shop is called Carvalho Shoe Service. So I used to answer "Hi, shoe repair." My dad noticed after about a year and never said anything about it.

My grandmother, my avó Rosa, had a stroke two years ago and has aphasia. She understands more than she can say. On Tuesdays and Thursdays after school we do flashcards I made for her, with pictures, in Portuguese. It's funny, because she's relearning the words while I'm learning them for the first time: she's better than me at agulha (needle), and I'm better at telemóvel (cell phone). Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, my aunt who died before I was born. At first I corrected her, and now I don't. She can say sapato, shoe, but she can't say my name yet.

I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics, or something about how the brain gets words back. I read a lot about aphasia now, and it's totally different from stuttering, though I'd kind of wanted them connected. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my speech therapist from middle school, if I'd be "allowed" to be one with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter, which I still don't fully believe.

Last month, for the first time, I answered the shop phone with the name. I was trying to picture the man's face, whether he was annoyed, and on the second try I almost hung up. He waited. When "Carvalho Shoe Service" came out on the third try, I didn't feel proud; I felt kind of embarrassed that it took three. Then he asked if we fix purses (we don't), like nothing had happened, and that was the best part, that it was nothing to him.

"Hi, shoe repair" was a lie too. A small one. It isn't on my list, and I only noticed when someone asked. I've been doing the same thing on the phone that I did with the chemistry test, swapping the true thing for an easier thing, and on the phone the true thing was our name. I'm not sure what to do with that yet. Maybe it goes on the list.
