I have played 48 funerals since June 2024. I know because I keep track.

I'm the organist at Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, Nebraska, and the funeral home calls me too, so a lot of my Saturdays are a funeral at 10:30 and then the lunch in the basement. I play the pedals in socks because my shoes slip. My little brother calls me "the Grim Reaper's DJ." I don't get sad at them, honestly. It's a job. What I didn't expect was that the counting would turn into the thing I can't stop thinking about.

In February of my junior year I had counted 31 funerals and 6 baptisms at Immanuel since I started. I put them in Google Sheets, added a trend line, and posted the chart in the Cedar County Community Corner group on Facebook: "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!" With a skull emoji. It got 63 comments. Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, wrote "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I replied "anecdotes aren't data," which I had learned from a YouTube video that same week. Then a man from Holy Trinity pointed out that the Catholics do most of the baptisms in town and Immanuel is basically the old people's church. I had only counted my church. My whole post was an anecdote.

I deleted it after four hours. My mom is the rural mail carrier and Kathy is on her route, so she made me write an apology and delivered it with the mail. She made me put a real stamp on it so it was legal.

The part I didn't tell anyone is that I liked it. I kept refreshing the comments in Spanish class. It was the first time anything I made got read by more people than my old piano teacher, even if most of them were mad.

But being wrong is how I found demography, which I didn't know was a word. I went on the Census website to prove I was right and ended up reading about "natural decrease," which is when a county has more deaths than births. It turns out that's a whole field. My school doesn't offer statistics, so now I'm taking it as dual credit through Northeast Community College on Zoom.

This is what I think about when I lose track of time. At a funeral, it's Leonard Tietz. During his service a pipe got stuck on and one low note kept going through the whole Lord's Prayer until I ran behind the curtain and shut off the blower. Pastor Jill said "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word," and everybody laughed, even his wife. After the lunch Duane, the funeral director, held the ladder while I pulled the pipe. It was low F-sharp. There was a dead wasp inside. In the census, Leonard is minus one.

Duane says every single one was somebody's whole world. I don't know if I like the math because it makes it bigger, like all the counties at once, or because it makes it smaller and easier. Sometimes I think the spreadsheet is so I don't have to feel 48 separate things. And I think part of me wanted the town to be dying, because if it's dying anyway then leaving isn't really leaving anybody. The 2070 thing was me writing myself a permission slip with a trend line.

Arlene Thoene, who was the organist at Immanuel for 51 years and taught me pedals, listens to the livestream from assisted living in Yankton and texts me afterward in all caps: "TOO FAST ON DOXOLOGY. BREATHE WITH THEM." I still don't know exactly what she means. I think I'm close. I think it might be the same thing I want to learn in statistics: how to count people without forgetting that every number was a person.
