"Ағаш дәптер емес."

My grandmother said it fast while she was painting garden pitch on the black cut on her apple tree. I understood half and asked my dad in the car. It means "A tree is not a notebook." It took me a long time to understand what she meant.

I live in Almaty, Kazakhstan. "Alma" means apple. The city is named like that because Malus sieversii, the wild ancestor of basically every apple in the world, just grows in the mountains here. I have 37 wild apple trees tagged in the foothills past Talgar. In September and October I go there on Saturdays after school (yes, we have school on Saturday). If it is a dad week, my dad drives me at 5:30 a.m. He works at Tengiz, the oil field, fourteen days on, fourteen off. If not, it's the bus and 40 minutes walking. I measure the sugar in the apples with a refractometer, and I can guess the Brix from one bite. My mom tested me on 40 apples and I was within one point 31 times.

So I thought I knew a lot about apples. Last May I learned that apples don't grow true from seed, every seed is a new apple, so a variety is kept by grafting. That means every Aport, the famous Almaty apple, is technically the same tree cloned for like 150 years. My äje has an old Aport tree at her dacha in Kamenka. At her 70th birthday, at the table, I told her that her tree was "genetically boring." My uncle laughed. My äje didn't.

Two weeks later, when she was in town, I sawed off a big branch and grafted wild sieversii onto it. I cut the sticks in the national park, which is not allowed, and carried them two days in my backpack in the heat. It was the wrong season. The sticks should be dormant and mine had leaves, so they dried out. And I didn't ask her. The graft died and the cut went black.

Dad was at Tengiz, so I told him on a video call like it was a science update. He didn't say anything. When he came back he drove me to the dacha and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound. That is when she said it.

The grafting part I can explain as a science mistake. The birthday part I can't. I said "genetically boring" to sound clever in front of my cousins. I wanted the whole table to know I knew something. There is no science excuse for that.

After the Aport thing, Gennady Pavlovich, who is 81 and sells apples at the Green Bazaar, taught me grafting properly. He made me do 200 cuts on willow sticks before he let me touch an apple. He has one tree with 14 varieties on it that he calls "the kommunalka." He says if a graft looks too neat, you didn't cut deep enough.

My äje calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, as a joke because I answer her in Russian. Everybody laughs, me too. But when I found out my best wild tree, Tree 23, was probably a hybrid with some city apple, I got really defensive, and I think it was for the same reason. Then with the Aport I did the opposite and called it boring for being a copy. I am still figuring out what that says about me.

I want to study conservation genetics and be the person who decides which trees get saved. There is a USDA orchard in Geneva, New York, with sieversii grown from seeds Americans collected here in the 90s. Our apples live in New York. But before I decide anything about any tree, I learned I have to ask whose tree it is, and what it means to them. A tree is not a notebook.
