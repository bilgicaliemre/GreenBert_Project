The first entry in my heel notebook is my dad's work boots. The most recent one is number 61.

Every Saturday I work at my dad's shoe repair shop, Carvalho Shoe Service on Pleasant Street in Fall River. It has been there since 1994. I mostly do the counter and write tickets, but since last summer I'm allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. The shop smells like Barge cement and leather dust, and so does my hoodie. My friends say I smell like a "cowboy's basement."

You can tell a lot about a person from their shoes. If the outer edge of the heel is worn down, they supinate. One time a lady came in and her right heel was ground down way more than the left. I said out loud that she must drive for work, because your right heel rests on the floor by the pedal. She looked at me like I was a psychic. That's when I started keeping my notebook of heels.

But there is one part of the shop I have always avoided, and that is the phone. I have a stutter, and it gets worse on the phone, especially on hard sounds like "C." This is a problem when the shop is called Carvalho. So for years I answered "Hi, shoe repair" instead of the name. My dad noticed after like a year and never said anything.

Last month I finally said it. On the second try I almost hung up. I kept trying to picture the guy's face on the other end and if he was annoyed. On the third try it came out: "Carvalho Shoe Service." I thought I would feel proud, but honestly I mostly felt embarrassed that it took three tries. Then he just asked if we fix purses. (We don't.) That was the best part, that it was nothing to him.

The other person who has taught me about words is my avó, my grandmother Rosa. Two years ago she had a stroke and now she has aphasia. She understands more than she can say. I made her flashcards with pictures in Portuguese, and we do them every Tuesday and Thursday after school. It's funny because she is relearning the words and I am learning some of them for the first time. She is better than me at "agulha" (needle). I'm better at "telemóvel" (cell phone). Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, which was my aunt's name, who died before I was born. At first I corrected her. Now I don't. She can't say my name yet, but she can say "sapato," which means shoe.

With my avó I try to do the same thing I do with heels, which is figuring out what's going on with someone from the outside when they can't tell you. With heels it's easy. With her it isn't. When she gets a word wrong I can't tell what she's thinking.

This is why I want to study speech-language pathology, or maybe linguistics, or something with how the brain gets words back. I found out that stuttering and aphasia are totally different things, which I kind of wanted to be connected, and they're not. In middle school I asked my speech therapist, Ms. Halvorsen, if I would be "allowed" to be a speech therapist with a stutter. She said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet, but I want to find out.

Once I asked my dad if he wanted me to take over the shop. He said, "The shop isn't a person, it doesn't need anyone." I still don't know if that was a yes or a no. But I know the shop taught me how to pay attention to people, even when they can't say what they mean, and that is what I want to keep doing, whether it's with shoes or with words.
