"Ағаш дәптер емес."

My äje, my grandmother, said it fast while painting garden pitch on the black cut in her apple tree. I understood half and asked my dad in the car. It means "A tree is not a notebook."

I live in Almaty, Kazakhstan. "Alma" means apple, and Malus sieversii, the wild ancestor of almost every apple, grows in the mountains here. I have 37 wild trees tagged in the foothills. I can guess an apple's sugar, the Brix, from one bite. My mom tested me on 40 apples with a refractometer and I was within one point 31 times.

So I thought I knew a lot about apples. Last May I learned that apples don't grow true from seed, so a variety is kept by grafting. Every Aport, the famous Almaty apple, is technically the same tree, cloned for about 150 years. My äje has an old Aport at her dacha. At her 70th birthday table, I told her that her tree was "genetically boring." My uncle laughed. My äje didn't.

Two weeks later, when she was in town, I sawed off a big branch and grafted wild sieversii onto it. I cut the sticks inside the national park, which is not allowed, and carried them for two days in a hot backpack. Wrong season: they should be dormant, and mine had leaves and dried out. And I didn't ask her. The graft died and the cut went black.

Dad was away at Tengiz, the oil field, so I told him on a video call like it was a science update. He didn't say anything. When he came back he drove me to the dacha and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound. That is when she said it.

The grafting part I can explain as a science mistake. The birthday part I can't. I said "genetically boring" because I wanted the table to know I knew something. There is no science excuse for that. What I told nobody: I was kind of glad when it died. If it had taken, a wild branch would be on her tree forever, basically my signature, and she would look at it every summer.

My best wild tree was Tree 23, 15.2 Brix. I took a bag of its apples to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds and said, "This one has been to the city." It grows near a picnic spot, probably a hybrid with a dacha apple. I wanted to argue. I had 15.2 in my notebook, like that proves where a tree comes from. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good. I was angry at her and also at the apples, which makes no sense.

My äje calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, as a joke, because I answer her in Russian. Everybody laughs, me too. I think that is why Tree 23 bothered me. Half one thing, half the other, not the real wild thing. I got defensive for a tree. And with the Aport I did the opposite: called her tree boring for being a copy and tried to put the wild thing into it.

After the Aport, Gennady Pavlovich, who is 81 and sells apples at the Green Bazaar, taught me grafting properly. He made me do 200 cuts on willow sticks before he let me touch an apple. He says if a graft looks too neat, you didn't cut deep enough.

I want to study conservation genetics and be the person who decides which trees get saved. Zhanna Serikovna told me some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. If it were my decision, I don't know if I would cut Tree 23. Probably the correct answer is yes. Its number is in my notebook, and the notebook can't decide for me.
