At 6:52 p.m. on July 18th, our canopy went up like a kite and cartwheeled two booths over into Mr. Duarte's honey table. Fourteen jars broke. Our steamer tipped and six dozen tamales went into the gravel. A little girl with a mango paleta was right there, and her dad yanked her back. Nobody got hurt. The four sandbags that should have held the canopy down were still in the truck. They were my job.

Twelve minutes earlier I had told my whole family "trust me."

I built a weather station on our roof in Tucson. The first month it said it rained 19 days in June. It didn't rain once. The swamp cooler was dripping into the gauge. I emailed the weather service about my "unusual totals," and a man named Glen wrote back one line: "Do you have an evaporative cooler?" So I knew data can fool you. I want to study atmospheric science. I thought that made me the kind of person who checks.

My family sells tamales at the Saturday night market behind the Mercado. On the drive I'd seen a storm cell over the Rincons, so once the canopy legs were up I sat on the cooler with radar open. My mom told me to get the sandbags. I said "in a sec." Twice, I think.

Around 6:40, brown dust came from the east. My brother Gabe, who almost never works the stand, said pack up. I said no, it's splitting, it's going north, trust me. My mom laughed and said "la científica says we stay."

The rain did go north. We got maybe four drops. What hit us was the outflow, the wind that runs out ahead of a storm, literally what I'd read about all summer. I was so locked on the rain that the wind just wasn't in my head.

But that isn't the whole reason. On our fridge whiteboard, Gabe worked out that on a normal Sunday at our other market, Rillito, my mom makes $4.10 an hour. She has a disc problem in her back, and he wants her to stop the weekly markets. When he said pack up, I was thinking if we left and it missed, that's one more point for his whiteboard. If we stayed and it missed, I'd be right in front of him. I just wanted to win.

Gabe didn't say anything. He just started picking up the glass. Him yelling would've been easier. My mom paid Mr. Duarte $168 that night, cash out of her apron. At home I went straight to the roof to check my station: 47 miles an hour, which means nothing, since we live six miles away. My mom was at the kitchen table going through the husks to see if any were still okay. I was on the roof with my graph.

Once, fighting in the truck on the way to Rillito, Gabe said I want the stand to stay exactly the same so I can go away and it's still there when I come back, like my nana's chair. I told him that was stupid. But I'm the one who's probably leaving. And I'm also the one saying keep doing it, standing on the gravel every Sunday, with her back.

Now I put the sandbags on first, before I even look at the sky. I still check radar every five minutes. I just don't say anything. Maybe I changed, or maybe I just stopped saying it out loud. Probably that one.

In September my mom asked me, just me, if she should drop the Saturday market next summer. I said I'd look at the numbers. They're done, in a tab next to my rain data. I haven't said anything to her yet.

I've paid her back $60 of the $168. Gabe thinks I paid it all. She hasn't asked, not one time. Part of me wants her to.
