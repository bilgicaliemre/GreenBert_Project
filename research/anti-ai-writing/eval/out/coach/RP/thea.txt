I have played 48 funerals since June 2024. I know because I keep track.

I'm the organist at Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, Nebraska, and the funeral home calls me too, so a lot of Saturdays are a funeral at 10:30 and lunch in the basement. I play the pedals in socks because my shoes slip. I don't get sad at them, honestly. It's a job.

By February of junior year I had counted 31 funerals and 6 baptisms at Immanuel. I put them in Google Sheets, added a trend line, and posted it in the Cedar County Community Corner Facebook group: "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!" With a skull emoji. It got 63 comments. Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, wrote "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I replied "anecdotes aren't data," which I had learned from a YouTube video that same week. Then a man from Holy Trinity pointed out that the Catholics do most of the baptisms in town and Immanuel is basically the old people's church. My whole post was an anecdote.

I deleted it after four hours. My mom, the rural mail carrier, made me write Kathy an apology and delivered it with the mail. She made me put a real stamp on it so it was legal.

The part I didn't tell anyone is that I liked it. I kept refreshing the comments in Spanish class. It was the first time anything I made got read by more people than Arlene, my old piano teacher. I liked the mad ones best.

Then I went on the Census website to prove I was right and ended up reading about "natural decrease," which is when a county has more deaths than births. A lot of counties out here have it. It's a whole field, demography, with real methods for what I'd tried to do with one church. The annoying part: the county probably is shrinking. Just not like that. Not by my math. I wanted to know how to do it right. My school doesn't offer statistics, so I take it on Zoom through Northeast Community College.

I already do a little of it at work. Before Duane Pinkelman, the funeral director, meets with a family, we both guess which hymn they'll pick. I'm up 19 to 14. "How Great Thou Art" is the safe bet, about 40 percent.

At Leonard Tietz's funeral, a pipe got stuck on, and one low note kept going through the whole Lord's Prayer until I ran behind the curtain and shut off the blower. Pastor Jill said "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word," and everybody laughed, even his wife. I was kind of mad, which doesn't make sense. I think I wanted it to be my fault so I could fix it. After the lunch Duane held the ladder while I pulled the pipe. It was low F-sharp. There was a dead wasp inside. In the census, Leonard is minus one.

Duane says every single one was somebody's whole world. I don't know if I like the math because it makes it bigger, like all the counties at once, or because it makes it smaller and easier. Sometimes I think the spreadsheet is so I don't have to feel 48 separate things. And I think part of me wanted the town to be dying, because if it's dying anyway then leaving isn't really leaving anybody. The 2070 thing was me writing myself a permission slip with a trend line. I haven't figured out if I'm coming back after college.

Arlene was the organist at Immanuel for 51 years. She listens to the Sunday livestream from assisted living in Yankton and texts me afterward in all caps: "TOO FAST ON DOXOLOGY. BREATHE WITH THEM." I still don't know exactly what she means. I think I'm close.
