That's the real reason you stepped back, and it's where the whole "hiding" idea comes from. Right now it sits at the back of the TikTok paragraph, after the caption, the month of checking and the nine days. By the time the reader gets there, they've moved on to Khôi, the lawn chair and the TikTok, and the bakery moment goes by in four sentences.

It's also softer than what you told me. In our second session you said: "If we fell it would be on somebody's phone, so I stepped back before it could be a fall. A step back looks like a decision. I picked the one that looked better on video." The draft keeps "A step back looks like a decision" and drops "I picked the one that looked better on video," which is the actual admission. "A fall just looks like a fall" is in its place, and it says less.

Your step: give that moment its own paragraph. I'd keep the order your draft already uses, because it works. You tell the bakery the way the crowd saw it, and then each paragraph after that admits something you didn't say the first time (the Saturdays, the lawn chair, the TikTok). The filming thought is the last and worst of those, so it should come last, on its own, right before the "hiding" line. Here is the order I'd try. 4. Reflection lines

"I needed him to fold. If he folded, then the high stand was just hard and anybody would have messed it up. If he didn't, it was me." This is the best reflection in the essay, and the van scene right before it earns it. Yale's admissions officers say they look for students who "sit in the experience of being wrong about something." This paragraph does that. Leave it alone, except maybe the sentence before it, "It took me a long time to understand why I said that," which only announces what's coming. "I needed him to fold" can open the paragraph by itself.

"This experience taught me that I didn't like being the tail because it was chill. I liked it because it was hiding, and those are not the same thing." The idea is yours and it's the turn of the essay, so keep it. The frame isn't yours. "This experience taught me" is the most common move in AI-written admissions essays (and in a lot of human ones), and it isn't quite accurate here: the bakery didn't teach you this. You put it together later, from five years of saying "it's chill" and a month of checking a TikTok. Rewrite that frame your own way. One thing so you don't overcorrect: the "I said chill, but it was hiding" contrast is fine. I'd usually flag that kind of reversal, but you really did tell people "chill" for five years, and the reader just watched you say it.

"When people could finally see me in it, I stopped showing up." This claims more than you told me. You said: "The first Saturday I skipped was nine days after it went up. I'm not saying that's why. I'm just saying the dates." The draft turns "and then" into "because." I think your interview version is more honest and also stronger, since the nine days are already on the page and the reader can do the math. Decide how sure you are, and write the sentence to match.

6. Paragraph endings

Here is the last sentence of each paragraph, in order:

"That was my fault." / "...when the tail comes off and it's me." / "I literally let her down." / "That was on me." / "Khôi did the other twelve shows." / "And he didn't." / "Bảo could have landed on the curb." / "...I stopped showing up." / "It's a start."

Seven of the nine end on a short or quotable punch line; only paragraphs 2 and 5 end on plain content. Each one is good alone. When almost every paragraph lands on a punch line, it starts to sound like a drumbeat, and readers notice. Keep two. I'd keep "That was my fault." and whichever you like better of "And he didn't." and the curb line. Let the others end on their last fact, or fold them into the sentence before.

Also, "That was my fault." and "That was on me." say the same thing. Say it once. The opening is the better place.

9. Corrections

1. Paragraph 2: "I'm Mexican, my mom is from Zacatecas, so people always do a double take" is a comma splice (two full sentences joined by a comma). Separately, the "so" only works if the reader knows the team is Vietnamese, and the draft never says so. A reader who doesn't recognize Vietnamese names won't know why anyone does a double take. Fix the punctuation, and decide whether the reader needs that one fact first.
2. Paragraph 2: "My best friend Bảo-Trân is the head and her dad" → add a comma before "and": "is the head, and her dad".
3. Paragraph 3: "for Tết". From "February" and "red envelope," some readers will guess it's a holiday and some won't. Ask someone who doesn't know the word. If they're lost, say once, inside the sentence, that it's the Vietnamese Lunar New Year.

5. Paragraph 9: "an answer about this Tết". A reader could take "this Tết" to mean last February's. "next Tết" removes that.
