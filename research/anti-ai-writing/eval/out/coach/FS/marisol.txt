1. The biggest problem: the ending settles things you haven't settled

The draft ends: "But when I do, I want it to be what the numbers actually say, not what I want them to say. I think that is what being a scientist actually means." The first sentence is a promise and the second is a moral. That moral could close any science applicant's essay, and admissions readers see that kind of line all the time.

It also doesn't match what you told me. You said the numbers are done and "I just haven't said anything back to her yet." And when I asked what you do differently now, you said: "I don't announce forecasts at the stand anymore. I still check radar like every five minutes. I just don't say anything. So, I don't know. Maybe I changed, or maybe I just stopped saying it out loud. Probably that one."

That answer is the best reflection you gave me, and it isn't in the draft. The prompt asks for the outcome, and that's an honest one: a real change (sandbags first, no more forecasts out loud) and a real doubt about how deep the change goes.

Steps:
1. Delete those last two sentences.
2. After "Now I put the sandbags on first, before I even look at the sky," write what you do and don't do at the stand now, and your doubt about it. Your wording above is already good; trim it only for grammar.
3. Your sentence "I haven't told her yet." can be the end, or close to it. If you want to say why you haven't, answer question 1 at the bottom first.


2. What a reader knows about you afterward

That you build things, you check your data, and you can admit you wanted to beat your brother. 3. Shape and opening

The opening works: a time-stamped scene, damage you can count, and "Putting them on was my job." confessing before the reader knows anything else about you.

The turn works too, in two steps. First you were "kind of right" about the rain and wrong about the wind, and then "that isn't the whole reason." Keep that order. I wouldn't move any paragraphs.
