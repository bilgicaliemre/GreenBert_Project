Hi Ansar,

This is a strong first draft. It has a real story, a real mistake, and details nobody else applying this year will have. The best line in it is: "There is no science excuse for that." In that line you stop defending yourself, and that's what admissions readers remember.

So the problems aren't about the material. They're about where your words go. You're at 644 words and the limit is 650, so anything you add has to come from somewhere else. Most of what follows is about making room for the parts that matter most.

I'm not going to rewrite sentences for you. The essay needs to sound like you, and right now it does. Instead I'll point at specific places and ask questions.


WHAT'S WORKING (don't cut these)

- The opening. Starting in Kazakh, then "I understood half and asked my dad in the car," is excellent. It also quietly sets up "shala-kazakh" later, because you only half understood your own grandmother. Don't explain that connection. Let the reader notice it.
- The concrete details: 37 tags, 31 out of 40, 200 willow cuts, "the kommunalka," "when she was in town." That last one is quiet but damning, and it works.
- "I told him on a video call like it was a science update. He didn't say anything." Your dad's silence says more than a lecture would have.
- Gennady's line about neat grafts. It's the best line anyone says in the essay, and (see point 4) it may be telling you something about your ending.


THE FOUR BIG CHANGES, MOST IMPORTANT FIRST

1. Shrink paragraph 2 to make room.

The "I live in Almaty" paragraph is 130 words, the longest in the essay, and most of it is scheduling: Saturday school, dad weeks, 5:30 a.m., the bus, 40 minutes walking. The reader needs three things from it: Almaty means apple, you study wild apples seriously, and your dad works away at Tengiz (because you need that later). Keep the Brix test with your mom. It's your best fun fact, and it sets up the pride that the whole story is about ("So I thought I knew a lot"). The travel logistics can mostly go. A rough guess is that you can free up 40 to 60 words here without losing anything the story needs.

2. You left out the most important thing you told me.

In our second session you said you were "kind of glad when it died," because a wild branch would have stayed on her tree forever, "basically my signature," and she'd have looked at it every summer.

That's the center of this essay, and it isn't in the draft. It's also what makes your title line make sense. A notebook is something you write your own name in. Right now the reader has to guess why the tree "is not a notebook." You already know the answer.

This is your call, and it's uncomfortable to put on paper. But compare it with the line that already works: "There is no science excuse for that." Same kind of honesty, one step further. If you add it, it probably belongs near paragraph 6, where you're already admitting the birthday part.

(You also mentioned telling your dad you cut the sticks "at the edge of the park" when it was well inside. You don't need that one. The "glad it died" admission does the same job better.)

3. Give your realization room, and give Tree 23 a real scene.

The shala-kazakh paragraph is the most original idea in the essay: you got defensive when your best wild tree turned out to be half city apple, and then you called the Aport boring for being a copy. Right now it gets 63 words, and it comes after the Gennady paragraph, which separates it from the confession it grows out of.

Two things to try:

a) Tree 23 gets half a sentence ("probably a hybrid with some city apple"), but you told me a whole scene: Zhanna Serikovna looked at it for ten seconds and said "This one has been to the city." You wanted to argue with her because you had 15.2 in your notebook. Then on the bus home you ate three of them, angry at her and at the apples. That scene shows "I got really defensive" so you don't have to tell it. Even three or four sentences of it would help a lot. (And notice that the word "notebook" turns up there too.)

b) Try moving the Gennady paragraph after the shala-kazakh paragraph. Then the order goes: what I did, the part with no excuse, what I figured out about myself, and the slow period of learning to do it properly. That last part is the "period of personal growth" the prompt asks for. Try it both ways and read them out loud.

You end that paragraph with "I am still figuring out what that says about me." Not having the full answer is fine, even good. But give the reader one tentative step past it, in your own words. You came close in our session ("I got defensive for a tree... But I think it's true").

4. The ending is a little too neat. Gennady already warned you about that.

Right now the last paragraph does four things: a career goal, the USDA orchard, a stated lesson, and the proverb repeated. It's tidy, and that's the problem. "If a graft looks too neat, you didn't cut deep enough."

Two things to think about:

- You say you've learned to "ask whose tree it is, and what it means to them." But in the essay you never ask äje what the Aport means to her. Did you? If yes, what did she say? That could be your ending. If no, that's worth knowing too.
- You have an unresolved question that's much better than a stated lesson. You want to be "the person who decides which trees get saved." Zhanna Serikovna told you some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. You don't know if you'd cut Tree 23. You said "probably the correct answer is yes." That's a real dilemma, and it ties the science, the defensiveness, and the half-and-half feeling together without you having to explain it.

"Our apples live in New York" is a great sentence. It may fit better in a "Why this college" or "Why this major" supplement, where you'd have room for it.


SMALLER LINE NOTES

- "My grandmother" in paragraph 1 becomes "My äje" in paragraph 3. A reader won't know they're the same person. Say what äje means the first time you use it.
- "The city is named like that because..." isn't natural English. "Named after" or "named for" is the usual phrase.
- "carried them two days" should be "carried them for two days."
- Last paragraph: "before I decide anything about any tree, I learned I have to ask..." mixes tenses in a confusing way. Read it out loud and you'll hear it.
- "It took me a long time to understand what she meant" (paragraph 1) tells us something the essay never shows. The essay covers a few weeks plus whatever came after. Cut it, or make sure the rest of the essay earns it.
- Casual phrases like "like 150 years" and "the Aport thing" are fine. They sound like you. Just don't let "like" show up more than once or twice.
- "(yes, we have school on Saturday)" is charming. If you need the words, it's an easy cut.


THINGS I'D LEAVE OUT OF THIS ESSAY

You have more good material than 650 words can hold. The 21:00 photo from your dad, the nardy score on the banana box, saying "alma" into the microphone at KVN: all good, all different essays. About the photo, you said yourself you "could be making that up because it sounds good." Trust that instinct here. Save it for a supplement or another prompt.


QUESTIONS TO ANSWER BEFORE YOUR NEXT DRAFT

Write rough answers, a few sentences each. Don't polish them.

1. After the graft died, did you say anything to äje? An apology, anything? What did she say or do?
2. Have you ever asked her what the Aport tree means to her?
3. Have you grafted something since then that Gennady approved of? What was it?
4. If you had to decide about Tree 23 tomorrow, what would you do, and how would it feel?

3. Decide whether "kind of glad when it died" goes in. If it does, write it in your own words near the birthday confession.
