Hi Nayeli,

This is a strong first draft. You open on a lion eating lettuce off a broom and take the blame in the very next sentence. After that you keep letting yourself look bad, and you never ask the reader to feel sorry for you. Most of my notes push you further in the direction you're already going. You don't need to change course.

Two quick things before the notes:

- The draft is exactly 650 words, which is the Common App limit. Anything you add has to be paid for with a cut. I've listed cut candidates near the end.
- I'm not going to rewrite your sentences for you. Your voice is the best thing in this essay, and it has to stay yours.

## What's working (protect these when you revise)

1. The opening. A ridiculous image, then "That was my fault." The reader is hooked.
2. The details. The mirror rolling under a Camry, the strip by the Dollar Tree, cymbals being "what they give the little kids," Khôi at 110 pounds. Nobody else could have written these.
3. The order of the confessions. Each paragraph admits something worse than the one before it. Your knee shook. Then you'd skipped six of eight practices. Then you traded shifts so the excuse would technically be true. Then the lawn chair line. Then you were thinking about who was filming. That build is what pulls the reader through, so keep it.
4. "I needed him to fold." The lawn chair paragraph is the smartest thinking in the essay. You explain your own bad logic without defending it.
5. "The first Saturday I skipped was nine days after it went up." You give the dates and let the reader connect them. That's restraint, and it works.
6. A setup and payoff you may not have noticed. In paragraph 2, people do a double take "when the tail comes off and it's me." In the TikTok paragraph the same thing happens, except this time it's on camera. That echo ties the whole story to being seen.
7. The ending doesn't pretend you're fixed. Going from 4:10 to 1:52 is honest and specific.

## The big things

### 1. Bảo disappears

She's the person you actually let down, and she's the one who could have landed on the curb. But after paragraph 3 she's barely in the essay. Right now the story is mostly about your pride (Khôi, the TikTok), while she's the one who paid for it.

When we talked, you told me the part you'd rather leave out. When the mirror rolled under the Camry you laughed inside the lion, and Bảo heard it. In the van she didn't look at you, "and she always looks at me in the van." You also said the two of you have never talked about it, even though you text every day.

I know you said there's no good version of that story and it sounds like you didn't care. But look at what the essay already admits: you stepped back partly because of how it would look on video. A reader who has taken that in can handle a nervous laugh. You also don't have to explain it. What you told me ("I did care. Maybe that's why I laughed? I don't know.") is honest, and an essay is allowed to leave a question open.

The van is already a scene in your essay, since that's where you made the lawn chair comment. It's also where Bảo wasn't looking at you. So this wouldn't need a new scene, just more of one you already have.

This is your call. If it feels too exposed, leave it out. But I think this is what would take the essay from very good to memorable.

### 2. Say the filming part as plainly as you said it to me

The draft says: "A step back looks like a decision. A fall just looks like a fall." When we talked, you said it more directly: you picked the one that looked better on video. In the draft, the reader has to work out for themselves that the step back was about the camera. That's the biggest admission in the essay, and right now it's tucked into the middle of the TikTok paragraph. Think about giving it more room (maybe its own short paragraph) and saying it the way you said it out loud.

Also read those two sentences aloud. "A step back looks like a decision" is great. Ask yourself whether "A fall just looks like a fall" adds anything.

### 3. The one sentence that doesn't sound like you

"This experience taught me that..." is the only line in the essay that sounds like a college essay instead of like you. It borrows the prompt's wording, and admissions readers see that phrase all the time. It also repeats "chill" from the paragraph right above it. What comes after it ("I liked it because it was hiding, and those are not the same thing. When people could finally see me in it, I stopped showing up.") is strong and can stand on its own. Try deleting that first sentence and see what's left.

### 4. Let the ending end

"It's a start." tells the reader how to feel about 1:52, but the numbers already do that. Try ending one sentence earlier and read it aloud both ways.

Optional, if you free up room: you told me you still have the furry tail pants, Chú Tâm never asked for them back, and you sleep in them. That's pretty direct evidence on your "do I actually miss it" question.

## A pattern worth thinking about

You don't have to put this in. It's just a question. The essay is about hiding in the tail, but the draft also shows you hiding in jokes. You close the worst moment with "I literally let her down." You said the TikTok "was funny, whatever." There's the laugh inside the lion, and (from our talk) you Venmo'd Khôi ten dollars "for takis" instead of saying sorry. Do you see that too?

If you do, you don't need to spell it out. Just ask yourself whether the pun at the end of paragraph 3 is the essay doing the same thing you did inside the lion. Your humor is a real strength, so don't strip it out. Maybe just let the worst moment not be a joke.

The takis Venmo, by the way, is short and really good. It shows the apology you never made, and it's funny and sad at the same time.

## Smaller notes

- "In December someone posted a TikTok..." You've already said "Last February," so a reader might stop and wonder which December. Make it clear it was a couple of months before Tết.
- "That was my fault" (paragraph 1) and "That was on me" (paragraph 4) are the same beat. You probably only need one.
- "It took me a long time to understand why I said that." Is that true, or did it click more recently? Either way, starting the paragraph with "I needed him to fold." hits harder without the warm-up.

## What I'd leave out

- The ice machines and your dad. You said you love the ice line and that's exactly why you don't trust it. I agree. It's not this essay. It could make a great "why engineering" supplemental, though.
- The Friday announcements ("nobody sees you"). It fits the hiding pattern, but bringing in a new setting this late would crowd the ending.

## Word budget

You're at 650. These are the lines doing the least work:

- "This experience taught me that I didn't like being the tail because it was chill." (16 words)
- "It took me a long time to understand why I said that." (12)
- "A fall just looks like a fall." (7)
- One of "That was my fault." / "That was on me." (4)
- "It's a start." (3)

That's about 40 words. It's enough for the laugh and the van, or for the takis Venmo, but probably not both plus the tail pants. Don't try to add everything. Part of why this draft works is that it's tight.
