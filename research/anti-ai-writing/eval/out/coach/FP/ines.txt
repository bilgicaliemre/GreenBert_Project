Hi Inês,

This is a strong first draft. I'm going to be direct about what works and what doesn't, because that's what will help. I'm not going to rewrite sentences for you. The essay has to sound like you, and a lot of it already does.

Two quick facts first:
- Your draft is exactly 650 words, which is the Common App limit. Anything you add means cutting something else.
- You were right to leave out your GPA, Science Olympiad, cross-country and Portuguese Club. Those belong in the Activities section, so keep them out of this essay.


WHAT'S WORKING (don't lose these)

1. Your details. Barge cement, "cowboy's basement," 61 entries, the right heel resting by the pedal, the purse guy, "agulha" vs. "telemóvel," "sapato." Nobody else could write these sentences.
2. You don't fake a happy ending. "I thought I would feel proud, but honestly I mostly felt embarrassed that it took three tries." A lot of essays about stuttering end with "and I found my voice." Yours admits it was awkward and the guy only wanted to know about purses. That honesty is your best quality as a writer.
3. "That was the best part, that it was nothing to him." Keep it.
4. "She can't say my name yet, but she can say 'sapato,' which means shoe." This is probably the best sentence in the essay.
5. Your dad's answer about the shop, and the fact that you admit you don't know what he meant.


THE MAIN PROBLEM

Right now the essay is four pieces in a row: heels, the phone, your avó, why you want to study speech therapy. Then paragraph 7 and the last sentence try to glue them together by telling the reader what they have in common.

Look at paragraph 7: "With my avó I try to do the same thing I do with heels..." When we talked before, you said: "I don't know if that's a real connection or if I just want it to be one, like the stutter and the aphasia." In the essay you admit the stutter/aphasia link isn't real, but you present the heels/avó link as if you're sure of it. Readers can tell when a connection is being announced instead of shown.

The last sentence has the same problem ("But I know the shop taught me how to pay attention to people..."). It's the only sentence that sounds like it was written for an admissions officer. Everything before it is honest and unsure, and then it turns into a lesson. "I still don't know if that was a yes or a no" is a stronger place to land.


SOMETHING IN YOUR DRAFT YOU MAY NOT HAVE NOTICED

Read these four moments together:
- You couldn't say "Carvalho" on the phone. That's your own last name, not just the shop's.
- You said "Hi, shoe repair" instead.
- Your avó calls you Lurdes, and you stopped correcting her.
- She can't say your name yet.

That's a name you couldn't say, a name you swapped out, a name you let her use, and your name she can't say. You didn't plan that, which is exactly why it might be the real thread. "Reading people from the outside" might be what you want the essay to be about. Names might be what it's actually about. I could be wrong, and you should decide, but reread the draft with that in mind.

Two questions that would make the essay stronger whichever way you go:
- Why did you stop correcting her? The draft says "At first I corrected her. Now I don't." and moves on. That's a big decision, and the reader wants to know what changed. Your answer might be the most important two sentences in the essay.
- Readers will see your name on your application, but the essay never points at the fact that Carvalho is you. Do you want it to?


THE BEST IDEA YOU LEFT OUT

When we talked, you said: "I think 'Hi, shoe repair' was a lie too. A small one." And: you were "swapping the true thing for an easier thing." That's the most original thought you've had about any of this, and it isn't in the essay.

But do you actually believe it? Here's a question worth sitting with. Ms. Halvorsen would probably call swapping a hard word for an easier one a coping strategy, not a lie. You want to become someone who helps people who stutter. If a client of yours did it, would you call it a lie? Whatever your honest answer is, only you could write that. Even one or two sentences of it in the phone section would turn the essay from "I overcame my stutter" into something readers haven't seen before.

About the chemistry story and the lie list: the list (212 items, mostly about being on time) is a great detail. I don't think the full story fits, though. It would take 150+ words you don't have, and a forged signature is a judgment call in an admissions essay that needs room to handle well. Your options:
  a) Leave it out. The phone idea works without it.
  b) Mention the list in one sentence, with no backstory.
  c) Save the whole story for a supplement that asks about a mistake or about honesty.
I'd lean toward (a) or (c), but it's your call.


SMALLER NOTES, PARAGRAPH BY PARAGRAPH

1. Opening. Good hook, but entry 1 is your dad's boots and we never find out what they told you. Your ending is about not being able to read your dad. If the opening showed you reading his boots, the first and last paragraphs would talk to each other. Right now they don't.
2. Paragraph 3. "You can tell a lot about a person from their shoes" is the most generic sentence in the essay, and your examples already prove it. Also: you say you started the notebook after the driving lady, but entry 1 is your dad's boots. A careful reader might stop and wonder why. If there's a reason, a few words would cover it.
3. The phone scene (paragraphs 4-5). This is your big moment, and it's mostly summary. Slow it down. You told me "the guy waited." That's not in the draft, and it's the best thing about him. We also hear about the second and third tries but never the first.
4. Paragraph 6. "The other person who has taught me about words." Other than who? This transition shows the seam between two separate stories. Look for a way into your avó that grows out of the phone paragraph.
5. Paragraph 8. "This is why I want to study..." turns the essay into a "why this major" answer. Keep the good parts: you wanted stuttering and aphasia to be connected and they aren't, and "I don't fully believe her yet." You also name three possible fields. Pick one, or keep only "how the brain gets words back," which sounds the most like you.
6. Filler words. "like a year," "honestly," "kind of," "totally," "It's funny because." A couple of these sound like you, and that's fine. Five of them start to sound careless. Keep the ones you'd really say out loud.
