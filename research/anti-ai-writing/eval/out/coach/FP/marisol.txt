Marisol, this is a strong first draft. The hardest part of this prompt is admitting something unflattering about yourself, and you already did it: "I just wanted to win." A lot of essays on this prompt never get that honest. So most of my feedback is about the parts around that line, not the line itself.

One thing to know first: you're at 647 words and the limit is 650. Anything you add has to come out of something else. I'll point to where.

**What's working (don't lose these)**

- The opening. 6:52, fourteen jars, the girl with the mango paleta. The reader is standing in the market with you by the second sentence.
- "Twelve minutes earlier I had told my whole family 'trust me.'" Keep it as its own one-line paragraph. It does a lot of work.
- Glen and the swamp cooler. It's funny, and it sets up the whole essay: you believed you were "the kind of person who checks," and the rest of the story tests that.
- The twist that your forecast was right about the rain and you still got hit by the wind. That's a scientist's mistake, not a careless one, and it's much more interesting than "I was wrong."
- "La científica says we stay." Your mom handed you the authority. That's why the mistake matters.
- Your mom at the kitchen table going through the husks while you're on the roof with your graph. This is the best image in the essay. It already says what the "That night I had to question something I believed about myself" paragraph tries to explain.

**1. The ending is neater than what you told me**

"Sandbags first, before I even look at the sky" is a great concrete detail. Keep it. The problem is what comes after it. The draft ends on a lesson: "I think that is what being a scientist actually means."

When we talked, you said something different and better. You still check radar every five minutes, you just don't say anything. Then: "Maybe I changed, or maybe I just stopped saying it out loud. Probably that one."

That's the real outcome, and it's more believable than a lesson. Admissions readers see a lot of essays where the writer comes out fully transformed. An essay that ends with "I'm not sure yet, and here's the thing I still haven't done" stands out because it sounds true.

You already have the unfinished thing: the numbers for your mom are done, in a tab next to your rain data, and you haven't told her. Right now the essay says you want your answer to be "what the numbers actually say." A reader will want to know two things you didn't write down:
- What do the numbers say?
- Why haven't you told her yet?

You don't have to answer those neatly in the essay. But you need to know the answers before you write the last paragraph, because the last paragraph depends on them. I'd cut the final sentence about what being a scientist means. The reader can get there without you saying it.

**2. "I just wanted to win." Why?**

This is the center of the essay, and it stops one step short. The reader learns that you wanted to beat Gabe, but not why beating him on this particular thing mattered so much.

You told me about the fight in the truck, when Gabe said you want the stand to stay exactly the same so it's still there when you come back, "like my nana's chair." Then you said: "I'm the one who's leaving, probably. And I'm also the one saying keep doing it, standing on the gravel every Sunday, with her back." And then you said you didn't know where you were going with that.

I think that might be where the essay is going. I'm not going to tell you what it means, because only you know. But it's worth sitting with. You don't need to resolve it. Even one or two honest sentences admitting that you're arguing to keep something you're about to leave would move the essay from "I was stubborn" to something much deeper. If it feels too raw to put in, that's okay. It's your call.

Notice that your outflow paragraph already has this shape: you were so locked on the rain that the wind wasn't in your head. You could ask yourself what else wasn't in your head that night. Just don't spell the comparison out for the reader. If it's there, they'll see it.

**3. Show what it cost the people around you**

Right now the damage in the essay is all things: jars, tamales, husks. The people mostly disappear after the canopy lands. Two things you told me could fix that in very few words:
- Gabe didn't say anything. He just started picking up the glass. (You also said him yelling would have been easier. That's a great line.)
- Your mom paid Mr. Duarte the $168 that night, in cash, out of her apron.

The fact that you've paid back $60 and she hasn't asked once is also powerful, and it fits an unfinished ending. You said you haven't told anyone that, so only use it if you're comfortable with admissions officers reading it.

**Where to find the words**

- The "That night I had to question something I believed about myself" paragraph (34 words). The roof and the kitchen table already showed this. Try cutting it completely and see if you miss it.
- The weather station paragraph (111 words, your longest). Keep Glen and the swamp cooler, but the list of parts can shrink. The reader doesn't need every component to get the point.
- "(not the TV kind, every adult asks that)." It's funny, but it pulls attention away right when the reader is still processing the storm.
- The last sentence of the essay.

That frees up roughly 50 to 70 words without losing anything important.

**Smaller things**

- The jump from the opening scene to "I want to study atmospheric science" is sudden. It reads a little like switching into résumé mode. Try starting that paragraph with the station on the roof and letting the major come through it, or tie it back to the storm.
- Saturday vs. Sunday: the story is at the Saturday night market, but Gabe's $4.10 is about "a normal Sunday." A reader who doesn't know you also do Rillito on Sundays will get confused. A short phrase fixes it.
- "I just wanted to win" and "Really I was using the radar to win an argument" say the same thing. Keep the first one. It hits harder.

**Before your next draft, answer these for yourself (on paper, not in the essay)**

1. What do the numbers in the tab say?
2. Why haven't you told your mom yet?
3. When Gabe said the thing about your nana's chair, what part didn't you want to hear?
4. Did you change, or did you just stop saying it out loud? You answered this once already. Is that still your answer?

Your voice is the best thing about this essay. It sounds like you: a little funny, a little blunt, specific about numbers. When you revise, don't let it get more formal. Send me the next draft when you have it and I'll go through it again.
