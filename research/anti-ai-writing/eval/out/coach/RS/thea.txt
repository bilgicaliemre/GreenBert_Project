I have played 48 funerals since June 2024. I know because I keep track.

I'm the organist at Immanuel Lutheran in Hartington, Nebraska, and the funeral home calls me too, so a lot of my Saturdays are a funeral at 10:30, then the lunch in the basement. Before Duane Pinkelman, the funeral director, meets with a family, we each guess which hymn they'll pick. I'm up 19 to 14. "How Great Thou Art" is the safe bet, about 40 percent. I don't get sad at funerals, honestly. It's a job.

By February of junior year I'd counted 31 funerals and 6 baptisms at Immanuel. I made a Google Sheets chart with a trend line and posted it in the Cedar County Community Corner Facebook group: "At this rate Immanuel is empty by 2045 and Hartington by about 2070. Just math!" With a skull emoji. It got 63 comments. Kathy Wieseler, who runs Little Sprouts daycare, wrote "Come count my 22 kids, honey." I replied "anecdotes aren't data," which I'd learned from a YouTube video that week. Then a man from Holy Trinity pointed out the Catholics do most of the town's baptisms and Immanuel is basically the old people's church. I had only counted my church. My whole post was an anecdote.

I deleted it after four hours, but first I screenshotted Kathy's comment and sent it to the quiz bowl chat to make fun of her. My mom, the rural mail carrier, has Kathy on her route, so she made me write an apology and delivered it with the mail. She made me put a real stamp on it so it was legal.

What I didn't tell anyone is that I liked it. I kept refreshing the comments in Spanish class. It was the first time anything I made got read by more people than Arlene, my old piano teacher. Even the mad ones. Especially the mad ones, which is bad, I know.

I went on the Census website to prove I was right and ended up reading about "natural decrease," when a county has more deaths than births, which a lot of counties out here have. That's a whole field: demography. The county probably is shrinking. Just not like that, and not by my math. My school doesn't offer statistics, so I'm taking it on Zoom through Northeast Community College.

At Leonard Tietz's funeral, a pipe got stuck on, and one low note kept going through the whole Lord's Prayer. I was sure everybody would think I did it. I ran behind the curtain and shut off the blower. Pastor Jill said, "Well, Leonard always did like to have the last word," and everybody laughed, even his wife. I was kind of mad, which doesn't make sense. I think I wanted it to be my fault so I could fix it. After the lunch, Duane held the ladder while I pulled the pipe. It was low F-sharp. There was a dead wasp inside. In the census, Leonard is minus one.

Duane says every single one was somebody's whole world. I don't know if I like the math because it makes the dying bigger, like all the counties at once, or smaller and easier. Sometimes I think the spreadsheet is so I don't have to feel 48 separate things.

I think part of me wanted the town to be dying. If it's dying anyway, then leaving isn't really leaving anybody. The 2070 thing was me writing myself a permission slip with a trend line. I still don't know if I'm coming back after college.

Arlene Thoene, the organist at Immanuel for 51 years before me, taught me pedals. She listens to the Sunday livestream from assisted living in Yankton and texts me afterward in all caps: "TOO FAST ON DOXOLOGY. BREATHE WITH THEM." I still don't know exactly what she means. I think I'm close.
