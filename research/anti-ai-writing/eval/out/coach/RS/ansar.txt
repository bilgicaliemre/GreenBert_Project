At her 70th birthday last May, I told my äje, my grandmother, that her apple tree was "genetically boring." My uncle laughed. My äje didn't.

Her tree is an old Aport, the famous Almaty apple, at her dacha. "Alma" means apple, and the city is named that because Malus sieversii, the wild ancestor of basically every apple, just grows in the mountains here. I have 37 wild trees tagged near Talgar. I measure their Brix, percent sugar in the juice. I'd just learned that apples don't grow true from seed. Every seed is a new apple, so a variety is kept by grafting. That means every Aport is technically the same tree, cloned for like 150 years.

Two weeks later, while äje was away from the dacha, I sawed off a big branch and grafted wild sieversii onto it. I cut the sticks inside the national park, which is not allowed. Later I told my dad it was "at the edge of the park." It was pretty far inside. It was also the wrong season: the sticks should have been dormant, but mine had leaves and dried out after two days in my backpack in the heat. And I didn't ask her. The graft died and the cut went black. I told nobody that I was kind of glad. If it had taken, there would be a wild branch on her tree forever that was basically my signature, and she would look at it every summer.

My dad was at Tengiz, the oil field where he works fourteen days on, fourteen off, so I told him on a video call like it was a science update. He didn't say anything. When he came back, he drove me to the dacha and made me sit with äje while she painted the wound with garden pitch. She said something fast in Kazakh. I understood half of it and asked my dad in the car. "Ағаш дәптер емес." A tree is not a notebook.

The grafting part I can explain as a science mistake. The birthday part has no science excuse. I said "genetically boring" to sound clever in front of my cousins. I wanted the whole table to know I knew something.

After that, Gennady Pavlovich, 81, who sells apples at the Green Bazaar, made me do 200 cuts on willow sticks before he let me touch an apple.

My best wild tree was Tree 23, 15.2 Brix. I took a bag to Zhanna Serikovna, a researcher at the botanical garden. She cut one open, looked for ten seconds and said, "This one has been to the city." It grows 30 meters from a picnic spot, probably a hybrid with some dacha apple. I wanted to argue. I had 15.2 in my notebook, like that proves where a tree comes from. She was being nice, and I was thinking, it's still a good apple. On the bus home I ate three. They were really good. I was angry at her and also at the apples, which makes no sense.

My äje calls me "shala-kazakh," half-Kazakh, as a joke, because I answer her in Russian. Everybody laughs, me too. I think Tree 23 bothered me because of that. A city one. Half one thing, half the other, not the real wild thing. I got defensive for a tree. That's a bit much, but I think it's true. With the Aport I did the opposite: called her tree boring for being a copy and tried to put the wild thing into it. I don't know what that says about me. Maybe nothing.

Zhanna Serikovna told me some places cut out hybrids so they don't pollinate the wild trees. I want to study conservation genetics and be the person who decides which trees get saved. If Tree 23 was my decision, I don't know if I would cut it. Probably the correct answer is yes.
