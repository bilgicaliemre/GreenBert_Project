At 6:52 p.m. on July 18, our canopy went up like a kite. It cartwheeled two booths over and landed on Mr. Duarte's honey table. Fourteen jars broke. Our steamer tipped over, and six dozen tamales went into the gravel. A little girl with a mango paleta was right there, and her dad yanked her back. Nobody got hurt. The four sandbags that should have held our canopy down were still in the truck. Putting them on was my job.

A few minutes earlier I had told my mom and my brother, "Trust me."

I want to study atmospheric science. On our roof in Tucson I built a weather station, with an anemometer made from ping-pong balls. The first month it said it rained 19 days in June. It didn't rain once. It was the swamp cooler dripping into the gauge. I emailed the weather service about my "unusual totals" and a man named Glen wrote back one line: "Do you have an evaporative cooler?" So I already knew data can fool you. I thought that made me the kind of person who checks.

My family sells tamales at the Saturday night market behind the Mercado in the summer, and on Sundays at Rillito all year. I had seen a storm cell over the Rincon Mountains on the drive, so once the canopy legs were up I sat on the cooler with radar open. My mom told me to get the sandbags. I said, "In a sec," twice, I think.

Around 6:40 we could see brown dust coming from the east. Gabe, who almost never works the stand, said we should pack up. I said, "No, it's splitting, it's going north, trust me." My mom laughed and said, "La científica says we stay."

The rain did go north. We got maybe four drops. My forecast was kind of right. What hit us was the outflow, the wind that runs out ahead of a storm, which is literally what I had been reading about all summer. I was so locked on where the rain was going that the wind just wasn't in my head.

But honestly that isn't the whole reason. Gabe did the stand's numbers on our fridge whiteboard, and on a normal Sunday my mom makes $4.10 an hour. She has a disc problem in her back, and he wants her to stop the weekly markets. When he said "pack up," I was thinking that if we left and it missed, that would be one more point for his whiteboard, like see, the stand even loses nights. If we stayed and it missed, I'd be right in front of him. I just wanted to win.

Afterward, Gabe didn't say anything. He just started picking up the glass. My mom paid Mr. Duarte $168 that night, cash, out of her apron, and I said I'd pay her back. We drove home and nobody talked. I went straight up on the roof to see what my station got. Forty-seven miles an hour, which means nothing, since our house is six miles away. My mom was at the kitchen table going through the husks to see if any were still okay. I was on the roof with my graph.

Now I put the sandbags on first, before I even look at the sky. I don't announce forecasts at the stand anymore. I still check radar like every five minutes. I just don't say anything. So maybe I changed, or maybe I just stopped saying it out loud. Probably that one.

I'm at $60 of the $168. My mom hasn't asked once, and part of me wants her to, which is weird. In September she asked me, just me, if she should drop the Saturday night market next summer. I said I'd look at the numbers. They're done, in a tab next to my rain data. I haven't told her yet.
