I hate the phone. I have a stutter that gets worse on the phone, especially on hard sounds like C. That's a problem, because my dad's shop is called Carvalho Shoe Service, and Carvalho is also my last name. So when the shop phone rang on Saturdays, I picked up and said, "Hi, shoe repair," instead of the name. My dad noticed after about a year and never said anything about it.

The shop has been on Pleasant Street in Fall River since 1994. It smells like Barge cement and leather dust, and so does my hoodie; my friends say I smell like a "cowboy's basement." I mostly work the counter and write tickets, and since last summer I've been allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine (I can resole a sneaker in 25 minutes). I can also tell how someone walks from the wear on their heel. An outer edge worn down means they supinate. One woman's right heel was ground down much more than her left, and I said out loud that she must drive for work, since the right heel rests on the floor by the pedal. She did, and she looked at me like I was a psychic. I keep a notebook of heels with 61 entries, and entry 1 is my dad's work boots.

My dad's right thumb is stained black from the dye. When I asked him once if he wanted me to take over the shop, he said, "The shop isn't a person, it doesn't need anyone," and I don't know if that was a yes or a no.

Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept that up for three weeks and even forged the parent signature on my progress report, until my teacher, Mr. Oduya, emailed home. My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test to her, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese, which I'm not good at for chemistry words. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed at that part.

On Tuesdays and Thursdays after school, I do flashcards with my avó, Rosa. She had a stroke two years ago and has aphasia; she understands more than she can say. I made the cards myself, with pictures and Portuguese words. It's funny, because she's relearning the words and I'm learning them for the first time: she's better than me at "agulha" (needle), and I'm better at "telemóvel" (cell phone). Sometimes she calls me Lurdes. That was my aunt's name, and my aunt died before I was born. At first I corrected her. Now I let it go, the way my dad let "Hi, shoe repair" go. She can't say Inês yet. She can say "sapato" (shoe).

I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics, or something with how the brain gets words back. I read a lot about aphasia now, and I found out that stuttering and aphasia are totally different things. I kind of wanted them to be connected. They're not. Avó is relearning words she lost, and on the phone I know exactly which word I want; it's my last name. I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my speech therapist from middle school, whether I'd be "allowed" to be a speech therapist with a stutter. She said some of the best ones stutter, and I don't fully believe her yet.

Until last month, I had never once answered the shop phone with the name. When I finally did, "Carvalho Shoe Service" took three tries, and the man on the other end waited. He was calling to ask if we fix purses. We don't.
