My dad's shoe repair shop on Pleasant Street in Fall River smells like Barge cement and leather dust. So does my hoodie; my friends say I smell like a "cowboy's basement." I work the counter there on Saturdays, and since last summer I've been allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. I can tell how someone walks from the wear on their heels. One woman's right heel was ground down way more than her left, and it turned out she drives for work, so that heel rests on the floor by the pedal. When I said it out loud, she looked at me like I was a psychic. I keep a notebook of heels with 61 entries; entry 1 is my dad's work boots.

I also keep a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even small ones like "I'm almost there." It has 212 items, most of them about being on time. It started in October of sophomore year, after I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept that up for three weeks. I forged the parent signature on the progress report, and before I did, I practiced my mom's signature about eight times on the back of a math worksheet. That's the part I'd rather leave out, because it means I planned it. Then my teacher, Mr. Oduya, emailed home.

My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test to her, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese, which I'm not good at when it comes to chemistry words. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed at that part. I finished the class with a B+. At the time, I didn't tell anyone I'd been relieved when Mr. Oduya's email came; keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting.

I hate the shop phone. I have a stutter that gets worse on the phone, especially on hard sounds like C, and the shop is called Carvalho Shoe Service. Carvalho is also my last name. So I answered every call with "Hi, shoe repair." My dad noticed after about a year and never said anything about it.

I want to study speech-language pathology, or something about how the brain gets words back. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my speech therapist from middle school, whether I'd be "allowed" to be a speech therapist with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter, and I don't fully believe her yet.

Last month I answered the shop phone with the name for the first time. I kept trying to picture the man's face, whether he was annoyed, and on the second try I almost hung up. He waited. When "Carvalho Shoe Service" finally came out, I didn't feel proud. I felt kind of embarrassed that it took three tries. Then he asked if we fix purses, like nothing had happened. (We don't.) That was the best part, that it was nothing to him.

Someone asked me recently what I understand now that I didn't then, and that's when I noticed "Hi, shoe repair" was a lie too. A small one, and it isn't on my list. I'd been doing the same thing with the phone that I did with the chemistry test: swapping the true thing for an easier thing. I'm not sure what to do with that yet. Maybe it goes on the list, though I still can't tell whether the list makes me more honest or just better at noticing.
