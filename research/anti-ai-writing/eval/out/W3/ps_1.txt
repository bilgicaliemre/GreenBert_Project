Before I forged my mom's signature, I practiced it about eight times on the back of a math worksheet. That's the part I'd rather leave out, because it means I planned it. Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test, told my mom it was an 85, and kept it up for three weeks, through a progress report that needed a parent signature, until my teacher, Mr. Oduya, emailed home. I didn't tell anyone I was relieved, but keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting.

My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese, and my Portuguese is weak on chemistry words. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I called it "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed.

I finished the class with a B+, and after that I made a rule: every lie I tell goes on a list on my phone, even small ones like "I'm almost there." Since October of sophomore year it has reached 212 items, mostly about being on time. I'm still not sure if the list makes me more honest or just better at noticing.

On Saturdays I work the counter at my dad's shop, Carvalho Shoe Service on Pleasant Street, open since 1994, and since last summer I'm allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. The shop smells like Barge cement and leather dust, and so does my hoodie; my friends say I smell like a cowboy's basement. I can tell how someone walks from the wear on their heels, and I keep a notebook of them: 61 entries, starting with my dad's work boots.

What I hate is the phone. I have a stutter that gets worse on it, especially on hard sounds like C, and the shop is called Carvalho, our last name. So I answered "Hi, shoe repair," and my dad, who noticed after about a year, never said anything about it.

Last month a man called, and I said "Carvalho Shoe Service" on the third try while he waited. I was picturing his face, wondering if he was annoyed, and on the second try I almost hung up. When the name came out I didn't feel proud, just kind of embarrassed it took three tries. Then he asked if we fix purses (we don't), as if nothing had happened, and that was the best part: it was nothing to him.

I only noticed recently that "Hi, shoe repair" was a lie too, a small one, and it isn't on my list. I've been doing the same thing with the phone that I did with the chemistry test, swapping the true thing for an easier thing. I'm not sure what to do with that, except maybe add it to the list.

Tuesdays and Thursdays after school, my avó, Rosa, and I go through Portuguese picture flashcards I made for her. She had a stroke two years ago and has aphasia; she understands more than she can say. She can't say my name yet, but she can say sapato, shoe. Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, my aunt who died before I was born, and I used to correct her, but now I don't. She's relearning the words while I learn them for the first time, so she beats me at agulha, needle, and I beat her at telemóvel.

I read a lot about aphasia now, and I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics. I kind of wanted stuttering and aphasia to be connected, but they're totally different things. I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my speech therapist from middle school, whether I'd be "allowed" to be a speech therapist with a stutter. She said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.
