Let Us Sleep: Why High School Should Start at 9:00

At 7:40 on a Tuesday morning, my first-period chemistry class looks like a bus station waiting room. Half the room has hoods up. One kid is asleep on his folded arms before attendance is even taken, and the teacher, who gave up fighting it months ago, starts the lecture anyway. Nobody is learning much. If schools want students to absorb what they're taught, the first bell should ring at 9:00 a.m. or later.

The simplest argument is that a tired brain makes a bad student. Anyone who has tried to balance a chemical equation at dawn knows the feeling of reading the same line four times. Teenagers also don't get to choose when they feel sleepy. Most of us can't fall asleep at 9:30 p.m. no matter how hard we try, so an early bell just means we sleep less. Those lost hours come straight out of our rest, and the damage follows us into every class after first period.

Then there's what an early start does to the rest of the day. Exhausted students make worse choices. They skip breakfast because there's no time. They chug energy drinks in the parking lot. They snap at friends and parents, and by the afternoon they're crashing right as practice or a part-time shift begins. Pushing the start time back an hour wouldn't fix every problem teenagers have. It would remove one that adults created and could easily undo.

Driving matters here too. Plenty of juniors and seniors drive themselves to school, often in the dark during winter, often running on very little sleep. I've watched classmates pull into the lot looking like they barely made it. A later start puts more alert drivers on the road, which parents and neighbors should want as much as students do.

Opponents usually raise practical concerns, and they deserve a fair hearing. A later start would push sports and clubs later into the evening. Bus routes shared with elementary and middle schools might need to be rearranged. Some parents leave for work early and count on their teenagers to get younger siblings out the door. These are real issues. Still, every one of them is a scheduling problem, and districts solve scheduling problems all the time. Practices could run a little later or move under field lights. Bus routes could be flipped so younger kids, who tend to wake up early anyway, ride first. Families already adjust every time a new semester shuffles their week.

Some people also claim early starts prepare students for the real world, where jobs begin at 8:00 or earlier. I don't buy it. Lots of adult jobs start at 9:00, or 10:00, or noon. More to the point, high school is supposed to be where we learn, and good habits are hard to build when you're worn out every single morning. Being miserable at sixteen won't make anyone better at their job at thirty.

What frustrates me most is how fixable this is. Schools spend time and money on tutoring programs, test prep sessions, and wellness assemblies, all meant to help students do better. Letting us sleep an extra hour would cost far less than most of those, and it might help more than all of them put together. The bell schedule isn't a law of nature. Someone picked it, and someone can change it.

So let the first bell ring at 9:00. Give students a fair chance to show up awake. My chemistry teacher would probably thank you.
