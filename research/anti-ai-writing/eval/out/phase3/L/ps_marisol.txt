Outflow

I can guess what people will order from their shoes. Chacos get green corn. Cowboy boots get red chile pork. Crocs get cheese and jalapeño. My brother Gabe says it's confirmation bias. The annoying part is that I've never counted. My rooftop weather station logs every minute, and I write rain down to the hundredth of an inch. The shoes I just believe.

Its first month, the station logged rain on 19 days in June. It hadn't rained once. A man named Glen at the weather service answered my email with one line: "Do you have an evaporative cooler?" I figured that taught me to check myself. Then came July 18th.

My family sells tamales at the night market behind the Mercado. We set up at 5:30, in 104 degrees. I'd seen a cell over the Rincons on the drive, so I sat on the cooler watching radar. The four sandbags, 25 pounds each, were my job. My mom told me to get them. I said "in a sec." I think I said it twice.

At 6:40 brown dust rose in the east, and Gabe said pack up. I told him the storm was splitting north. Then I said, "Trust me." My mom laughed. "La científica says we stay."

At 6:52 the gust front hit. Our canopy went up like a kite and cartwheeled two booths over into Mr. Duarte's honey table. Fourteen jars, $168. Six dozen tamales went into the gravel. A little girl with a mango paleta was right there, and her dad yanked her back by the arm. Nobody got hurt. Gabe didn't yell. He just started picking up glass.

The rain did go north. We got maybe four drops. My forecast was kind of right, and it didn't matter, because I'd spent all summer reading about outflow, the wind that runs out ahead of a storm, and that night it never entered my head.

What I was thinking when Gabe said pack up is worse. His math on our fridge whiteboard says my mom makes $4.10 an hour on a normal Sunday. With the disc problem in her lower back, he wants her to quit the weekly markets. I keep saying people come for her. Gabe says that's not a business plan. If we packed up and the storm missed, that was one more point for the whiteboard. I wanted to win. That was the whole thought.

At home I went straight to the roof to check my station: 47 miles an hour, which means nothing, since we live six miles from the Mercado. Downstairs my mom was checking husks at the kitchen table. I was on the roof with my graph.

In a fight once, Gabe said I want the stand to stay exactly the same so I can leave and still find it there, like my nana's chair. I told him that was stupid. But I'm probably the one leaving, for U of A or Norman, and I'm the one telling my mom to keep standing on gravel every Sunday with her back. I always believed I was arguing for her. Like the shoes, I never checked.

Sandbags go down first now, before I look at the sky. I don't announce forecasts at the stand. I still check radar every five minutes. Maybe I changed, or maybe I just stopped saying it out loud. Probably the second one.

I've paid my mom back $60 of the $168 she handed Mr. Duarte out of her apron. She hasn't asked about the rest, and part of me wants her to.

In September she asked me, not Gabe, whether to drop the Saturday night market next summer. I said I'd look at the numbers. I did. They're in my Google Sheet, in a tab next to the rain data. I haven't said anything back to her yet.
