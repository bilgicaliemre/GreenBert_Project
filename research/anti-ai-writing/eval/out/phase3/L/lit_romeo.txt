They Stumble That Run Fast

Romeo and Juliet tells us how it ends before it begins. In the Prologue, the Chorus says that "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross’d lovers take their life," and that their "misadventur’d piteous overthrows / Doth with their death bury their parents’ strife." Words like "fatal" and "star-cross’d" make the deaths sound as if they were written in the sky before anyone speaks a line. If that were the whole story, though, there would be little reason to watch. The Chorus also picks the word "misadventur’d," which points to accidents and bad timing, the kind of trouble people walk into on their own. The Prologue hands us the ending. The rest of the play shows two young people hurrying toward it.

The clearest evidence comes in Act II, Scene 3, when Romeo asks Friar Lawrence to marry him to Juliet that same day. "O let us hence; I stand on sudden haste," he says. The Friar answers with what sounds like a warning aimed at the audience as much as at Romeo: "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast." Shakespeare spells out the play's lesson in plain words in the second act, and Romeo doesn't listen. By Act III, Scene 1, he has killed Tybalt, and his first reaction is "O, I am fortune’s fool!" It's a memorable line and a convenient one. Calling himself fortune's fool lets Romeo play the victim of bad luck at the very moment he has done something terrible with his own hands. We have just watched him kill Tybalt, so the excuse is hard to accept. Luck didn't make that choice for him.

Act V, Scene 1 runs through the pattern one last time. When Balthasar tells Romeo that Juliet is dead, Romeo answers, "Is it even so? Then I defy you, stars!" He seems to believe he is standing up to fate. Then look at what he does with that defiance: "Get me ink and paper, / And hire post-horses. I will hence tonight." He asks a single question and has his plan before the speech is over. He even reaches for the word he used with the Friar back in Act II. "O let us hence" becomes "I will hence tonight." Romeo's way of fighting the stars is the same rushing the Friar warned him about, so his defiance ends up helping the Prologue come true. The play gives fate the first word and lets its characters blame the stars when things go wrong. What it actually puts on stage, scene after scene, is people moving too fast to think. Fate may know where this story ends. The lovers choose the speed, and the Friar already told us what happens to those who run.
