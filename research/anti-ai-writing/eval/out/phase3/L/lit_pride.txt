The Groundwork of a Dislike

Elizabeth Bennet makes up her mind about Mr. Darcy in about a minute. At the Meryton ball she overhears him say that she "is tolerable: but not handsome enough to tempt me," and that he is in no mood to bother with "young ladies who are slighted by other men." It is a rude thing to say, and Austen lets the reader dislike him for it. Elizabeth has every right to be offended. Where she goes wrong is in treating that one evening as the final word. When she turns down his proposal in Chapter 34, she admits as much. From "the first moment" of knowing him, his manners gave her "the fullest belief of your arrogance, your conceit, and your selfish disdain of the feelings of others," and this became "that groundwork of disapprobation, on which succeeding events have built so immovable a dislike." The building image gives her away. A groundwork gets laid once and then nobody looks at it again. Everything she learned about Darcy afterward was stacked on a foundation she never checked, and she calls the result "immovable" as if that proved it was solid.

Darcy's letter brings the whole thing down. Alone in Chapter 36, Elizabeth cries, "How despicably have I acted!" and then, more painfully, "I, who have prided myself on my discernment!" The skill she valued most in herself is the one that failed her. She also sees why. "Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other," she has "courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away." "Courted" is an active verb. She went out looking for her prejudice, because one man flattered her and another hurt her pride. So her verdict on Darcy was really a record of how each man made her feel. That is why the speech ends where it does: "Till this moment, I never knew myself." Misreading Darcy and misreading herself turn out to be the same mistake.

What, then, does the novel say about judging people quickly? Austen never pretends Darcy behaved well at the ball. Her warning is about what a fast judgment does afterward. Once Elizabeth decided who Darcy was, every new fact got sorted to fit, and her pride in her own sharpness kept her from noticing. The ending sets up a contrast. When Jane asks how long she has loved him, Elizabeth answers, "It has been coming on so gradually, that I hardly know when it began." Then she jokes that she must date it "from my first seeing his beautiful grounds at Pemberley." The joke lands because it mocks the kind of quick, shallow reason she once trusted. Her dislike had an exact starting point, a single remark at a dance. Her love has none. Austen seems to suggest that knowing a person takes time, and that the people most sure of their first impressions are often the ones who should doubt them most.
