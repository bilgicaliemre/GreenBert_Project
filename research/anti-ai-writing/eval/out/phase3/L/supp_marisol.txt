On monsoon Saturdays I'm on our flat roof by 6 a.m., before it's 100 degrees, wiping pigeon mess off my rain gauge. I built my weather station on a Raspberry Pi from my uncle Memo. Its first month, it logged rain on 19 days of a bone-dry June. The swamp cooler was dripping in.

Noon to four is tamalada in our kitchen, where my nana Chayo tells everyone from her chair that the masa is too thick. Mine get redone. Then I run the Square reader at our market stand. My brother Gabe did the math on our fridge whiteboard: on a normal Sunday, my mom makes $4.10 an hour. He's right about the money. I still argue with him.

Last July a cell sat over the Rincons. Gabe saw dust and said pack up. I had radar open and said it was splitting north. "Trust me," I actually said. The rain did go north. I was so locked on it that I forgot the outflow, the wind ahead of a storm, and the sandbags, my job, were still in the truck. Our canopy cartwheeled into Mr. Duarte's honey table. Now sandbags go down before I check the sky.

My dad checks the station's heat index before his mail route and calls it "Marisol's channel." Around 3 p.m., everyone in Tucson stares at the mountains, and the models are bad at saying where storms will start. I want to study atmospheric science and find out.
