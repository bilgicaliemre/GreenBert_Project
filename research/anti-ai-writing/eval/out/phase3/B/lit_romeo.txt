Fate and Haste in Romeo and Juliet

Before the first scene, the Chorus tells the audience how Romeo and Juliet will end: "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross’d lovers take their life; / Whose misadventur’d piteous overthrows / Doth with their death bury their parents’ strife." Among these passages this is the strongest evidence for fate, because the speaker already knows the ending. The lovers are "star-cross’d," thwarted by the stars before the action starts, and their fall is "misadventur’d," which means unlucky. Even "fatal" belongs to the "loins" of "these two foes," so the prologue finds the causes in the stars and the feud and names no fault in the lovers. The scenes quoted after it move the blame onto Romeo. He claims his haste as his own choice, and he brings up fortune and the stars right next to his own actions.

In Act II, Scene 3, Romeo asks Friar Lawrence to marry him to Juliet that same day and says, "O let us hence; I stand on sudden haste." To "stand on" something is to insist on it, so Romeo names the hurry as his own position. The Friar replies, "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast." He's giving advice about a wedding, not foretelling deaths, but his proverb explains a fall differently from the Chorus. In the prologue the lovers' "overthrows" are "misadventur’d," a matter of luck. In the Friar's line people "stumble" because they "run fast," so the fall comes from their own speed. In Act III, Scene 1, just after killing Tybalt, Romeo cries, "O, I am fortune’s fool!" Romeo's own hand did the killing, yet the line makes him fortune's plaything. As evidence for fate it's weaker than the prologue, since the person blaming fortune is the person who has just killed a man.

In Act V, Scene 1, on hearing from Balthasar that Juliet is dead, Romeo says, "Is it even so? Then I defy you, stars! / Thou know’st my lodging. Get me ink and paper, / And hire post-horses. I will hence tonight." He asks one question, and his next sentence already treats the answer as settled. In these lines his defiance of the stars turns straight into orders and a plan to leave that night. "I will hence tonight" also repeats the "hence" of "O let us hence," so the word that marked his hurry in Act II marks it again here. The excerpts stop before the deaths, so they can't show how this night's plan leads to them. On the evidence they do give, the play blames haste more than fate. The Chorus makes the ending certain, but in the later scenes fortune and the stars come up only in Romeo's outbursts, while the haste is a choice he makes twice, once before the Friar's warning and once after it.
