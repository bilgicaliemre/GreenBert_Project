Elizabeth's Immovable Dislike

Elizabeth Bennet's first impression of Mr. Darcy has a real basis. At the Meryton ball in Chapter 3 she overhears him call her "tolerable: but not handsome enough to tempt me." The impression misleads her anyway, and by her own later account she helps it do so: she lets later events build on it, and her pride in her own judgment gives her no reason to check it. Refusing his first proposal in Chapter 34, she tells Darcy his manners gave her "the fullest belief" in his arrogance and conceit "from the first moment, I may almost say," and that this became the "groundwork of disapprobation, on which succeeding events have built so immovable a dislike." In her image, later events don't test the groundwork. They are built on it. She even offers the dislike's firmness as a reason to turn him down, as if being immovable showed she had judged well.

When Darcy's letter tests that groundwork in Chapter 36, Elizabeth, alone after reading it, says, "How despicably have I acted!" and then, "I, who have prided myself on my discernment!" With no argument to win, this speech counts for more than the refusal. She traces the mistake to its start: "Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other, on the very beginning of our acquaintance." The "neglect" fits Darcy's remark at the ball. Her verbs make her the one acting, since she has "courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away." In her own account, then, she invited the first impression and pushed away the reasoning that could have corrected it. Her last line, "Till this moment, I never knew myself," shows she had been wrong about herself as well as about the people she judged.

By Chapter 59 the immovable dislike has moved. When Jane asks how long she has loved Darcy, Elizabeth says it "has been coming on so gradually, that I hardly know when it began." She could date the dislike to "the first moment, I may almost say," but she can hardly date the love at all. She is half joking, so the line is weak evidence alone, but the joke itself seems to mock her old habit. When she does name a start, "my first seeing his beautiful grounds at Pemberley," she again dates a feeling from a first sight, this time pinned on his land instead of on him. These passages don't call a quick judgment false for being quick, since Darcy really did call Elizabeth "tolerable." The trouble, in her own words, is pride. Her Chapter 36 speech comes close to naming both halves of the title: she "prided" herself, and she "courted prepossession," a judgment formed in advance, which is nearly what prejudice means. In Elizabeth's case the two work as one mistake: the prejudice formed almost at once, and her pride kept her from questioning it until she read Darcy's letter.
