Around 3 o'clock on monsoon afternoons, everybody in Tucson stares at the mountains. I want to know where the storms start, and the models are bad at it.

On July 18, at my mom's tamale stand, I sat on the cooler watching a cell over the Rincons on radar. The sandbags were my job, and when my mom said get them, I said "in a sec." When dust came, my brother Gabe said pack up. I said it was going north. I actually said "trust me." The rain did go north. At 6:52 the gust front hit, and our canopy cartwheeled into Mr. Duarte's honey table: fourteen jars, $168.

I'd read about outflow winds all summer, but I was so locked on the rain that the wind wasn't in my head. Gabe's fridge whiteboard says my mom makes $4.10 an hour on a normal Sunday, and he wants her off the weekly markets. If we left and it missed, that was another point for him, and I just wanted to win.

Now the sandbags go down before I look at the sky. I still check radar every five minutes, but I don't announce forecasts at the stand. Maybe I changed, or maybe I just stopped saying it out loud. Probably that one.

My mom asked me, not Gabe, whether to drop the Saturday night market. The numbers are done, in a tab beside my rain data. I haven't answered her. I still owe her $108 for the honey.
