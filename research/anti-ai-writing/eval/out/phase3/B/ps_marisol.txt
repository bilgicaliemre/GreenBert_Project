At our tamale stand, I can guess orders from shoes: Chacos mean green corn, cowboy boots mean red chile pork. Then a lady in Chacos wanted pork and told me, "You don't know me." She was right. I record rain to the hundredth of an inch on my rooftop weather station but have never once written down a pair of shoes.

My brother Gabe did count the stand. His fridge-whiteboard math includes the $45 booth fee and all our hours: my mom makes $4.10 an hour on a normal Sunday. She has a disc problem in her lower back. Gabe wants her to quit the weekly markets. I keep saying people come for her: Mr. Hollis has bought exactly seven every Sunday for five years. Gabe says Mr. Hollis is not a business plan.

On July 18th, driving to the night market, I saw a storm cell over the Rincon Mountains, so after setup I sat on the cooler watching radar. The canopy sandbags were my job. When my mom told me to get them, I said "in a sec," twice I think, and they stayed in the truck.

Around 6:40, brown dust came from the east and Gabe said let's pack up. I said the cell was splitting north and actually said "trust me." My mom laughed: "La científica says we stay." At 6:52 the gust front hit. Our canopy went up like a kite and cartwheeled two booths over into Mr. Duarte's honey table: fourteen jars, $168. Nobody got hurt, but a little girl with a mango paleta was right there until her dad yanked her away. Gabe didn't say anything. He just started picking up the glass, and I think him yelling would have been easier.

The rain did go north, so my forecast was kind of right. What hit us was outflow, the wind that runs ahead of a storm, which I'd read about all summer. I was so locked on the rain that the wind wasn't in my head at all.

When Gabe said pack up, I thought that if we left and the storm missed, that was one more point for his whiteboard. If we stayed and it missed, I'd be right, in front of him. It wasn't really about customers or tamales. I just wanted to win. At home I went straight to the roof to check my station: 47 miles an hour, meaningless six miles from the market. My mom was at the kitchen table going through the husks to see if any were still okay. And I was up on the roof with my graph.

Gabe is right about the money. I still argue with him. Once, in the truck, he said I want the stand to stay exactly the same so I can go away and it's still there when I come back. I said that was stupid. But I'm the one who's leaving, probably. I'm also the one saying my mom should keep standing on the gravel every Sunday with her back, and I haven't figured out where that thought ends.

Now the sandbags go down before I look at the sky. I still check radar about every five minutes, but I don't announce forecasts at the stand anymore. Maybe I changed, or maybe I only stopped saying it out loud, and it's probably the second one.

My mom paid Mr. Duarte the $168 that night, cash out of her apron, and I said I'd pay her back. I'm at $60. Gabe thinks I already did. She hasn't asked once, and part of me wants her to, which is weird.

In September she asked me, not Gabe, whether to drop the Saturday night market next summer. I said I'd look at the numbers. I did. They're in my Google Sheet, in a tab next to the rain data, and I haven't said anything back to her yet.
