Start high school at nine

The simplest answer to a teenager who can't stay awake in first period is to tell him to go to bed earlier. Behind it is a reasonable idea: adults have to get up for work, so students should learn to get up for school. But the advice only works if sleep starts when the lights go off. A school can't make a sixteen-year-old fall asleep at ten, though it does decide when he has to wake up. High schools should start at 9:00 a.m. or later, and the district, not individual families, should pay for the problems that a later afternoon creates.

Suppose a high school starts at 7:30. A student who catches the bus at 6:45 sets his alarm for 6:00. He takes the advice and goes to bed at ten, but he lies awake until eleven, so he gets seven hours of sleep. Going to bed early bought him an hour of lying in the dark. Now move the first bell to 9:00 and change nothing about him. His alarm goes off at 7:30, and he sleeps eight and a half hours. He didn't need more willpower to get those ninety minutes, and he didn't skip the lesson about work either, since he still has to get up when the alarm rings and be in his seat on time.

The strongest objection is that those ninety minutes have to come from somewhere. If the school day stays the same length, a 9:00 start means a 4:00 dismissal instead of 2:30. Practice ends later, after-school shifts start later, and a junior who is supposed to pick up her little brother from elementary school at 3:00 is still in class. If the district runs its buses in shifts, moving the high school later may also mean moving another school earlier. These costs are real, and they would land hardest on families who can't rearrange their afternoons.

So a later start can't just be a new time printed on the bell schedule. The childcare problem is the part of the objection that changes my plan: if high school lets out at 4:00, and especially if the elementary schools move earlier to free up buses, the district should run after-school care at those schools until the older siblings get out of class, and count that care as part of the cost of moving the bell. Practices are a smaller problem than they look. In the example above, a practice from 4:15 to 6:30 still leaves time for dinner and three hours of homework before eleven, so the athlete keeps his extra sleep. A student whose homework already runs past midnight would only break even, because his night moves later along with his alarm, and a student with a job may lose part of a shift. I'd still make that trade, because those students can ask for a later shift or plan homework around practice, while the start time is set by the school and no student can change it.

An early start has costs too, but students pay them in sleep, so they never appear in a district budget. A 9:00 start moves the cost into the afternoon, onto bus routes and after-school programs that the district has to pay for and defend at school board meetings.
