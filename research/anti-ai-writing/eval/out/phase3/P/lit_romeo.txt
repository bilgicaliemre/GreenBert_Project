Fortune's Fool or His Own? Fate and Haste in Romeo and Juliet

Romeo and Juliet tells the audience how it will end before either lover appears. The Chorus announces that "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross’d lovers take their life; / Whose misadventur’d piteous overthrows / Doth with their death bury their parents’ strife." Nearly every key word points away from personal choice. The lovers are "star-cross’d," so the heavens oppose them from the start. The loins that produce them are "fatal," as if death were bred into the family line. Their fall is "misadventur’d," a word that suggests bad luck more than bad judgment. Even "take their life" works two ways, meaning both that they are born and that they will die by their own hands. On this evidence the play seems to blame fate. A prologue, however, only tells us where the story ends. The scenes that follow show how it gets there, and they keep returning to a human failing: speed.

Friar Lawrence states that failing most clearly. When Romeo asks to be married to Juliet that same day, he says, "O let us hence; I stand on sudden haste." The Friar answers, "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast." The line works almost like a second prologue, a moral forecast set beside the Chorus's cosmic one. The Chorus says the stars will cross the lovers; the Friar says running will trip them. Romeo does not listen. Just after killing Tybalt, he cries, "O, I am fortune’s fool!" The timing matters. He has acted on impulse, with his own hand, yet his first move is to call himself fortune's plaything. Shakespeare lets the audience hear the gap between what Romeo did and what he blames, and the line sounds less like insight than like an excuse.

Romeo's last decision joins the two ideas. When Balthasar tells him that Juliet is dead, he replies, "Is it even so? Then I defy you, stars! / Thou know’st my lodging. Get me ink and paper, / And hire post-horses. I will hence tonight." Romeo believes he is rebelling against fate, but his rebellion takes the form of pure haste: one brief question, then orders for paper and horses, then a departure that very night. "I will hence tonight" echoes his earlier "O let us hence," and the echo exposes the play's real argument. In defying the stars, Romeo runs exactly as the Friar warned, and his running delivers the ending the Chorus promised. Fate in Romeo and Juliet names the destination, but haste chooses the road and sets the pace. The stars may have crossed the lovers, yet the play shows that Romeo stumbles because he will not slow down.
