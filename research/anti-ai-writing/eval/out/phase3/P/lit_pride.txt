First Impressions and the Groundwork of Prejudice

Elizabeth Bennet's opinion of Mr. Darcy is formed in a single overheard sentence. At the Meryton ball, Darcy dismisses her as "tolerable: but not handsome enough to tempt me," and adds that he is in no humour to bother with "young ladies who are slighted by other men." The insult is real, and Austen gives the reader no reason to excuse it. That is what makes Elizabeth's first impression so convincing: it rests on something Darcy actually said. Her mistake is not noticing his rudeness but letting one evening stand for his whole character. When she refuses his first proposal in Chapter 34, she admits as much without seeing it as a fault. His manners, she tells him, formed "that groundwork of disapprobation, on which succeeding events have built so immovable a dislike." The language is architectural. A first impression becomes a foundation, and everything she learns afterward is stacked on top of it instead of tested against it. She seems proud that her dislike is "immovable," as if firmness were proof of accuracy.

Darcy's letter knocks that structure down, and Elizabeth's response in Chapter 36 shows what Austen is really after. "How despicably have I acted!" she cries. "I, who have prided myself on my discernment!" The irony is sharp: the quality she valued most in herself is exactly the one that failed. She then names the cause with unusual honesty: "Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other, on the very beginning of our acquaintance, I have courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away." Her judgment was steered by vanity. She warmed to the man who flattered her, turned against the man who slighted her, and mistook those feelings for insight. The verbs matter too. She "courted" prejudice and "driven reason away," which makes the error something she did, not something that happened to her. Her final line, "Till this moment, I never knew myself," turns the misjudgment inward. Misreading Darcy was also a way of misreading herself.

The novel's view of quick judgment comes through most clearly in a contrast. Elizabeth can date her dislike precisely, "from the first moment," but when Jane asks how long she has loved Darcy, she answers that "it has been coming on so gradually, that I hardly know when it began." Her half-joking claim that it started with "his beautiful grounds at Pemberley" pokes fun at the idea that feelings have a clean starting point at all. Dislike arrived all at once; understanding took time and new evidence. Austen does not suggest that first impressions are always false, since Darcy really was rude at the ball. She suggests that they are incomplete, and that they become dangerous when pride makes them feel final. A novel whose title pairs pride with prejudice ends up showing how closely the two are tied: the more confident we are in a quick judgment, the less willing we are to revise it.
