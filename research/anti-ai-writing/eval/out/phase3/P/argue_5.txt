Let Teenagers Wake Up: The Case for a 9:00 a.m. Start

Every weekday morning, a familiar scene plays out in high school classrooms. The first bell rings, the teacher starts explaining the causes of the Civil War or the properties of logarithms, and half the room stares blankly at the board. Someone in the back is fighting to keep his eyes open. These students are not lazy, and they are not uninterested. They are tired. High schools should start the school day at 9:00 a.m. or later, because a later start would let students arrive ready to learn, would make mornings safer and less frantic, and would treat teenagers' needs as seriously as the convenience of the schedule.

The strongest reason for a later start is simple: school exists so that students learn, and exhausted students do not learn well. Anyone who has sat through a first-period class knows the difference between 7:30 a.m. and 10:30 a.m. Early in the morning, discussions fall flat because no one has the energy to raise a hand. A first-period quiz can feel like a test of who managed to sleep rather than who understood the material. Many teenagers find it hard to fall asleep early even when they try; going to bed at 9:30 p.m. often just means staring at the ceiling. When the alarm then goes off at 6:00 a.m., the result is a student who is physically present but mentally somewhere else. Pushing the start time back would not turn every student into an eager scholar, but it would give teachers a room full of people who are actually awake.

A later start would also make mornings safer and calmer. Many students drive themselves to school, and a sixteen-year-old behind the wheel in the dark, half asleep, is a danger to himself and to everyone else on the road. Bus riders often wait on dark street corners before sunrise. Even at home, early mornings are a rush: skipped breakfasts, forgotten homework, arguments with parents about getting out the door. An extra hour would not solve every problem, but it would turn a daily scramble into something closer to an ordinary morning.

Critics raise fair concerns. If school starts later, it ends later, which cuts into sports practices, part-time jobs, and time for homework. Families who rely on older siblings for after-school childcare might struggle, and bus routes are hard to rearrange. These are real costs, and schools should not ignore them. But they are problems of logistics, and logistics can be adjusted. Practices can shift, employers can offer slightly later shifts, and districts can redesign bus routes, as they already do whenever a school opens or closes. What cannot be adjusted by a memo is a teenager's need for sleep. It makes more sense to bend the schedule around students than to keep bending students around the schedule.

Some people also argue that early starts teach responsibility and prepare students for the working world. Yet plenty of adult jobs do not begin at 7:30 a.m., and responsibility is shown by doing good work, not by enduring a schedule that makes good work harder. Being responsible means arriving prepared, and a rested student is far more prepared than an exhausted one.

High school is where young people build the habits and knowledge they will carry into adulthood. Right now, many of them spend the first hours of each day just trying to stay conscious. Moving the start time to 9:00 a.m. or later is a practical change that puts learning first, and schools should make it.
