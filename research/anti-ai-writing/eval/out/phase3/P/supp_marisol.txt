At 3 p.m. in a Tucson monsoon, everyone watches the mountains to see where the storms will start. The models are bad at that. I want to be good at it.

My weather station sits on our roof beside the swamp cooler: a Raspberry Pi from my uncle Memo, a $31 rain gauge, an anemometer made from ping-pong balls. Its first month, it logged rain on 19 days of a bone-dry June. The cooler was dripping in. My dad checks the heat index before his mail route and calls it "Marisol's channel."

The rest of my world is a tamale stand. Saturdays are tamalada in our kitchen, my nana Chayo in her chair telling us the masa is too thick. Sundays I run the Square reader at Rillito. At the July 18 night market, I sat on the cooler watching radar instead of setting our four sandbags. My brother Gabe said pack up. I said, "Trust me." The rain did go north. The gust front didn't. Our canopy cartwheeled into Mr. Duarte's honey table: fourteen jars, $168. I'd cared more about winning an argument than protecting my mom's stand.

Now I set sandbags before looking at the sky. That night showed me what I want from atmospheric science: to see the whole storm, not just the part I'm rooting for. Gabe's whiteboard says my mom earns $4.10 an hour. She asked me whether to drop the Saturday market. I've run the numbers. I owe her an answer, and $108.
