Let Practice Be Practice

A basketball player who misses free throws in an empty gym at nine o'clock at night does not lose a game. The misses are the point. They show her where her elbow drifts and how her release changes when she is tired, so that by the time she steps to the line in front of a crowd, she has already failed enough times to know what to fix. Schools say they value this kind of learning, yet in most classrooms homework is graded as if every attempt were a performance. Homework should be practice only. Teachers should assign it, check it, and discuss it, but it should not count toward a student's grade.

The first problem with grading homework is that it punishes the mistakes learning depends on. When a wrong answer on a worksheet costs points, students learn to avoid wrong answers instead of learning the material. The fastest way to avoid them is to copy from a friend, search for the answer online, or let an app do the work. Picture two students in the same algebra class. One struggles honestly through ten problems and gets six right. The other copies all ten from a classmate during lunch. The grade book rewards the second student. Worse, the teacher looks at a stack of perfect papers, assumes the class understands, moves on, and only discovers the truth when the test scores come back.

Graded homework also measures a student's home life as much as their understanding. One student goes home to a quiet room and a parent who remembers chemistry. Another works a shift after school, watches younger siblings until late, or shares a small apartment with no place to think. A test taken in class gives everyone the same time, the same room, and the same rules. Homework does not. If a grade is supposed to show what a student knows and can do, it should not also be quietly measuring how much free time and support that student has.

The strongest argument on the other side is that students will not do homework if it does not count. That concern is fair. Teenagers are busy, and they are practical about where their effort goes. But points are not the only reason to do the work. If homework closely matches what shows up on quizzes and tests, students who skip it will feel the consequences on the assessments that do count. Teachers can still check for completion, give written feedback, and go over common mistakes in class. Besides, the students who do homework only for points are the ones most likely to copy it, which means the points were never producing learning in the first place.

Some people also argue that homework grades help students who understand the material but freeze on tests. That is a real problem, but padding a grade with worksheet points hides it rather than solving it. A better answer is to offer more ways to show understanding, such as retakes, projects, in-class writing, or explaining a solution out loud to the teacher.

The basketball player in the empty gym improves because nobody is keeping score. Once practice starts counting against you, it stops being practice and turns into another performance, with all the pressure and shortcuts that come with it. Schools should let homework be the place where students are allowed to be wrong, and save grades for measuring what they have learned once they have had a fair chance to learn it.
