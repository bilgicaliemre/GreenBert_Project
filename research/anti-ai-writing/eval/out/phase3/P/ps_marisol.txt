Outflow

I record rain to the hundredth of an inch. On August 9th, my rooftop station logged 1.62 inches in 38 minutes, with a 23-minute hole where my nana unplugged everything, because she thinks the lightning sensor attracts lightning.

I have never measured the shoes. At our family's tamale stand, I guess orders from footwear: Chacos mean green corn, cowboy boots mean red chile pork. Then a woman in Chacos ordered pork and told me, "You don't know me." She was right. My brother Gabe calls it confirmation bias. I never counted. I just believed it.

The shoes weren't the only thing I'd never counted.

Gabe did the stand's math on our fridge whiteboard: booth fees, ingredients, gas, everyone's hours. On a normal Sunday, my mom makes $4.10 an hour, with a disc problem in her lower back. Gabe wants her to quit the weekly markets. I keep saying people come for her: Mr. Hollis has bought exactly seven tamales every Sunday for five years. Gabe says Mr. Hollis is not a business plan.

On July 18th, driving to the Saturday night market, I spotted a storm cell over the Rincons. After setup I sat on the cooler watching radar. The sandbags, four at 25 pounds each, were my job. My mom told me to get them. I said "in a sec." Twice, I think.

Around 6:40, brown dust rolled in from the east. Gabe said pack up. I said the cell was splitting north. I actually said, "Trust me." My mom laughed: "La científica says we stay."

At 6:52 the gust front hit. Our canopy went up like a kite and cartwheeled two booths over into Mr. Duarte's honey table: fourteen jars, $168. Six dozen tamales hit the gravel. A little girl with a mango paleta was right there until her dad yanked her by the arm. Gabe didn't say anything. He just started picking up glass.

The rain did go north; we got maybe four drops. What hit us was outflow, the wind that runs ahead of a storm, which I'd read about all summer. I was so locked on the rain that I forgot the wind.

But meteorology isn't what I got most wrong. I wasn't thinking about customers. I was thinking that if we left and the storm missed, that was one more point for Gabe's whiteboard. I just wanted to win.

At home, I went straight to the roof. My station showed 47 miles an hour, meaningless six miles from the Mercado. My mom sat at the kitchen table, going through the husks to see if any were still usable. I was up on the roof with my graph.

Once, fighting in the truck, Gabe told me I want the stand to stay exactly the same so I can leave and find it there when I come back, like my nana's chair. I said that was stupid. But I'm the one probably leaving, and the one telling my mom to keep standing on gravel every Sunday with her back.

Now the sandbags go down before I look at the sky. I still check radar every five minutes; I just don't announce forecasts anymore. I can't tell whether I've changed or only stopped saying it out loud.

My mom paid Mr. Duarte that night, cash from her apron. I said I'd pay her back. I'm at $60. She hasn't asked once.

In September she asked me, just me, whether to drop the Saturday night market next summer. I said I'd look at the numbers. They're done, in a tab next to the rain data. I haven't told her what they say.

So I owe her $108 and an answer, and the answer is harder. I can measure rain to the hundredth of an inch. I'm still learning to say a number out loud when it might cost me something I wanted to keep.
