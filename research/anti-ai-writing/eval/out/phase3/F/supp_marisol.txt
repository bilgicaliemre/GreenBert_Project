On monsoon Saturdays I'm on our roof by 6 a.m., checking the weather station I built. By noon it's tamalada in our kitchen, where Nana Chayo sits in her chair telling everybody the masa is too thick. We sell the tamales at our market stand.

Around 3 p.m., everybody in Tucson stares at the mountains. I want to know where the storms will start, which the models are bad at.

On July 18, at the night market, dust was coming from the east and my brother Gabe said pack up. "It's splitting, it's going north," I said. "Trust me." The rain did go north. But at 6:52 the gust front hit, the sandbags (my job) were still in the truck, and our canopy cartwheeled into Mr. Duarte's honey table: fourteen jars, $168. I was so locked on where the rain was going, the wind wasn't in my head. Mostly, I wanted to beat Gabe, whose whiteboard says my mom makes $4.10 an hour at the stand.

Now the sandbags go down before I look at the sky. In September my mom asked me, not Gabe, whether to drop the Saturday market. The numbers are in a tab next to my rain data. I haven't answered her. Gabe says I want the stand to stay the same so it's still there when I come back, like Nana's chair. I'm probably the one leaving, and also the one telling her to keep standing on gravel every Sunday, with her back.
