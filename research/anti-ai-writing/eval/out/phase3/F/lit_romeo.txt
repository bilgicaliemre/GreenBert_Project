They stumble that run fast

Before the first scene begins, Shakespeare's Chorus tells the audience whom to blame. Romeo and Juliet are "star-cross’d lovers" born from "the fatal loins of these two foes," and "fatal" works in both of its senses: deadly, and decided by fate. Taken alone, the Prologue reads like a verdict against the sky. The scenes that follow blame haste instead. Inside the play, the character who blames the stars and fortune is Romeo, and he does it just after he has acted or just before he acts too fast. Even the Chorus hedges. Its phrase for the lovers' end is "misadventur’d piteous overthrows," and a misadventure is a mishap or a piece of bad luck, which is a smaller cause than a destiny written in the stars.

In Act II, Scene 3, Romeo asks Friar Lawrence to marry him to Juliet that same day: "O let us hence; I stand on sudden haste." "Stand on" means insist on, and "sudden haste" says the same thing twice, as if ordinary haste were too slow for him. The Friar answers with the play's plainest statement about cause: "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast." Nobody in that sentence is tripped by the stars; the runner falls because of his own speed. In Act III, Scene 1, moments after killing Tybalt, Romeo cries, "O, I am fortune’s fool!" A fool in this sense is a dupe or a plaything. Romeo has just killed a man himself, yet the line makes fortune the one who acted and turns him into the one acted upon.

In Act V, Scene 1, Balthasar tells Romeo that Juliet is dead. Romeo asks one question, "Is it even so?", and answers it in the same line: "Then I defy you, stars!" The defiance turns straight into errands: "Get me ink and paper, / And hire post-horses. I will hence tonight." "Hence" is the word he used to hurry the Friar in Act II, and it means the same thing in both scenes: go at once. Romeo thinks he is fighting the stars, but the only weapon he has is the speed the Friar warned him about, and it carries him toward the death the Prologue announced. The haste reading does have a limit. Because the Chorus gives away the ending before the first scene, the play never shows a slower Romeo who lives, so it cannot prove that patience would have saved him. What it can do is give the Friar his warning in Act II and leave the audience to remember it when Romeo sends for post-horses.
