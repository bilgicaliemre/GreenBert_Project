The groundwork of a dislike

Elizabeth Bennet's first impression of Mr. Darcy is accurate about one evening and wrong about the man. At the Meryton ball in Chapter 3 she overhears him call her "tolerable: but not handsome enough to tempt me," which is as rude as she takes it to be. The rest of his sentence is a quick judgment of its own. He is "in no humour at present to give consequence to young ladies who are slighted by other men," so he rates Elizabeth by the fact that other men have passed her over, and the words "at present" admit that his mood is doing the rating. Elizabeth's dislike begins as a reply to that snap judgment.

When she refuses his first proposal in Chapter 34, Elizabeth describes that dislike as a building. His manners, she says, gave her "the fullest belief of your arrogance" from "the first moment, I may almost say," and laid "that groundwork of disapprobation, on which succeeding events have built so immovable a dislike." She means it as a charge against Darcy, but the image describes her own method. A groundwork is laid first, and whatever comes later has to fit on top of it, so the "succeeding events" were never weighed on their own. Even her hedge, "I may almost say," half admits that she had decided before she knew him. Two chapters later, alone after reading Darcy's letter, she names the cause herself: "Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other, on the very beginning of our acquaintance, I have courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away where either were concerned." Both judgments began as feelings, pleasure at being preferred and offense at being neglected, and "courted" makes her the active party: she went looking for the prepossession. The cry "I, who have prided myself on my discernment!" explains why the groundwork held so long, because she trusted her own judgment too much to test it.

In Chapter 59, when Jane asks how long she has loved Darcy, Elizabeth answers that her love "has been coming on so gradually, that I hardly know when it began." Her dislike had an exact starting point, "the very beginning, from the first moment"; her love has none. She then half jokes that she "must date it from my first seeing his beautiful grounds at Pemberley." "First seeing" echoes "the first moment," but now she offers a first impression only half seriously, and this one is about his grounds, while the old one claimed to read his character. Austen stops short of calling first impressions false, since Darcy really was rude at the ball. What the novel faults is treating one evening as a foundation that later evidence cannot move, and it makes the correction expensive: after the letter Elizabeth has to revise her opinion of herself as well as of Darcy, and she admits, "Till this moment, I never knew myself."
