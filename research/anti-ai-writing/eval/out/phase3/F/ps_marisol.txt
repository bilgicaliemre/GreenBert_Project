Outflow

At 6:40 on July 18th, dust was coming at the night market from the east, brown, and my brother Gabe, working our tamale stand with us for once, said we should pack up. I had radar open, and I told him the storm cell over the Rincon Mountains was splitting and going north. Then I said, "Trust me." My mom laughed: "La científica says we stay."

The canopy weights were my job, four 25-pound sandbags, and they were still in the truck. Since setup I'd been on the cooler watching the cell, and when my mom told me to get them I said "in a sec," twice, I think. At 6:52 the gust front hit. Our canopy went up like a kite and cartwheeled two booths over into Mr. Duarte's honey table: fourteen jars, $168. Our steamer went over, six dozen tamales into the gravel. Nobody got hurt, but a little girl with a mango paleta was right there, and her dad yanked her back by the arm. Gabe didn't say anything. He just started picking up the glass, and I think him yelling would have been easier.

The rain did go north; we got about four drops. What hit us was the outflow, the wind that runs out ahead of a storm, literally what I'd read about all summer. I was so locked on where the rain was going that the wind wasn't in my head at all. At home I went straight up to the roof to check my weather station: 47 miles an hour, which means nothing, since our house is six miles from the Mercado. My mom was at the kitchen table going through the husks to see if any were still okay, and I was on the roof with my graph.

When Gabe said pack up, I wasn't thinking about customers or tamales. On our fridge whiteboard he worked out that after the $45 booth fee, ingredients, gas and all our hours, my mom makes $4.10 an hour on a normal Sunday. She has a disc problem in her lower back, and he wants her to quit the weekly markets. If we packed up and it missed, that was one more point for his whiteboard. If we stayed and it missed, I'd be right, in front of him. I just wanted to win.

My side of that argument is that people come for her. Mr. Hollis has bought exactly seven tamales every Sunday for five years. Gabe says Mr. Hollis is not a business plan, and about the money, he's right. I record rain to the hundredth of an inch, but in this argument Gabe has the numbers and I have Mr. Hollis. In the truck once, mid-fight, Gabe said I want the stand to stay exactly the same so I can leave and find it there when I come back, like my nana's chair. I said that was stupid. But I'm probably the one who's leaving, and I'm also the one telling my mom to keep standing on that gravel every Sunday with her back.

Now the sandbags go down before I look at the sky, and I don't announce forecasts at the stand. I still check radar about every five minutes; I just don't say anything. Maybe I changed, or maybe I only stopped saying it out loud. Probably the second one. That night my mom paid Mr. Duarte $168 in cash from her apron, and I said I'd pay her back. I'm at $60. She hasn't asked once, and part of me wants her to.

In September she asked me, not Gabe, whether she should drop the Saturday night market next summer. I said I'd look at the numbers, and I did. They're finished, in a tab of my Google Sheet next to the rain data, and I haven't said anything back to her yet.
