Move the first bell to nine

Anyone who has sat through a first-period class that starts before eight knows how it looks: hoods up and heads on desks while the teacher waits out the silence after a question. High schools should start the day at 9:00 a.m. or later. The change would cost something, mostly in sports schedules and bus routes, but those costs are smaller than what early starts take from students every morning.

Suppose school starts at 7:30 and the bus comes at 6:50. Someone who needs to shower and eat something has to be up around 6:00, so eight hours of sleep means being asleep by 10:00 p.m. Now fill in the afternoon: practice or a work shift until 6:00, dinner, then two or three hours of homework. That plan only works if nothing runs late, and on a school night something usually does. Starting at 9:00 shifts the whole chain by ninety minutes, which moves bedtime to 11:30, when most teenagers are still awake anyway.

The usual adult answer is that teenagers should put their phones away and go to bed earlier. Phones are part of the problem. But that advice assumes a student who lies down at 9:30 will fall asleep, and for a lot of teenagers that isn't what happens. They lie awake, and the hour between lights-out and sleep is when the phone comes back out. Moving the start time doesn't rely on anyone's willpower at 9:30 at night; it lines the school day up with the hours students already sleep.

Early starts also fall hardest on the students with the least room to adjust. Whoever lives farthest from school rides the longest bus route, so in winter those students are standing at the stop before sunrise. Someone who gets a ride from a parent can sleep until the last minute. Then there are the juniors and seniors who drive themselves to school half awake, which puts them and everyone else on the road at risk.

The serious objections are practical. A later dismissal pushes practice past sunset in late fall. Students with after-school jobs would lose an hour of work. Many districts run the same buses for elementary, middle and high school in shifts, so moving the high school bell means redrawing every route. A district that makes this change has to plan for all of that openly. Fields can be lit, as plenty already are for Friday night games. The bus order can flip so younger children ride first; they tend to wake up early on their own, as anyone with a little brother or sister knows from Saturday mornings. Lost work hours are the hardest cost, and I don't see a clean fix for them. Still, a part-time job schedule is a weak reason to keep every student in class before they are awake enough to learn.

A 9:00 start won't fix everything. If students spend the extra morning on later nights, or teachers answer it with more homework, the gain disappears. But the current schedule asks teenagers to think clearly at an hour when many of them can barely stay upright, then treats the result as a discipline problem. The school board can't control when a teenager puts down a phone or how much homework a chemistry teacher assigns. It does control when the first bell rings, and that one decision reaches every student in the building.
