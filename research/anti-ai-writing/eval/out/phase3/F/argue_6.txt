Homework should be practice only

A grade on a report card is supposed to tell a parent, a college or the student what that student can do in a subject. Homework that counts toward the grade makes that report less accurate, because the teacher has no way to know who did the work or how. Teachers should keep assigning and checking homework, but it should not count toward a student's grade. The grade should come from work done in class, where the teacher can see who is doing it.

A finished worksheet might show that the student understood the lesson. It might also show that an older sister walked her through it, that a friend texted a photo of his answers before first period, or that the answers came from a website or a chatbot. The teacher grading the stack sees the same neat page in every case. Once that page is worth points, the grade measures access to help and willingness to copy along with understanding, and a student who did every problem alone and got several wrong can end up with a lower homework average than one who copied a correct set in the hallway.

Grading homework punishes the mistakes that homework is supposed to bring out. Practice is where a student should discover that she cannot factor a quadratic or does not know when a comma goes before "and," because finding out on Tuesday's homework leaves time to fix it before Friday's test. If each wrong answer costs points, the sensible move is to avoid wrong answers, and the fastest way to avoid them is to copy or look them up. A student who hands in a page of honest errors shows the teacher what needs to be taught again, yet a points system marks that student down for it.

Homework is the one part of school that happens at home, so it carries the differences between students' evenings into their grades. One student has a quiet desk and a parent who remembers chemistry. Another works a closing shift or babysits younger siblings until they fall asleep, then starts the assignment. Tests ask both students the same questions under the same conditions; homework does not. When homework counts, part of the distance between those two evenings appears on the report card as if it were a difference in ability.

The strongest argument for grading homework is that students will not do work that does not count. Some will not, at least at first. But points do not guarantee practice either; they guarantee a completed page, which can be copied in five minutes. A student who skips the practice on ungraded homework will find the gap on the test, which is where that consequence belongs. Teachers can also get homework done without points. They can collect it and comment on it, or require finished homework before a student may retake a test, so a student who skipped the practice and failed has to go back and do it.

Making homework practice only has a cost. Some students who need points to get started will do less, and teachers will spend time checking work that no longer feeds the gradebook. A teacher can work on effort and habits directly, through comments and retake rules. Graded homework causes a problem that cannot be fixed later: once the points are in the gradebook, no one can separate the student's understanding from the help that went into the page. If homework stopped counting, a B in chemistry would mean that the student showed B-level chemistry on work the teacher watched her do.
