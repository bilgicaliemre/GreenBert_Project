Outflow

I record rain to the hundredth of an inch. On August 9th my rooftop station logged 1.62 inches in 38 minutes, with a 23-minute hole where my nana unplugged everything, because she thinks the lightning sensor attracts lightning.

The shoes I have never written down. At our family's tamale stand I guess orders from footwear: Chacos mean green corn, cowboy boots mean red chile pork. Then a woman in Chacos ordered pork and told me, "You don't know me." She was right, I didn't. My brother Gabe calls it confirmation bias. I never actually counted. I just kind of believe it.

Gabe did the stand's math on our fridge whiteboard: booth fee, masa, pork, husks, gas, and all our hours. He figures my mom makes $4.10 an hour on a normal Sunday, and she has a disc problem in her lower back. He wants her to stop the weekly markets. I keep saying people come for her. My evidence is Mr. Hollis, who has bought exactly seven every Sunday for five years. Gabe says Mr. Hollis is not a business plan.

On July 18th, driving to the Saturday night market, I saw a storm cell over the Rincons. After setup I sat on the cooler watching radar. The sandbags were my job, four at 25 pounds each, and they were still in the truck. My mom told me to get them. I said "in a sec," twice I think.

Around 6:40 brown dust came in from the east and Gabe said pack up. I told him the cell was splitting north. I actually said "trust me." My mom laughed: "La científica says we stay."

At 6:52 the gust front hit. Our canopy went up like a kite and cartwheeled two booths over into Mr. Duarte's honey table, fourteen jars, $168. Six dozen tamales went into the gravel. A little girl with a mango paleta was right there until her dad yanked her by the arm. Gabe didn't say anything and started picking up the glass. I think yelling would have been easier.

The rain did go north, and we got maybe four drops. What hit us was outflow, the wind that runs out ahead of a storm, which I'd read about all summer. I was so locked on the rain that I never thought about the wind.

When Gabe said pack up, I was thinking that if we left and the storm missed, that was one more point for his whiteboard. I wanted to win.

At home I went straight to the roof to check my station: 47 miles an hour, which means nothing six miles from the Mercado. My mom sat at the kitchen table going through the husks to see if any were still okay.

In the truck once, Gabe told me I want the stand to stay exactly the same so I can leave and find it there when I come back, like my nana's chair. I said that was stupid. But I'm the one who's probably leaving, and the one telling my mom to keep standing on gravel every Sunday with her back. He's right about the money. I still argue with him.

Now the sandbags go down before I look at the sky. I check radar every five minutes and don't announce forecasts anymore, so I can't tell whether I've changed or just stopped saying it out loud. Probably the second one.

In September my mom asked me, not Gabe, whether to drop the Saturday night market next summer. I said I'd look at the numbers. They're finished, in a tab next to the rain data, and I haven't told her what they say.

She paid Mr. Duarte that night, cash from her apron, and I said I'd pay her back. I'm at $60. She hasn't asked once, and part of me wants her to.
