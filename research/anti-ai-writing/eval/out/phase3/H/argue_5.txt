Let Teenagers Wake Up

The first bell rings, and a teacher starts in on the causes of the Civil War or the properties of logarithms. Half the room stares at the board without taking anything in. Someone in the back row is losing a fight to keep his eyes open. Adults tend to read this as laziness or boredom, but most of these students are just tired. High schools should start the day at 9:00 a.m. or later. Students would arrive ready to learn, mornings would be safer and calmer, and schools would finally weigh what teenagers need as heavily as what is convenient for the schedule.

School exists so that students learn, and exhausted students do not learn well. Anyone who has sat through a first-period class knows how different 7:30 a.m. feels from 10:30 a.m. Early in the morning, discussions die because nobody has the energy to raise a hand. A first-period quiz can end up measuring who managed to sleep instead of who understood the material. Going to bed earlier sounds like an easy fix, but many teenagers can't fall asleep early even when they try. Lights out at 9:30 p.m. often just means an hour of staring at the ceiling. Then the alarm goes off at 6:00 a.m., and the student who walks into first period is there in body and not much else. A later start would at least give teachers a room of people who are awake, which is more than a lot of first periods get now.

Mornings would also be safer. Many students drive themselves to school, and a half-asleep sixteen-year-old driving in the dark is a danger to himself and to everyone else on the road. Bus riders often wait on dark street corners before the sun is up. At home, the early start makes every morning a rush. Breakfast gets skipped and homework gets left behind, and there is often an argument about getting out the door on time. An extra hour would give a family time to eat and leave the house without a fight.

Critics have fair concerns. If school starts later, it ends later, which cuts into sports practices, part-time jobs, and homework time. Families who count on older siblings to watch younger kids after school might struggle, and bus routes are hard to rearrange. Schools should take these costs seriously. Still, each one is a scheduling problem, and schedules change all the time. Practice can move later, and an employer can push a shift back an hour. Districts already redraw bus routes whenever a school opens or closes. A teenager's need for sleep does not change because the district sends out a memo, so the schedule is the thing that should give.

Some people also argue that early starts teach responsibility and prepare students for the working world. Plenty of adult jobs don't begin at 7:30 a.m., though. Responsibility shows in the quality of a student's work, and sitting through a schedule that makes the work harder doesn't build it. If being responsible means showing up prepared, a rested student is far more prepared than an exhausted one.

Right now, many high school students spend the first hours of every school day just trying to stay awake. Moving the start time to 9:00 a.m. or later is a practical change that puts learning ahead of the bus schedule, and schools should make it.
