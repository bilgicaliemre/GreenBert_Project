First Impressions and the Groundwork of Prejudice

Elizabeth Bennet forms her opinion of Mr. Darcy from one overheard sentence. At the Meryton ball, Darcy calls her "tolerable: but not handsome enough to tempt me," and adds that he is in no humour to bother with "young ladies who are slighted by other men." The insult is real, and Austen gives the reader no reason to excuse it, so Elizabeth's dislike has a fair starting point. Her error is letting one evening stand for Darcy's whole character. When she refuses his first proposal in Chapter 34, she describes this process herself without seeing anything wrong with it. His manners, she tells him, formed "that groundwork of disapprobation, on which succeeding events have built so immovable a dislike." She chooses an image from building: the first impression is the foundation, and she stacks each later event on top of it without ever testing the foundation itself. Calling the dislike "immovable" even sounds like a boast, as if firmness proved accuracy.

Darcy's letter knocks that structure down. Alone in Chapter 36, Elizabeth cries, "How despicably have I acted!" and then, "I, who have prided myself on my discernment!" The quality she valued most in herself is the one that failed her. She traces the failure to vanity: "Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other, on the very beginning of our acquaintance, I have courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away where either were concerned." Warming to the man who preferred her and turning against the man who slighted her, she took those feelings for insight. The verbs she picks, "courted" and "driven," put the blame on her own choices. Her last line, "Till this moment, I never knew myself," shows that misreading Darcy was also a failure to know herself.

The novel sets the speed of Elizabeth's dislike against the slowness of her love. She can date the dislike to "the first moment" of their acquaintance, but when Jane asks how long she has loved Darcy, she says that "it has been coming on so gradually, that I hardly know when it began." Her half-joking choice to date it from "his beautiful grounds at Pemberley" mocks the idea that feelings have a clean starting point. Dislike arrived all at once; understanding took time and new evidence. Darcy was rude at the ball, so Elizabeth's first impression was accurate as far as it went. Austen shows that it was incomplete, and that it became dangerous once pride made it feel final. The title pairs pride with prejudice, and Elizabeth's case shows how one feeds the other: the surer she was of a quick judgment, the less willing she was to revise it.
