Let Practice Be Practice

A basketball player who misses free throws in an empty gym at nine o'clock at night doesn't lose a game. Missing is why she's there. Each miss shows her where her elbow drifts or how her release changes when she's tired, so by the time she steps to the line in front of a crowd, she has already failed enough times to know what to fix. Schools say they value this kind of learning, yet most classrooms grade homework as if every attempt were a performance. Homework should be practice only. Teachers should keep assigning it and going over it in class, but it shouldn't count toward a student's grade.

The first problem with grading homework is that it punishes the mistakes learning depends on. When a wrong answer on a worksheet costs points, students learn to avoid wrong answers instead of learning the material, and the fastest way to avoid them is to copy from a friend or let a search engine or an app do the work. Picture two students in the same algebra class. One struggles honestly through ten problems and gets six right. The other copies all ten from a classmate during lunch. The grade book rewards the second student. Worse, the teacher looks at a stack of perfect papers, assumes the class understands, and moves on. Nobody finds out otherwise until the test scores come back.

Graded homework also measures a student's home life as much as their understanding. One student goes home to a quiet room and a parent who remembers chemistry. Another works a shift after school or watches younger siblings until late, maybe in an apartment too small to have a quiet corner. A test taken in class puts everyone in the same room, under the same rules, with the same amount of time. Homework doesn't. If a grade is supposed to show what a student knows and can do, it shouldn't also be measuring how much free time and help that student has at home.

The strongest argument on the other side is that students won't do homework if it doesn't count. That's a fair worry. Teenagers are busy, and they're practical about where their effort goes. Points aren't the only reason to do the work, though. If homework closely matches what shows up on quizzes and tests, students who skip it will feel it on the assessments that do count. Teachers can still check whether it's done and write feedback on it, and the class can go over the common mistakes together. Besides, the students who do homework only for the points are the ones most likely to copy it, so for them the points were never producing much learning anyway.

Some people also argue that homework grades help students who understand the material but freeze on tests. That's a real problem, but padding a grade with worksheet points only hides it. A better fix is to give students more ways to show what they understand, such as retakes, projects, in-class writing, or explaining a solution out loud to the teacher.

The basketball player in the empty gym improves because nobody is keeping score. Once practice starts counting against you, it becomes one more performance, with all the pressure and shortcuts that come with it. Schools should let homework be the place where students are allowed to be wrong, and save grades for measuring what they've learned after they've had a fair chance to learn it.
