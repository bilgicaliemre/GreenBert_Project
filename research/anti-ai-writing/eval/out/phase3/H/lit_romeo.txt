Fate and haste in Romeo and Juliet

Romeo and Juliet tells the audience how it ends before either lover walks on stage. The Chorus says, "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross’d lovers take their life; / Whose misadventur’d piteous overthrows / Doth with their death bury their parents’ strife." Almost every loaded word here points away from personal choice. The lovers are "star-cross’d," so the heavens are against them from the start, and calling the parents' loins "fatal" makes it sound as if death were bred into the family line. "Misadventur’d" suggests bad luck more than bad judgment. Even "take their life" works two ways, since the lovers are born and also die by their own hands. On this evidence the play blames fate. A prologue only tells us where the story ends, though. The scenes after it show how the story gets there, and they keep coming back to one human failing, which is speed.

Friar Lawrence names that failing most clearly. When Romeo asks to be married to Juliet that same day, he says, "O let us hence; I stand on sudden haste." The Friar answers, "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast." This works almost like a second prologue. The Chorus predicts that the stars will cross the lovers, and the Friar predicts that running will trip them. Romeo ignores the warning. Just after killing Tybalt, he cries, "O, I am fortune’s fool!" He has acted on impulse and with his own hand, yet the first thing he does is call himself fortune's plaything. Shakespeare lets the audience hear the gap between what Romeo did and what he blames, and the line comes across as an excuse.

Romeo's last decision brings fate and haste together. When Balthasar tells him that Juliet is dead, he replies, "Is it even so? Then I defy you, stars! / Thou know’st my lodging. Get me ink and paper, / And hire post-horses. I will hence tonight." He believes he is rebelling against fate, but the rebellion is just more hurry. He asks one short question and then gives orders for paper and horses so he can leave that night. "I will hence tonight" also echoes his earlier "O let us hence," which ties this moment back to the scene where the Friar told him to slow down. By defying the stars, Romeo runs exactly as the Friar warned, and his running brings about the ending the Chorus promised. So the play does let fate decide how the story ends, but it blames Romeo's refusal to slow down for getting there.
