Why 2000 had a February 29 and 1900 didn't

The usual explanation for leap years is that Earth takes 365 and a quarter days to go around the Sun, so the leftover quarters add up to one extra day every four years. That's close, but the real year is about 11 minutes shorter than 365¼ days, and the rule that skips most century leap years exists to remove those 11 minutes. It's why 2000 was a leap year, 1900 wasn't, and 2100 won't be.

Calendars follow the seasons, so the year that counts is the time from one spring equinox to the next: about 365.2422 days. (A full orbit measured against the distant stars takes about 20 minutes longer, because the direction of Earth's axis slowly wobbles.) A calendar of whole days can't match that exactly. Without leap days, it would slip against the seasons by about 24 days every century.

Julius Caesar's calendar, which took effect in 45 BC, added a leap day every fourth year with no exceptions. That makes the average year 365.25 days, about 11 minutes too long, and 11 minutes a year adds up to a full day in about 128 years. In the 300s AD the spring equinox fell around March 21, the date churches used to work out Easter. By the late 1500s it was arriving around March 11. The arithmetic predicts that gap: roughly 1,250 years at one day per 128 years comes to almost 10 days.

Pope Gregory XIII's reform of 1582 deleted those ten days at once. In the countries that switched, Thursday, October 4, was followed by Friday, October 15. The reform also changed the leap-year rule so the drift wouldn't return. Over 400 years, Caesar's rule overshoots the seasons by about 3.1 days, so the new rule drops three leap days in every 400 years, and it takes them from the century years: a year ending in 00 is a leap year only if it divides evenly by 400. Under that rule, 1700, 1800 and 1900 lost their February 29. Britain switched only in 1752 and had to skip 11 days instead of 10, since 1700 had already been a leap year under the old rule.

The year 2000 kept its leap day because 3.1 is much closer to three than to four. Skipping every century year would remove four days per 400 years, almost a full day too many, and the calendar would drift the other way by a day in under 500 years. Removing three leaves an average year of 365.2425 days, less than half a minute longer than the seasons, which adds up to a single day of error only after more than 3,000 years.
