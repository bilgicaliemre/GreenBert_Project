At the Meryton ball in Chapter 3, Elizabeth overhears Darcy say she is "tolerable: but not handsome enough to tempt me." The remark is rude, and her resentment is fair. Her mistake comes afterward, when she lets one overheard sentence become the base of everything she thinks about him. Austen tracks this error through the way Elizabeth talks about beginnings: when she can name the exact moment her opinion of a person started, that opinion deserves suspicion. Darcy's line is a quick judgment too. He waves her off as one of the "young ladies who are slighted by other men," but he at least ties it to his mood "at present," while the judgment Elizabeth forms of him has no limit of that kind.

In Chapter 34, as she refuses his first proposal, Elizabeth describes her error exactly while offering it as a defense. Her dislike dates "From the very beginning, from the first moment," and the one hedge in the sentence, "I may almost say," softens only the date. About his character she has "the fullest belief." His manners laid a "groundwork of disapprobation," and "succeeding events have built" on it. In that picture, later events don't test the first impression. They add to it, until the dislike is "immovable," a word meant to end the discussion. Two chapters later, alone after reading Darcy's letter, she goes back to "the very beginning of our acquaintance," this time as the place where she went wrong. Both reasons are about how she was treated: "Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other." The "neglect" fits the scene at the ball, and it turns Darcy's mistake around. He judged her by how other men treated her, and she judged him by how he treated her. Her verbs put the fault on her ("courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away"), and so does her confidence: "I, who have prided myself on my discernment!" The moment she does date here comes after reading, not at a first meeting, and its verdict falls on herself: "Till this moment, I never knew myself."

Chapter 59 shows the other kind of judgment. When Jane asks how long she has loved Darcy, Elizabeth says it "has been coming on so gradually, that I hardly know when it began." The woman who dated her dislike to "the first moment" can't date her love. She does name a starting point, "my first seeing his beautiful grounds at Pemberley," but she's half joking, and the joke sounds like her teasing the habit Chapter 34 showed. The novel suggests that a judgment formed at a first meeting out of hurt pride, even one with a real insult behind it, becomes the ground for everything after it, and the person holding it mistakes it for discernment. The opinion Elizabeth can trust is her love, which came on too gradually to date.
