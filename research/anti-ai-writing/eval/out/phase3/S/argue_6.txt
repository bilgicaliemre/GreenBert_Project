Keep homework out of the grade

Homework is supposed to be practice, the place where a student gets a new kind of problem wrong before it shows up on a test. If that's its job, grading it for accuracy makes little sense, because it takes away points at the moment mistakes are most useful. The obvious compromise is completion credit: homework gets a fixed share of the final grade, and any assignment turned in with every problem attempted earns full points. That fails too, because turning an assignment in is not the same as learning the material. Homework should still be assigned and handed back with comments, but it shouldn't count toward the grade at all.

A grade should report what a student can do with the material. A made-up example shows how much completion credit blurs that. Suppose homework is worth 20 percent of a class grade and tests are worth the other 80. Student A works a closing shift three nights a week, so she turns in only half the homework, but she averages 90 on tests. Student B copies a friend's answers every night, so he gets full homework credit, and he averages 75 on tests. With homework counted, A gets 72 points from tests and 10 from homework, for an 82. B gets 60 from tests and 20 from homework, for an 80. The report card now shows two nearly identical students, even though their tests are 15 points apart.

Removing the points also protects what homework can tell a teacher: where the class is stuck. When homework is worth points, a student stuck on a problem late at night has a reason to copy the answer instead of leaving it blank. If the assignment earns nothing, copying gains nothing, and the blank or the wrong answer comes in as it is. If a third of the class set up the same equation wrong, the teacher can see it and start the next class there. A stack of copied answers would have shown her a class that understood everything.

The strongest objection is that without points, busy students will skip the practice and find out on the test what they missed. If a student has a job and four other classes, an ungraded worksheet is the easiest thing to drop, and a point value is a reason not to drop it. Practice needs some pull, so homework needs something attached to it, just not a grade of its own. A teacher can open the next class with a short quiz built from the homework problems, and the quiz counts. Since it's done in class without a friend's answers, it shows what the student can do alone. Student B's copied answers wouldn't help him there, while Student A would do fine on material she already knows. A teacher can also make finished homework the requirement for retaking a test. Then a student who skipped the practice and failed the test has to go back and do it, but a student who skipped it and passed loses nothing, because the test already showed she didn't need it.

This plan does cost the students whose homework points were holding up low test scores. With the weights in the example, full homework credit lifts a 65 test average to a 72. That loss is real, but the 72 covers up a gap the tests had already found. The retake rule gives that student another way to raise the grade: do the practice, take the test again, and earn a score that says what she now knows.
