At my mom's tamale stand I can guess people's orders from their shoes. Chacos, green corn. Crocs, cheese and jalapeño. My brother Gabe calls it confirmation bias, and I can't prove him wrong, because I've never written it down once. Rain I write down to the hundredth of an inch, from a weather station I built on our roof in Tucson.

Gabe did the stand's numbers on our fridge whiteboard. Counting all our hours, my mom makes $4.10 an hour on a normal Sunday, and she has a disc thing in her lower back. He wants her to quit the weekly markets. I keep saying people come for her, like Mr. Hollis, who has bought exactly seven every Sunday for five years. Gabe says Mr. Hollis is not a business plan. He's right about the money, and my side, like the shoes, is something I just kind of believe.

On July 18th Gabe worked the Saturday night market with us. I'd seen a storm cell over the Rincon Mountains on the drive, so after setup I sat on the cooler watching radar. The canopy's four 25-pound sandbags were my job, and they were still in the truck. My mom told me to get them. I said "in a sec," twice, I think. Around 6:40 brown dust came from the east, and Gabe said let's pack up. I said the cell was splitting and going north. I actually said "trust me." My mom laughed: "La científica says we stay." At 6:52 the gust front hit. Our canopy went up like a kite and cartwheeled two booths over into Mr. Duarte's honey table: fourteen jars, $168. A little girl with a mango paleta was right there, and her dad yanked her by the arm. Nobody got hurt. Gabe didn't say anything, just started picking up the glass, and I think him yelling would have been easier.

The rain did go north. We got about four drops. What hit us was the outflow, the wind that runs out ahead of a storm, which I'd been reading about all summer. I was so locked on where the rain was going that the wind wasn't in my head at all. At home my mom went through the husks at the kitchen table, seeing if any were still okay. I was up on the roof with my graph.

What I was thinking when Gabe said pack up is worse to admit. If we left and the storm missed, that was one more lost night for his whiteboard. If we stayed and it missed, I'd be right in front of him. It wasn't really about customers or tamales. I just wanted to win.

Gabe once told me I want the stand to stay exactly the same so I can go away and it'll still be there when I come back, like my nana Chayo's chair. I told him that was stupid. Now I don't know. I'm the one who's leaving, probably, and I'm also the one saying keep doing it, standing on the gravel every Sunday, with her back.

I put the sandbags down first now, before I even look at the sky. I still check radar about every five minutes, but I don't announce forecasts at the stand anymore. Maybe I changed, or maybe I just stopped saying it out loud. Probably that one. My mom paid Mr. Duarte the $168 that night, cash, out of her apron, and I said I'd pay her back. I'm at $60. She hasn't asked once, and part of me wishes she would.

In September she asked me, just me, not Gabe, whether she should drop the Saturday night market next summer. I said I'd look at the numbers. They're done, in a tab of my Google Sheet next to the rain, and I haven't said anything back to her yet.
