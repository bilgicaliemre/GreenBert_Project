How do noise-cancelling headphones make sound disappear?

If you've switched on noise cancelling on a bus or a plane, the engine's rumble probably faded to a faint hum while the conversation two rows back still came through. Both are sound, so why does one fade and not the other? Noise-cancelling headphones work by adding sound: they play a flipped copy of the noise, timed to meet the original inside your ear cup.

Sound is a pressure wave. A vibrating object squeezes and stretches the air, and those pressure changes travel to your eardrum and push it in and out. The number of push-pull cycles per second is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). People hear from roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, and an engine's drone sits near the low end.

When two sound waves reach the same spot, their pressures add. If one wave's high-pressure peaks always arrive with the low-pressure troughs of an equal wave, they add up to almost nothing. Physicists call this destructive interference, and the headphones set it up on purpose. A small microphone on the outside of each ear cup picks up the noise, a chip builds the inverted wave (peaks swapped for troughs), and the speaker plays it along with your music. None of this makes the bus quieter for anyone else, because the cancelling only works in the small pocket of air between the speaker and your eardrum.

The hard part is timing. The microphone, chip and speaker each take a moment, and how much that delay hurts depends on how long one cycle lasts. Suppose the electronics run a tenth of a millisecond late. A 100 Hz hum takes 10 milliseconds per cycle, so that delay is a 1 percent slip and the cancelling still mostly works. A 5,000 Hz sound, in the range of the hiss in an "s", takes only 0.2 milliseconds per cycle. There the same delay is half a cycle, which shifts the inverted wave so its peaks line up with the noise's peaks, and the sound gets louder instead of quieter. To avoid that, designers keep the active cancelling to low frequencies.

On the bus, the engine's rumble is low and steady, so the electronics can stay in step with it. Much of what makes speech understandable, such as the hiss of an "s" or the snap of a "t", sits at a few thousand hertz, where the electronics have mostly stepped back. Those sounds are turned down only by the ear cushions and the seal around your ear. Padding blocks high frequencies better than low ones, the reverse of the electronics, so the voices reach you muffled but not cancelled.
