Fate and haste in Romeo and Juliet

The Prologue makes the strongest case for fate. Before anyone on stage has done anything, the Chorus calls Romeo and Juliet "star-cross’d lovers" and their downfalls "misadventur’d piteous overthrows," and the Chorus has no reason to excuse anyone. Still, its words for fate and bad luck ("fatal," "star-cross’d," "misadventur’d") are all adjectives. "Star-cross’d" is a past participle, so the stars' work is done before the sentence reaches its verb, and that verb belongs to the lovers: they "take their life" (are born) from "the fatal loins of these two foes." In the later passages too, fate describes the characters or gets spoken to, but it is never the subject of a verb. People do things, and the action that keeps coming back is hurrying. The play blames haste. The stars and the parents' feud set the conditions, and inside the scenes, fate is a word Romeo reaches for when he has just acted or is about to.

Haste gets named outright in Act II, Scene 3, when Romeo asks Friar Lawrence to marry him to Juliet that same day: "O let us hence; I stand on sudden haste." The Friar answers, "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast." His verb disagrees with the Chorus's noun. An overthrow is done to you, but a stumble comes from your own feet, and the Friar ties it to speed, not to the stars. Taken alone, this is one character's advice. Romeo's own words in Act V, Scene 1 show he still hasn't taken it. Told that Juliet is dead, he asks, "Is it even so?" and, with no answer, defies the stars and gives orders: "Get me ink and paper, / And hire post-horses. I will hence tonight." "Hence" is the word he used to rush the Friar toward the wedding. He answers the chance to marry Juliet and the news of her death with the same word, and both times it means leave now.

Among the characters in these passages, only Romeo blames fate, and he does it at the moments he acts. Just after killing Tybalt in Act III, Scene 1, he cries, "O, I am fortune’s fool!" That's testimony from the killer himself, and it hands the deed to fortune. In Act V he turns around: "Then I defy you, stars!" Now he's fate's opponent instead of its fool, and his defiance takes the form of post-horses and a departure that night. By his own account, what he does next is done against the stars, not by them. The Chorus's "star-cross’d" keeps the play from clearing fate completely, since the lovers are born to two foes and the Prologue announces their deaths before the first scene. The haste shown here is also Romeo's alone, since no passage quotes Juliet. But no star in them makes a choice. Romeo does, at the speed the Friar said ends in a stumble, so the play puts the blame on haste.
