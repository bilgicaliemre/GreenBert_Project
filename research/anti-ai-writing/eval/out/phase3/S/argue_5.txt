Move the first bell to nine

The easy answer to a tired teenager is "go to bed earlier," and by that logic a later first bell would only let high school students stay up later. That answer treats going to bed and falling asleep as the same thing. A student decides when the light goes off, but nobody decides exactly when sleep comes, and the school's start time sets the alarm. High schools should start at 9:00, because the morning is the end of the night that students can't move on their own.

Suppose a junior's school starts at 7:30. Her bus comes at 6:50, so her alarm is set for 6:10. She has practice until 5:30, eats dinner, and finishes her homework at 9:30. She takes the advice and is in bed by 9:30, but suppose she isn't sleepy until 11:00. She lies awake for an hour and a half and gets seven hours and ten minutes of sleep. Now move the start to 9:00. Her alarm moves to 7:40. Dismissal, practice and homework all slide by ninety minutes, so she finishes at 11:00, about when she would have fallen asleep anyway, and she sleeps until 7:40, a total of eight hours and forty minutes. Her evening didn't get shorter, and she didn't need more discipline. The hour and a half she used to spend lying awake became an hour and a half of sleep at the end of the night.

Take a second junior with the same schedule who does fall asleep at 9:30. Under the 7:30 start she sleeps from 9:30 to 6:10, which is eight hours and forty minutes. Under the 9:00 start her evening slides the same ninety minutes, and she sleeps from 11:00 to 7:40, which is also eight hours and forty minutes. So the later start gives the first student an extra hour and a half and costs the second one nothing. Both of them do stay up later under the new schedule, just as the "go to bed earlier" side predicts, but staying up later isn't the same as sleeping less.

The strongest objection is about the afternoon. If the school day stays the same length, a 9:00 start means students leave about ninety minutes later. A student who works a shift that starts at 4:00, or who has to be home when a younger brother gets off the elementary school bus, has an afternoon set by someone else's clock. For that student, the later dismissal takes ninety minutes out of time that was already spoken for. That objection is right, and a district that only moved the bell would be trading those students' afternoons for everyone else's mornings. So the 9:00 start should be the default, with a way out: an early track that starts at 7:30 and ends at the old dismissal time, with its own bus. Students on that track would still be trading sleep for time, but they'd be choosing that trade for a reason they know, and the school would stop making it for everyone. The early track has a price: a second bus run, and teachers willing to teach its classes.

A district weighing the change could ask its own juniors two questions. On a night when you go to bed early, what time do you actually fall asleep? And what would you lose if school ended ninety minutes later? The answers to the first question show how many students a 9:00 start would help, and the answers to the second show how many seats the early track would need.
