Phones off from the first bell to the last

Most high school classrooms have at least one phone lying face-down on a desk. When it buzzes, the student who owns it stops listening, and often the student next to them does too. High schools should ban student phones for the entire school day, lunch and passing periods included: phones turned off and zipped into backpacks from the first bell to the last, with exceptions only for medical needs.

Students who defend phones in class usually say they only check them for a second, and that is probably true. The second is not where the time goes. A phone on the desk keeps a question open all period: did anyone answer, and what is the group chat saying now? Looking takes a second, but wondering lasts the whole class, and even a student disciplined enough never to touch the phone is spending some attention on not touching it. Turning the phone off before first period settles the question once instead of every few minutes.

The usual compromise is to ban phones in class and allow them at lunch and between classes. That keeps the problem alive. Conversations that start in the hallway continue under desks after the bell, and teachers spend the start of class asking for phones to be put away, one student at a time. Under a full-day ban there is nothing to negotiate, since any phone in sight breaks the rule. The full-day ban also solves a problem students cannot solve alone. A student who turns her phone off on her own misses the plans and the jokes, because the group chat keeps going without her. When every phone in the building is off, the chat pauses for everyone and nobody falls behind.

The strongest objection comes from parents who want to reach their children if something goes wrong. Phones that are off and in backpacks have not been taken away; in a real emergency, a student can get to one in seconds. The ban only stops the everyday texts, like who is picking whom up after practice. Those messages can wait until dismissal or go through the front office, the way families reached students before everyone carried a phone.

Another objection is that students will have phones for life, so school should teach them to manage one instead of taking it away. Students already practice that every evening and every weekend, with no one watching. The school day is the one part of the week built around paying attention to something difficult for a full class period. Making every class a contest between students and their phones, refereed by the teacher, leaves less time for the lesson. The ban does cost something real. Phones work as calculators and as cameras for copying notes off the whiteboard, and students would lose both. I think the trade is worth it, because a pencil, a school calculator or a laptop can do most of those jobs.

None of this fixes what happens after school. Every notification from the day will be waiting at dismissal, and some students will spend the bus ride catching up. I do not know whether a phone-free school day makes students better at putting the phone down at night or only more eager to grab it at the last bell; that part belongs to students and their families. But a text sent during second period reads the same after the last bell, and the lesson taught in second period happens only once.
