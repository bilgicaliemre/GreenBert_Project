Until last month, I had never once answered the phone at my dad's shop with its name. The shop is Carvalho Shoe Service, on Pleasant Street since 1994. It smells like Barge cement and leather dust, and so does my hoodie. On Saturdays I work the counter, and since last summer I'm allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. I hate the phone. My stutter gets worse on it, especially on hard sounds like "C," which is a problem, because the shop's name starts with my last name. So I said "Hi, shoe repair." My dad noticed after about a year and never said anything.

Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept that up for three weeks, and when the progress report needed a parent signature, I forged hers. The forging is the part I'd rather leave out, because I practiced her signature about eight times first, on the back of a math worksheet. It wasn't a panic thing; I planned it. When Mr. Oduya emailed home, I was relieved, though I didn't tell anyone that. Keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting. My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test to her, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese, which I'm not good at for chemistry words. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed.

I ended the class with a B+, and after that I made a rule: I keep a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even small ones like "I'm almost there." Since October of sophomore year, 212 lies have gone on it, most of them about being on time. I'm still not sure if the list makes me more honest or just better at noticing.

Last month a man called the shop, and I said the name. I was trying to picture his face, whether he was annoyed, and on the second try I almost hung up. He waited. When "Carvalho Shoe Service" came out on the third try, I didn't feel proud; I felt kind of embarrassed that it took that many. Then he asked whether we fix purses (we don't), as if nothing had happened, and that was the best part: it was nothing to him.

I only noticed later, when someone asked me about that call, that "Hi, shoe repair" was a lie too. A small one, and it isn't on my list. I'd been doing the same thing with the phone that I did with the chem test, swapping the true thing for an easier thing: an 85 for a 54, "shoe repair" for Carvalho. I'm not sure what to do with that yet, except maybe add it to the list.

I want to study speech-language pathology, or something about how the brain gets words back, and I still don't know whether I can do that with a stutter. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my speech therapist from middle school, if I'd be "allowed," she said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet. My avó, Rosa, had a stroke two years ago and has aphasia; she understands more than she can say. On Tuesdays and Thursdays after school we go through flashcards I made her, pictures with Portuguese words, and she's relearning the words while I'm learning them for the first time. She's better than me at "agulha," needle, and I'm better at "telemóvel," cell phone. I read a lot about aphasia now, and I kind of wanted it to be connected to my stutter, but they're totally different things. My avó can't say my name yet, but she can say "sapato," shoe.
