My avó, Rosa, can say "sapato" (shoe), but not my name yet. She has aphasia from a stroke two years ago and understands more than she can say; sometimes she calls me Lurdes, the name of an aunt who died before I was born. Tuesdays and Thursdays we do Portuguese picture flashcards I made her. She's relearning words I'm learning for the first time; she's better at "agulha" (needle), I'm better at "telemóvel."

Now I read a lot about aphasia, because I want to know how a brain gets words back. I kind of wanted it to connect to my stutter, but they're totally different things.

I keep a notebook of heels from my dad's shoe repair shop, 61 entries. One woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left; it turned out she drives for work, and that heel rests by the pedal. Heels and avó feel like the same problem: figuring out what's going on with someone from the outside, when they can't tell you. With heels it's easy. With avó it isn't: when she gets a word wrong, I can't tell what she's thinking. I don't know if that connection is real or one I just want, like the stutter and the aphasia.

I want to study speech-language pathology. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my middle school speech therapist, if I'd be "allowed" to be one with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.
