I can tell how someone walks from the wear on their heels. I keep a notebook of them, 61 entries, and entry 1 is my dad's work boots. When one woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left, I said out loud that she must drive for work, since that heel rests on the floor by the pedal, and she looked at me like I was a psychic.

My dad's shop, Carvalho Shoe Service, has been on Pleasant Street since 1994. I work the counter on Saturdays. My friends say I smell like a cowboy's basement: Barge cement and leather dust. I hate the phone. I have a stutter that gets worse on it, especially on hard sounds like C, and the shop is called Carvalho, so I used to say "Hi, shoe repair" instead. My dad noticed after about a year and never said anything.

Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept it up for three weeks and forged her signature on the progress report, after practicing it about eight times on the back of a math worksheet. That's the part I'd rather leave out, because it means I planned it. When my teacher, Mr. Oduya, emailed home, I was relieved, and I told nobody that; keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting. My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed.

I finished the class with a B+ and made a rule: I keep a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even small ones like "I'm almost there." It has 212 items since October of sophomore year, most of them about being on time. I'm still not sure if the list makes me more honest or just better at noticing.

On Tuesdays and Thursdays after school I do flashcards with my avó, my grandmother Rosa, who has aphasia from a stroke two years ago. She understands more than she can say. She can say "sapato," but she can't say my name yet. I made the cards with pictures and Portuguese words, which she's relearning and I'm learning for the first time.

Heels and avó both mean figuring out what's going on with someone from the outside, when they can't tell you. Heels are easy. With avó I can't tell what she's thinking when she gets a word wrong, and I don't know if the connection is real or if I just want it to be. I want to study speech-language pathology, or how the brain gets words back, but when I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my middle school speech therapist, if I'd be "allowed" to be one with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter, and I don't fully believe her yet.

Last month I answered the shop phone with "Carvalho Shoe Service." It took three tries, and the man on the line waited. I was trying to picture his face, whether he was annoyed, and on the second try I almost hung up. When the name came out I didn't feel proud, just kind of embarrassed it took three tries. Then he asked if we fix purses (we don't), like nothing had happened, and that was the best part, that it was nothing to him.

I think "Hi, shoe repair" was a lie too. It's a small one, and it isn't on my list; I only noticed when someone asked me about that call. I've been doing the same thing with the phone that I did with the chemistry test, swapping the true thing for an easier thing. Maybe it goes on the list.
