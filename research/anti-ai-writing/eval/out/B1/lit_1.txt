A green light on a dock

In The Great Gatsby the green light is worth the most when Gatsby is far from it, and the novel shows its value falling as soon as he gets close. In Chapter 1 Gatsby "stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way," and the narrator, glancing seaward, "distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." The sentence keeps shrinking its object: there is "nothing except" the light, the light is "minute," and the narrator cannot say for certain what it is attached to ("might have been"). The large things in the scene are Gatsby's outstretched arms and the trembling the narrator "could have sworn" to, so whatever the light means here, Gatsby is the one supplying it.

Chapter 5 tests that meaning, because Gatsby is finally talking to Daisy, and he tells her, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock." The words "always" and "all night" give him away: he knows how long it burns because he has watched it. Once he says this to her, the light has nothing left to do. The narrator guesses ("Possibly") that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever," and then explains why. Across "the great distance" between Gatsby and Daisy, the light "had seemed very near to her, almost touching her. It had seemed as close as a star to the moon." That comparison works against itself. A star looks close to the moon only to someone standing far below both, so the closeness was an effect of the distance and ends when the distance ends: "Now it was again a green light on a dock." After "colossal significance," those flat words read like a demotion. "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one" sounds like bookkeeping, and it implies Gatsby had other enchanted objects that could go the same way.

By Chapter 9 the light no longer points at Daisy. "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." Daisy is missing from that sentence; the light is now "the orgastic future," and the sentence begins with Gatsby and ends with "us." The narrator, who in Chapter 1 could barely make the light out, now talks like a fellow believer: "It eluded us then, but that's no matter—to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther." Stretching out the arms was Gatsby's gesture in Chapter 1, and here the narrator hands it to everyone. I read this as the narrator joining Gatsby's habit instead of judging it. The passage breaks off at "And one fine morning——," and the unfinished sentence lets the narrator skip over what that morning would bring. Chapter 5 already answered that: Gatsby got there, and the light went back to being "a green light on a dock."
