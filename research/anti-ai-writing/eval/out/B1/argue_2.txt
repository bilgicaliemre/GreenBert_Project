Phones away, bell to bell

High schools should ban student phones for the whole school day, from the first bell to the last. That includes lunch and the walk between classes. Phones would stay in lockers or zipped inside backpacks, switched off. The softer rule, banning phones only during class, sounds like a fair compromise, but it is the version most likely to fail.

A phone distracts even when nobody touches it. It sits face-down on the desk or in a hoodie pocket, and it buzzes. The student may not check it, but part of their attention has already left for the group chat: who wrote, and what did they say? When the teacher moves on to the next step of a proof, that student is listening with whatever is left. Apps send notifications because notifications bring people back. A teenager is being asked to ignore that pull for an entire class period, every period of the day, which plenty of adults cannot manage at their own desks.

A class-only rule hands that fight to the teacher. Each period opens with "phones away," and someone usually slides one under the desk anyway. The teacher can stop the lesson to deal with it or pretend not to see, and different teachers choose differently. Students notice. They learn that the rule depends on who is standing at the front of the room. When the phone is in a locker, there is nothing to negotiate, and class can start when the bell rings.

Lunch and passing periods need the rule as much as class does. The hallway is where a fight gets filmed, and the video can reach the whole grade before the people in it reach their next class. Someone who trips on the stairs or cries after a hard test should be seen by the few people standing there, not by everyone in a group chat. Lunch is also the longest stretch of the day when students are free to talk to each other, and that is hard to do across a table where everyone is looking down at a screen.

The strongest objection is about safety: if there is an emergency, parents want to reach their kids. But the phones are not leaving the building. They are in a locker or a backpack a few feet away. The front office has a phone, and a parent who needs a student in the middle of the day can call it, the way parents did for decades before students carried cell phones.

Another objection is that students need to learn to manage their phones, and a ban only postpones that lesson. Self-control does need practice. School is an expensive place to practice it, though, because the price of losing is a missed explanation in chemistry or a quiz question nobody remembers being taught. Students can work on managing a phone in the hours when they are not supposed to be learning something else.

The ban needs exceptions, settled in advance. Some students use their phones to read a glucose monitor or for other medical reasons, and they should keep them. Those exceptions should be approved through the nurse's office, not by each teacher, or the ban slides back into the class-by-class bargaining it was meant to end.
