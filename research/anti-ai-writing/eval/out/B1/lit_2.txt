Distance and the green light

In The Great Gatsby the green light stands for Daisy only while Gatsby cannot reach her. Its meaning depends on distance: when the distance closes, the light shrinks back into an ordinary object, and by Chapter 9 it stands for something nobody can reach. In Chapter 1 the narrator is not even sure what it is. He notices that Gatsby "stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way," and he glances out to sea "involuntarily," as if the gesture pulled his eyes along with it. He finds "nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." Each detail makes the light smaller. It is "single," it is "minute," and it belongs to a dock only through "might have been." Whatever importance it has comes from Gatsby, who seems to be "trembling" in front of something the narrator can barely see.

Chapter 5 confirms that the light is on Daisy's dock, and in the same chapter the narrator suspects it has stopped meaning anything. Gatsby tells her, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock." "Always" and "all night" suggest he has watched it long enough to know its habits. The narrator then guesses ("Possibly") that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever," and explains it with an odd comparison: from across the water the light "had seemed as close as a star to the moon." A star looks close to the moon only from far below, so the simile describes a closeness that exists only at a distance. With the distance gone, the light is "again a green light on a dock." "Again" tells us it was ordinary before Gatsby attached Daisy to it. The passage closes in the language of bookkeeping: "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one." Gatsby keeps an inventory of enchanted things, and getting near what one of them stood for takes it off the list.

In Chapter 9 the narrator gives the light a new definition: "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." A future that recedes "year by year" moves away as fast as anyone approaches it, so this light cannot be reached, and it cannot go flat the way it did in Chapter 5. Then the narrator joins in: "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther." Stretching out the arms is Gatsby's gesture from Chapter 1, the one the narrator called "curious." The next words, "And one fine morning," break off before saying what happens that morning, so even the grammar never arrives. The passage leaves the narrator's attitude unsettled. "It eluded us then, but that's no matter" could be stubborn hope or a refusal to admit the chase has failed, and nothing in these lines decides which.
