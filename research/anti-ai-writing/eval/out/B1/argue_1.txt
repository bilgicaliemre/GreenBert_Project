Lock the phones up at first bell

The usual school phone rule sounds reasonable: phones stay in your bag or face down on the desk unless the teacher says otherwise. But the phone is still in the room. It buzzes against a desk during a quiz, or lights up inside an open backpack, and half the class turns to look. High schools should ban phones for the whole school day. Students would lock them away or hand them in at the first bell and get them back at dismissal, and the ban would cover lunch and passing periods too.

A rule that only covers class time hands the job of enforcing it to every teacher, every period. Teachers do that job differently. One lets students use their phones as calculators; the next takes a phone the moment she sees it. Students figure out quickly which rooms are strict, and class starts with a reminder to put phones away instead of with the lesson. A full-day ban is enforced once, at the door, and nobody has to argue about it again until the last bell.

A face-down phone still costs attention. When it buzzes, you don't have to read the message for it to pull at you. Part of your mind goes to guessing who it was and whether it can wait, and that guessing doesn't stop until you check. A phone in a locker can't buzz.

Lunch belongs in the ban, even though students will hate that part most. For a lot of students, lunch is the only time in the day to talk to friends who aren't in their classes. With phones out, a lunch table can turn into a row of people reading separate screens. Hallways have their own problem: a fight or an embarrassing fall can be filmed and posted before the next bell, and the person in the video has to sit through fifth period knowing who has seen it.

The strongest argument against a ban is that teenagers need to learn to manage their phones, and a school that takes them away teaches nothing. I agree that we have to learn it, but school is a bad place to start. Many of the apps on a student's phone earn money from ads, so they are designed to keep people scrolling. Asking a sixteen-year-old to resist that for an entire school day, while also following a chemistry lecture, sets students up to fail and then punishes them for failing. Evenings and weekends are a better place to practice, because losing an hour to a phone there doesn't mean missing a lesson.

Parents' worry about emergencies is the objection I take most seriously. For most emergencies, such as a sick grandparent or a change in who is picking a student up, a parent can call the front office, which is how families reached students before cell phones existed. Lockdowns are harder. A student hiding in a dark classroom would want to text home, and a parent would want to read that text. I don't think a ban fully answers this. What a school can do is tell parents exactly how and how quickly it will contact them during a lockdown, then test that promise in every lockdown drill. A school that can't make that promise isn't ready to collect phones yet.
