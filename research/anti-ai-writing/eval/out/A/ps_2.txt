Hi, Shoe Repair

Until last month, I answered the phone at my dad's shop by saying, "Hi, shoe repair."

The shop is Carvalho Shoe Service, on Pleasant Street in Fall River, open since 1994. I have a stutter that gets worse on the phone, and worst on hard sounds like "C," which is a problem when your family's name starts with one. So I left the name out. My dad noticed after about a year. He never said anything.

On Saturdays I work the counter and write tickets, and since last summer I've been allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. The shop smells like Barge cement and leather dust, and so does my hoodie. My friends say I smell like a cowboy's basement.

My best skill there requires no talking. I can tell how someone walks from the wear on their heel. An outer edge worn down means they supinate. One woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left, and I said out loud that she must drive for work, her right heel resting on the floor by the pedal. She did, and she looked at me like I was a psychic. I keep a notebook of heels now. It has 61 entries. Entry 1 is my dad's work boots.

I keep another list, on my phone, and I'm less proud of why. Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chemistry test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept it up for three weeks, even forging her signature on a progress report, until my teacher, Mr. Oduya, emailed home. My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test to her, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese. It took two hours. When I couldn't find the word for "mole," I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed. I finished the class with a B+. Since then I have written down every lie I tell, even "I'm almost there." The list has 212 items, most of them about being on time. I'm still not sure whether it makes me more honest or just better at noticing.

Noticing is most of what I do on Tuesdays and Thursdays after school, with my avó, Rosa. Two years ago she had a stroke, and now she has aphasia. She understands more than she can say. I made her flashcards with pictures, in Portuguese, and we go through them together. She is relearning words I am learning for the first time. She beats me on "agulha," needle. I beat her on "telemóvel," cell phone. Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, the name of an aunt who died before I was born. At first I corrected her. Now I don't. She can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato." Shoe.

I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics, maybe whatever explains how the brain gets words back. I read about aphasia all the time now. I was almost disappointed to learn that stuttering and aphasia are completely different things. I wanted them to be connected, and they aren't. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my speech therapist from middle school, whether I'd be "allowed" to become a speech therapist with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.

Last month the shop phone rang, and I said "Carvalho Shoe Service." It took three tries. The man on the other end waited. He wanted to know if we fix purses. We don't.

My avó and I are both working on names. She can't say my name yet, and until last month I couldn't say our family's name to a stranger on the phone. Between her flashcards and that patient caller, I've learned that the most useful thing a listener can do is wait. I'd like to make a career of it.
