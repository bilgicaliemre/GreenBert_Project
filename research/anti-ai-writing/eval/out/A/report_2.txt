Claim Extraction

The stage of GreenBERT built so far is a rule-based pipeline that isolates the sentences of a corporate ESG report that make statements about environmental, social or governance conduct. Each PDF report is converted to text with PyMuPDF, retaining the page number of every passage, and then segmented into sentences with NLTK.

Every sentence must first pass a gate: it must contain an ESG topic word together with either an action verb or a hard quantity. Action verbs include first-person and past-tense forms such as "we reduced", while hard quantities are numbers carrying units, or counts. A sentence that mentions an ESG topic but contains neither element is treated as description rather than as a claim and is dropped. Sentences that clear the gate then pass through a series of non-claim filters, which remove boilerplate, reference-dense text, navigation elements and headers, policy content, descriptions of governance roles, activity reports, methodology text, general statements and risk descriptions. A further filter removes broken fragments, which we detect by using a part-of-speech tagger to check that the sentence contains a verb. Because filtering can discard genuine claims, we save every rejected sentence with its drop reason so that recall can be audited.

Each retained claim is annotated along four dimensions. We assign an ESG label of E, S or G according to which category's keywords dominate the sentence. We classify the claim type as a Future Promise, a Strong claim (one containing a hard quantity) or a Vague claim; for example, "We are committed to a sustainable future" is labelled Vague, whereas "We reduced Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 4% against our 2019 baseline" is labelled Strong. We record whether supporting evidence is present (Yes, Partial or No), and we attach a Risk_Signal. Of the six reports currently loaded, one Unilever report yields 583 claims and one Dr Pepper report yields 218.

All our evaluation figures are in-sample and should therefore be read as optimistic. On a 26-sentence development set the extractor reaches 92% precision, but because the filters were tuned on these sentences we do not treat this as a headline result. An independent manual review of 100 claims produced by an earlier version (v3) found 67% precision. Automated full-corpus audits by AI agents, which are stricter than human reviewers, gave precision of 33% for v3, 39% for v4.0 and 44% for the final v4. Recall, estimated by auditing 1,767 rejected sentences, rose from 48% under v4.0 to 73% under the final v4. The residual errors concern meaning rather than surface wording, which indicates that keyword rules have reached a semantic ceiling; this motivates the next stage, a BERT classifier that has not yet been built.
