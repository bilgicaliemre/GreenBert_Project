How the Brain Gets Words Back

My avó, Rosa, can say "sapato." She can't say my name yet.

Two years ago she had a stroke, and the aphasia it left behind means she understands more than she can say. Every Tuesday and Thursday after school, we work through flashcards I made for her: pictures, Portuguese words. She is relearning them, and I am learning some for the first time. She beats me at "agulha," needle. I beat her at "telemóvel."

At that table, a question took hold of me: where do words go, and how does the brain get them back? Why does "shoe" come back before a granddaughter's name? Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, my aunt, who died before I was born. I used to correct her. Now I listen, because even a wrong word is a word she found.

I started reading about aphasia partly hoping it would explain my own stutter, which gets worse on hard sounds like "C." That's inconvenient when the family shoe repair shop is called Carvalho. I wanted the two conditions to be connected. They aren't. They're completely different, and learning that disappointed me and made me more curious at the same time.

I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics. When I asked my middle school speech therapist, Ms. Halvorsen, whether I'd be "allowed" to become one with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet. I'd like to find out.
