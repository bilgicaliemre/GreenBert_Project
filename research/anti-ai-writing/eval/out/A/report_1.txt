Claim Extraction

The first stage of the GreenBERT pipeline is a rule-based extractor that separates the claims in a corporate ESG report from the surrounding description and boilerplate. Each report is converted from PDF to text with PyMuPDF, preserving page numbers, and the text is then split into sentences with NLTK.

Every sentence must first pass a lexical gate. A sentence is retained only if it contains an ESG topic word and, in addition, either an action verb, including first-person and past-tense forms such as "we reduced", or a hard quantity, such as a number with a unit or a count. A sentence that names an ESG topic without an action or a quantity is treated as description and dropped. Sentences that pass the gate are then screened by a series of non-claim filters, which remove boilerplate, reference-dense text, navigation elements and headers, broken fragments (detected by using a part-of-speech tagger to check for a verb), policy content, descriptions of governance roles, activity reports, methodology text, general statements and risk descriptions. We save every rejected sentence together with its drop reason, so that recall can be audited directly.

Each retained claim then receives four labels. ESG_Type assigns E, S or G by keyword dominance. Claim_Type distinguishes a Future Promise, a Strong claim supported by a hard quantity, and a Vague claim: "We are committed to a sustainable future" is labelled Vague, whereas "We reduced Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 4% against our 2019 baseline" is labelled Strong. Evidence is recorded as Yes, Partial or No, and a Risk_Signal is attached to each claim. Six reports are currently loaded; the extractor produced 583 claims from a Unilever report and 218 from a Dr Pepper report.

We evaluated the extractor in several ways. On a 26-sentence development set it reached 92% precision, but because the filters were tuned on these sentences we do not treat this figure as a headline result. An independent manual review of 100 claims from an earlier version (v3) found 67% precision. Automated audits of the full corpus by AI agents gave precision of 33% for v3, 39% for v4.0 and 44% for the final v4, and recall, estimated by auditing 1,767 rejected sentences, rose from 48% for v4.0 to 73% for the final v4. These numbers carry important caveats. All of them are in-sample and therefore optimistic. The agent audits are stricter than human reviewers, so their precision figures are not directly comparable with the manual review. Finally, the remaining errors concern meaning rather than vocabulary, which suggests that keyword rules have reached a semantic ceiling; this motivates the BERT classifier planned for the next stage.
