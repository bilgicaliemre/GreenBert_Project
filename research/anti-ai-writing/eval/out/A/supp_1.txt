On Tuesdays and Thursdays after school, my avó Rosa and I work through Portuguese picture flashcards I made for her. Two years ago she had a stroke, and now she has aphasia; she understands more than she can say. She's relearning words I'm learning for the first time. She beats me at "agulha," needle. I beat her at "telemóvel," cell phone.

Those afternoons handed me a question I can't put down: how does a brain find its words again? I read a lot about aphasia now. I wanted it to be connected to my stutter, which gets worse on hard sounds like "C." It isn't; they're totally different things. That stung, then helped. I had to stop reading about myself and start reading about her.

Some days she calls me Lurdes, the aunt who died before I was born. I used to correct her; now I don't. Avó can't say my name yet, but she can say "sapato," shoe, a word I hear every Saturday at my dad's repair shop. Why does one word survive and another not? I hope to chase that through speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics.

I once asked my middle school speech therapist if I'd be "allowed" to do this job with a stutter. She said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet. But last month I answered the shop phone "Carvalho Shoe Service" on the third try, and the man waited. I'd like to be the one who waits.
