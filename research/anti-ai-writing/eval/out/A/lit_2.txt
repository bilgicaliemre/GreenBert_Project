The Receding Light

In The Great Gatsby, the green light begins as something almost meaningless and grows into one of the novel's largest ideas, but at every stage it matters only because it is out of reach. When the light first appears in Chapter 1, the narrator watches Gatsby from a distance: "he stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way, and, far as I was from him, I could have sworn he was trembling." Looking where Gatsby looks, he "distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." The contrast is striking. Gatsby's gesture is dramatic and full of feeling, yet its object is so small and ordinary that the narrator cannot be sure what it is. The phrase "minute and far away" makes distance the light's defining quality, and the reader senses that it is important because of the longing aimed at it, not because of anything it is in itself.

In Chapter 5, the light gains a specific meaning and then immediately begins to lose it. Speaking to Daisy, Gatsby tells her, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock." The words "always" and "all night" suggest a steady vigil, as if the light had been keeping watch the same way Gatsby had. Yet the narrator reflects that "the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever." While Daisy was unreachable, the light "had seemed very near to her, almost touching her," as close "as a star to the moon." Now that the distance between them has closed, "it was again a green light on a dock." The word "again" is important because it reminds us that the light was always just a light; Gatsby's imagination is what made it special. The narrator's remark that "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one" turns Gatsby's love into an inventory of charmed things, and it points to a painful irony: getting closer to what he wants makes it smaller.

By Chapter 9, the light has separated from Daisy altogether and become a symbol of hope itself. "Gatsby believed in the green light," the narrator says, "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." The verb "believed" makes the light sound like an object of faith, and the move from Gatsby alone to "us" expands one man's private dream into a human one. The next lines, "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther," echo the outstretched arms of Chapter 1, so that Gatsby's lonely gesture becomes everyone's. Even the unfinished ending, "And one fine morning——," acts out what it describes, since the sentence reaches toward a future it never arrives at. The green light therefore changes from a mysterious point across the water, to a sign of one woman, to the endlessly receding future, but its core never changes. It can only shine while it stays far away.
