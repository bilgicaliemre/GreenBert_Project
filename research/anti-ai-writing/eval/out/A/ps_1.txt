Carvalho Shoe Service

On Saturdays I work the counter at my dad's shoe repair shop on Pleasant Street. It has been there since 1994, and it smells like Barge cement and leather dust, a smell that now lives in my hoodie too. My friends say I smell like a cowboy's basement. I write the tickets, and since last summer I've been allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. I can resole a sneaker in twenty-five minutes.

What I could not do, for years, was answer the phone.

I have a stutter. It gets worse on the phone, and it is worst on hard sounds like "C," which is a problem when the shop is called Carvalho. So I found a workaround: "Hi, shoe repair." It sounded friendly enough. It also meant that I never said my own family's name to a single caller. My dad noticed after about a year. He never said anything about it.

The shop taught me a different kind of listening. I can usually tell how someone walks from the wear on their heels. An outer edge ground down means they supinate. Once a woman brought in a pair where the right heel was worn far more than the left. I pointed it out, and it turned out she drives for work; her right heel rests on the floor by the pedal. She looked at me like I was a psychic. I keep a notebook of heels now. It has 61 entries. Entry 1 is my dad's work boots. People tell you a lot without saying a word, if you know where to look.

That mattered more than I expected. Two years ago my avó, Rosa, had a stroke, and now she has aphasia. She understands more than she can say. Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, the name of my aunt who died before I was born. At first I corrected her. Now I don't. Every Tuesday and Thursday after school, we work through flashcards I made for her, pictures with Portuguese words. The funny part is that she is relearning these words while I am learning many of them for the first time. She is better than me at "agulha," needle. I am better at "telemóvel," cell phone. She can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato." Shoe.

Sitting across from her, I started wondering how the brain gets words back, and that question has become what I want to study: speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics. I read a lot about aphasia now. One of the first things I learned is that stuttering and aphasia are completely different things. I had kind of wanted them to be connected, as if my grandmother's struggle and mine were one problem, and understanding one would explain the other. They aren't. I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my speech therapist from middle school, whether I would even be "allowed" to be a speech therapist with a stutter. She told me some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet. But I want to find out if she's right.

Last month the shop phone rang, and I picked it up and said, "Carvalho Shoe Service." It took three tries. The man on the other end waited through all of them. He wanted to know if we fix purses. We don't. But I said our name, and he heard it, and for once the word that belonged to me came out of my own mouth instead of being traded for a safer one.

My avó is still working on mine. I'll wait for her, too.
