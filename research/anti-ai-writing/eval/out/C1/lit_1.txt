The green light and the problem of distance

Across The Great Gatsby, the green light changes from a distant puzzle to a sign of Daisy to an image of all human longing, but at every stage it means something only while it stays out of reach. In Chapter 1 it is a mystery, and Fitzgerald holds back its meaning on purpose. Gatsby, Nick tells us, "stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way." Following his gaze, Nick "distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." He cannot even be sure what the object is; it only "might have been" a dock. Nick, like the reader, sees the direction of Gatsby's desire and nothing more: a gesture of longing and the tiny object it points toward. All Nick can report about the light is its size and its distance. Yet he "could have sworn" Gatsby was trembling, so whatever the light stands for matters to Gatsby intensely.

Chapter 5 reveals the light's private meaning and then almost immediately takes it away. Gatsby tells Daisy, "You always have a green light that burns all night at the end of your dock." The words "always" and "all night" make the light sound like a vigil, something steady that he has kept watch over. Nick guesses that being near Daisy has drained the symbol of its power: "Possibly it had occurred to him that the colossal significance of that light had now vanished forever." Compared to "the great distance that had separated him from Daisy," the light "had seemed very near to her, almost touching her." It had seemed "as close as a star to the moon," though a star and the moon look close only from far below. Once the distance closes, "it was again a green light on a dock," and getting closer to what he wanted has made Gatsby's dream smaller. The light was enchanted only while it stood in for something he could not reach. As Nick puts it, "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one."

In Chapter 9 Nick makes the light much larger than Gatsby's love for Daisy. "Gatsby believed in the green light," he writes, "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." The sentence begins with "Gatsby" and ends with "us," so the light becomes the hope everyone carries for a better future, a future that moves away as we approach it. Nick's next lines echo the opening scene. In Chapter 1 Gatsby "stretched out his arms"; now "to-morrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther." Gatsby's lonely gesture has become a shared human habit. Even Nick's sentence never arrives: the passage ends at "And one fine morning——," a promise left unfinished.
