The Phones Can Wait

Walk into almost any high school classroom a few minutes before the bell and you will see the same scene: rows of students bent over their phones, thumbs moving, barely speaking to the person next to them. Phones are so normal in school that it is easy to forget they were ever absent, but being normal does not make them good for students. High schools should ban student phones during the school day. Phones undermine learning and weaken the social life of a school, and they leave each student to resist, alone and all day, a device designed to hold their attention.

The most obvious problem is distraction. A phone is designed to pull its owner back to it again and again. Every buzz is a small interruption, and even a silent phone can tug at a student's thoughts. Anyone who has tried to follow a teacher's explanation of a chemistry problem while wondering whether a friend has replied knows how hard it is to do both. Learning difficult material takes sustained focus, the kind that builds over twenty or thirty uninterrupted minutes. A phone that interrupts every few minutes makes that kind of learning nearly impossible, for the student holding it and for the classmates who see its screen light up.

Supporters of phones in school often argue that the devices are educational tools: students can look up information, use a calculator or check assignments online. That is true, but nearly all of those jobs can be done on school-issued laptops or classroom computers, which teachers can monitor and which do not come loaded with social media and games. A phone's educational value is real but small and its potential for distraction is large, so it makes sense to set it aside.

Phones also change the way students treat one another. Lunch and passing periods are when students talk, joke, argue and make friends outside their usual circles, and those moments shrink when everyone retreats into a screen. A quiet student who might have been pulled into a conversation is left alone instead, surrounded by classmates who are all looking down at their phones. Conflicts follow students through the hallway as well: a rumor posted during second period can spread through the whole building before third. A school day without phones gives students a few hours of relief from that social pressure.

The strongest objection to a ban is safety. Parents understandably want to reach their children in an emergency. A ban, however, does not have to mean confiscating phones or making students leave them at home. Schools can keep phones in lockers or locked pouches until dismissal, and the front office stays available for urgent calls, as it always has. In a real crisis, clear direction from trained staff is more useful than a building full of students texting rumors to one another.

Finally, a ban is fair to students. Asking a teenager to ignore a device designed to be irresistible, hour after hour, is asking a great deal. A clear school-wide rule puts every student in the same position: the temptation is gone for the day, and teachers and students no longer argue over whether a phone is out.

Phones are not evil, and students will use them for the rest of their lives. But school is meant for focused thinking and for the people sitting next to you. For seven hours a day, the phones can wait.
