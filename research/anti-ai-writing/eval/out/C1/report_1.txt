Claim extraction

The first stage of the GreenBERT pipeline, and the only one built so far, is a rule-based extractor that separates the claims in a corporate ESG report from the surrounding description and boilerplate. Each report is converted from PDF to text with PyMuPDF, keeping page numbers, and the text is split into sentences with NLTK.

Every sentence must first pass a lexical gate. A sentence is retained only if it contains an ESG topic word and either an action verb (including first-person and past-tense forms such as "we reduced") or a hard quantity (a number with a unit, or a count). A sentence that names an ESG topic without an action or a quantity is treated as description and dropped. Sentences that pass the gate then go through a series of non-claim filters, which remove boilerplate, reference-dense text, navigation elements and headers, broken fragments (checked for a verb with a part-of-speech tagger), policy content, descriptions of governance roles, activity reports, methodology text, general statements and risk descriptions. We save every rejected sentence with its drop reason so that recall can be audited.

Each retained claim then receives four labels. ESG_Type assigns E, S or G by keyword dominance. Claim_Type marks a claim as a Future Promise, as Strong when a hard quantity backs it, or as Vague. "We are committed to a sustainable future" is labelled Vague; "We reduced Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 4% against our 2019 baseline" is labelled Strong. Evidence is recorded as Yes, Partial or No, and each claim also carries a Risk_Signal. Of the six reports loaded so far, a Unilever report yielded 583 claims and a Dr Pepper report 218.

We evaluated the extractor first on a 26-sentence development set, where it reached 92% precision; because the filters were tuned on these sentences, we do not treat this figure as a headline result. An independent manual review of 100 claims from an earlier version (v3) found 67% precision. Automated audits of the full corpus by AI agents gave precision of 33% for v3, 39% for v4.0 and 44% for the final v4. Recall, estimated by auditing 1,767 rejected sentences, rose from 48% for v4.0 to 73% for the final v4. All of these figures are in-sample and therefore optimistic. The agents are also stricter than human reviewers, so their 33% for v3 cannot be compared directly with the 67% from the manual review of the same version. Finally, the remaining errors concern meaning rather than vocabulary, which suggests that keyword rules have reached a semantic ceiling. That ceiling is the reason for the next stage, a BERT classifier that has not yet been built.
