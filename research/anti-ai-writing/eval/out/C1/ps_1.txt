Carvalho Shoe Service

On Saturdays I work the counter at my dad's shoe repair shop on Pleasant Street. It has been there since 1994, and it smells like Barge cement and leather dust. So does my hoodie; my friends say I smell like a cowboy's basement. I write the tickets, and since last summer I've been allowed to do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. I can resole a sneaker in twenty-five minutes. My dad's right thumb is stained black from the dye.

I hate the phone. I have a stutter, and it gets worse on the phone, especially on hard sounds like "C," which is a problem when the shop is called Carvalho. So I used to answer with "Hi, shoe repair." That meant I never said my own family's name to a caller. My dad noticed after about a year and never said anything about it. I asked him once if he wanted me to take over the shop, and he said, "The shop isn't a person, it doesn't need anyone." I don't know if that was a yes or a no.

I can tell how someone walks from the wear on their heels. An outer edge ground down means they supinate. Once a woman brought in a pair where the right heel was worn far more than the left. I pointed it out, and it turned out she drives for work, so her right heel rests on the floor by the pedal. She looked at me like I was a psychic. I keep a notebook of heels. It has 61 entries, and entry 1 is my dad's work boots.

Two years ago my avó, Rosa, had a stroke, and now she has aphasia. She understands more than she can say. Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, the name of my aunt who died before I was born. At first I corrected her. Now I don't. Every Tuesday and Thursday after school we go through flashcards I made for her, pictures with Portuguese words. The funny part is that she's relearning the words while I'm learning them for the first time. She's better than me at "agulha," needle. I'm better at "telemóvel," cell phone.

I'm worse at chemistry words. Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept that up for three weeks and even forged the parent signature on a progress report. Then my teacher, Mr. Oduya, emailed home. My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test to her, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen." She laughed at that part.

I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics, or something with how the brain gets words back. I read a lot about aphasia now. I found out that stuttering and aphasia are totally different things. I kind of wanted them to be connected, and they're not. I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my speech therapist from middle school, whether I would even be "allowed" to be a speech therapist with a stutter. She told me some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.

Last month the shop phone rang, and I picked it up and said, "Carvalho Shoe Service." It was the first time I had answered with our name, and it took three tries. The man on the other end waited through all of them. He wanted to know if we fix purses. We don't.

My avó can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato," shoe.
