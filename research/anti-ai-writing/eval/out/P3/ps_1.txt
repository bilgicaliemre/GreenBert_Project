For about a year, I answered the phone at my dad's shop with "Hi, shoe repair."

The shop is Carvalho Shoe Service, on Pleasant Street in Fall River. I work the counter on Saturdays and, since last summer, do heels and half-soles on the finishing machine. I also have a stutter. It gets worse on the phone, and worst on hard sounds like "C." So I skipped our name. My dad noticed after about a year and never said a word.

I keep a list on my phone of every lie I tell. It has 212 items since October of sophomore year, mostly about being on time. It started with a chemistry test. I got a 54 on my first AP Chem exam, told my mom it was an 85, and kept that up for three weeks. I forged her signature on a progress report, after practicing it about eight times on the back of a math worksheet. That's the part I'd rather leave out, because it means I planned it.

When Mr. Oduya emailed home, I was secretly relieved. Keeping the 85 straight was exhausting.

My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test, question by question, at the kitchen table, in Portuguese, which I don't have for chemistry. It took two hours. When I didn't know the word for "mole," I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed. I finished with a B+.

I only recently realized "Hi, shoe repair" was a lie too. A small one, and not on the list. On the phone I'd been doing exactly what I did with the test: swapping the true thing for an easier thing.

Other people are easier for me to read. I keep a notebook of heels, 61 entries so far; entry 1 is my dad's work boots. One woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left, and I said she must drive for work, her heel resting by the pedal. She looked at me like I was a psychic.

My avó, Rosa, is harder. She had a stroke two years ago and has aphasia; she understands more than she can say. On Tuesdays and Thursdays we go through picture flashcards I made in Portuguese. She's relearning words I'm learning for the first time. She beats me at "agulha," needle. I beat her at "telemóvel." Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, my aunt who died before I was born. I used to correct her. Now I don't. She can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato."

I want the heels and my grandmother to be the same skill: reading someone from the outside when they can't tell you. But I can't tell what avó is thinking when she gets a word wrong. I also wanted my stutter and her aphasia to be connected; they're completely different things. I don't know whether this connection is real or one I just want.

I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe linguistics. When I asked Ms. Halvorsen, my middle school speech therapist, if I'd be "allowed" to do that with a stutter, she said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.

Last month I answered the shop phone with "Carvalho Shoe Service." It took three tries. I was picturing the man's face, waiting for annoyance. On the second try I almost hung up. When the name came out, I didn't feel proud, just embarrassed. Then he asked whether we fix purses, as if nothing had happened. We don't. That was the best part: to him, it was nothing.

I haven't put "Hi, shoe repair" on the list. Maybe I will. I'm still not sure whether the list makes me more honest or just better at noticing. But noticing is how I caught the lie I'd been telling into that phone for a year.
