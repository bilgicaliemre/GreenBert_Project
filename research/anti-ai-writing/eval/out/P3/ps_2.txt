Last month a man called Carvalho Shoe Service to ask if we fix purses. We don't. It took me three tries to answer with the shop's name. On the second try I almost hung up. When it finally came out, I didn't feel proud; I felt embarrassed. Then he asked about purses like nothing had happened. That was the best part. It was nothing to him.

I have a stutter that gets worse on the phone, especially on hard sounds like "C," which is a problem when the shop and my last name both start with one. My dad has run the shop on Pleasant Street since 1994, and I work the counter on Saturdays. For years I answered the phone "Hi, shoe repair." My dad noticed after about a year. He never said anything.

Only recently did I realize "Hi, shoe repair" was a lie. A small one, but a lie.

I know something about lies. Sophomore year I got a 54 on my first AP Chem test and told my mom it was an 85. I kept it up for three weeks and forged her signature on the progress report. The part I'd rather leave out: I practiced that signature on the back of a math worksheet about eight times first. It wasn't panic. I planned it. When Mr. Oduya emailed home, I told nobody I was relieved. Keeping the 85 straight had been exhausting.

My mom didn't yell. She asked me to explain the test to her, question by question, in Portuguese, which I'm not good at for chemistry words. It took two hours. I didn't know the word for "mole," so I said "the unit thing, like a dozen," and she laughed. Afterward I started keeping a list on my phone of every lie I tell, even "I'm almost there." It has 212 items, mostly about being on time. I'm still not sure whether it makes me more honest or just better at noticing.

"Hi, shoe repair" isn't on it. With the phone, I'd been doing what I did with the test: swapping the true thing for an easier thing.

I am good at noticing. I can tell how someone walks from the wear on their heel, and I keep a notebook of heels: 61 entries, the first one my dad's work boots. Once a woman's right heel was ground down far more than her left; I said she must drive for work, and she looked at me like I was a psychic.

With my avó, Rosa, it isn't that easy. Two years ago she had a stroke, and now she has aphasia; she understands more than she can say. On Tuesdays and Thursdays we go through picture flashcards I made in Portuguese. She's relearning words I'm learning for the first time. She beats me at "agulha," needle; I beat her at "telemóvel." Sometimes she calls me Lurdes, my aunt who died before I was born. I used to correct her. Now I don't. She can't say my name yet. She can say "sapato."

Heels and my grandmother are both about figuring out what's going on with someone who can't tell you. I don't know if that connection is real or if I just want it to be, the way I wanted stuttering and aphasia to be connected. They aren't.

I want to study speech-language pathology, maybe how the brain gets words back. I once asked Ms. Halvorsen, my middle school speech therapist, whether I'd be "allowed" to be a speech therapist with a stutter. She said some of the best ones stutter. I don't fully believe her yet.

For now, I have a list of 212 small untruths, a notebook of 61 heels, and one phone call where the true thing took three tries and the man on the line didn't mind. That seems worth practicing.
