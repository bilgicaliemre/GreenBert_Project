My grandmother can say "sapato" but not my name.

Avó Rosa had a stroke two years ago and now has aphasia. She understands more than she can say. Tuesdays and Thursdays after school, we do picture flashcards I made her in Portuguese, and we're both learning: she's getting words back, and I'm meeting many for the first time. She beats me at "agulha." I beat her at "telemóvel."

What I want to know is how a brain gets a word back. Why "shoe" came before "Inês." Why she sometimes calls me Lurdes, my aunt who died before I was born. I read a lot about aphasia now. I learned that stuttering, which I have, and aphasia are completely different things. I'd wanted them to be connected.

On Saturdays at my dad's shoe repair shop, I read people from the outside. An outer edge worn down means someone supinates. One woman's right heel was ground lower than her left because she drives for work and it rests by the pedal. I keep a notebook of heels. It has 61 entries.

Heels are easy. With my avó, I can't tell what she's thinking when she gets a word wrong. That's what I want to study, through speech-language pathology or linguistics: how to understand what's happening with someone who can't tell you. I'm not sure the heels and the flashcards are connected, or if I just want them to be. I'd like to find out.
